# Ide v. British Airways PLC
529 F. Supp. 3d 73 (S.D.N.Y. 2021), No. 20-CV-3542 (JMF), Document 53, March 26, 2021 | Contracts

## Court, allegations, and transaction
Judge Jesse M. Furman decided British Airways’s motion to compel one plaintiff to arbitrate and its motion to dismiss the remaining plaintiffs’ putative-class claims. This was a pleading-stage decision, not a finding that the airline actually committed the alleged conduct or a judgment awarding refunds. The court assumed the amended complaint and incorporated documents to be true for the dismissal motion; arbitration involved a distinct evidentiary standard permitting materials outside the complaint. No separate opinion appears. (Slip 1–2, 5–7 & n. 1.)

British Airways’s General Conditions of Carriage promised a customer whose flight was cancelled a choice among three remedies: carriage as soon as possible on an available scheduled service, carriage later at the customer’s convenience within the ticket’s validity period, or an involuntary fare refund. The refund included the fare, carrier charges and surcharges, and paid taxes, fees, and charges. The options did not expressly include a future travel voucher. (Slip 2.)

The complaint alleged that after the pandemic caused mass cancellations, the airline changed its website to steer customers towards vouchers. A button labeled to cancel and refund flights led to a voucher application; the online refund option was removed. Telephone contact then became the only refund route, but many calls were disconnected and others involved hours on hold. These were allegations of deliberate obstruction, not simply a judicial finding that heavy demand itself constituted breach. (Slip 2–3, 13–14 & n. 8.)

Stephen Ide unsuccessfully tried to reach the airline, obtained a voucher as the only available relief, and later sought cash instead. Four later calls were disconnected; an agent eventually denied conversion because the voucher had issued, and the website would not allow the suggested complaint escalation. Karen Steele-Clarke believed the online form sought a refund but received a voucher, then sought a refund through email and other channels. Her seat-selection charge was refunded, but her fare was not. Philip Tenn submitted a voucher form but requested a refund by telephone and email before receiving a voucher; he allegedly received neither. Donald Dominique bought through Expedia, could not reach British Airways, and was told by Expedia that the airline authorized vouchers alone; he too received neither form of relief. (Slip 3–5.)

## Arbitration and the permitted dismissal record
Only Dominique had bought through Expedia. Its agreement required individual arbitration of claims against travel suppliers such as British Airways, and he did not oppose compelling arbitration. He did oppose dismissal with prejudice while arbitration proceeded. Applying circuit authority requiring a stay when requested, the court compelled arbitration and stayed only his claims. The other three plaintiffs continued in court. (Slip 5–6 & n. 1.)

British Airways separately argued that Tenn had failed to appear for his outbound flight, rendering the return ticket without value under the carriage terms. Its support was a Passenger Name Record submitted with the motion. The complaint neither referred to that record nor incorporated its contents, so it fell outside the narrow materials permissible on dismissal. The airline did not request conversion to summary judgment, and the court declined to convert before discovery, particularly given Tenn’s contention that discovery could show earlier contact. Tenn’s claim therefore was evaluated with those of Ide and Steele-Clarke; the ruling did not find whether he actually missed the outbound flight. (Slip 6–9 & n. 3.)

## Two plausible contract theories
The existence of a contract and plaintiffs’ performance were undisputed for purposes of the motion. The airline disputed breach and damages. Its damages argument was relegated to a footnote, insufficiently raising the issue, and failed substantively anyway: restricted in-kind compensation plausibly was worth less than the cash or other promised remedy. No amount of compensatory loss was fixed. (Slip 9–10.)

First, the plaintiffs plausibly alleged that the airline failed to offer a promised option. The contract unambiguously allowed a customer to choose among the three listed remedies. British Airways asserted that a voucher was essentially equivalent to later carriage at the customer’s convenience. But Ide’s voucher was only part payment for a future booking, with a twelve-month travel deadline; an archived airline webpage imposed another expiration date. The claimed equivalence was plausibly disputable. The court also noted that the parties had not addressed the precise validity periods of plaintiffs’ tickets, preserving that issue rather than deciding that every voucher necessarily had a shorter validity period. (Slip 10–12 & nn. 6–7.)

Note 5 addressed a distinct possible defense: requesting a voucher did not establish modification, waiver, estoppel, or accord and satisfaction on the complaint’s face. Those were affirmative defenses requiring proof of their elements; the airline did not argue that voucher requests automatically relieved it of the promised remedies. References to Department of Transportation guidance likewise did not turn this suit into enforcement of an independent regulatory refund duty. The court understood them as contextual, and any separate claim based on that guidance had been abandoned through failure to respond to dismissal arguments. (Slip 10–11 & nn. 4–5.)

Second, the complaint plausibly alleged prevention of the customers’ exercise of their contractual choice. British Airways characterized calling for a refund as an unsatisfied condition precedent. New York’s prevention doctrine barred avoiding a contractual duty by frustrating the condition’s occurrence. Removing refund forms and funneling requests through inaccessible call centers allegedly prevented selection of the promised remedy. The airline argued the difficulty resulted from the public-health emergency rather than intentional conduct; the complaint alleged intentional website changes, and the factual dispute could not support dismissal. The court distinguished analogous airline cases dismissed for insufficiently particular allegations: these named plaintiffs described their own unsuccessful attempts. (Slip 12–15 & n. 8.)

## Airline preemption and limits on relief
The Airline Deregulation Act preempts state laws related to air-carrier price, route, or service, but Wolens permits enforcement of an airline’s voluntarily undertaken contractual obligations. Ginsberg distinguishes an implied obligation protecting the parties’ bargain from a state-imposed community standard that enlarges it. Under New York law, prevention serves the parties’ intent and reasonable expectations. Using that doctrine to enforce the airline’s promised remedial choice therefore did not impose a separate external obligation, and these contract claims were not preempted. The ruling did not exempt all implied-covenant claims categorically. (Slip 15–17.)

The court struck the requests for statutory damages as unopposed. It held punitive or exemplary damages preempted because they fell outside the exception for enforcing the parties’ bargain. It also rejected injunctive relief because money could adequately compensate the alleged injury; liability, if established, would not present damages that were difficult to calculate. Leave to amend to restore those requests was denied as futile because the defects were substantive. (Slip 17–19.)

## Exact disposition and source scope
The court compelled Dominique’s arbitration and stayed the action only as to him. It denied dismissal of the other three plaintiffs’ contract claims, granted dismissal of their requests for statutory, punitive or exemplary damages and injunctive relief, required an answer within three weeks, and reinstated the pretrial conference. It neither certified a class nor awarded compensation. Ide demonstrates that a promised customer choice cannot plausibly be satisfied by substituting a less valuable remedy or making the choice functionally inaccessible, while leaving proof, defenses, and damages for later proceedings. (Slip 18–19.)
