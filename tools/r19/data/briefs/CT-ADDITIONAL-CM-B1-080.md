# Pennsylvania Academy of Fine Arts v. Grant
404 Pa. Super. 62, 590 A.2d 9 (1991) | Contracts

## Facts and procedural posture
Pennsylvania Academy of Fine Arts acquired Mellon Bank (East), N.A.’s lease of a Center City Philadelphia parking lot on January 30, 1989. The lease expired July 31, 1989 and permitted purchase if the lessee exercised the option at least six months before expiration. Section 801 required written notices delivered or mailed by certified or registered mail. A January 30 letter announcing PAFA’s exercise, accompanied by the necessary deposit check, was mailed January 31 by prepaid certified mail with return receipt requested. The lessor, Thelma Grant as trustee of Max Grant’s residuary trust, received it February 3. On February 28 she returned the check, asserting that receipt after the six-month cutoff made the exercise ineffective. These are the original opinion’s January and February dates, rather than the July and August dates in the assigned editorial account. (404 Pa. Super. at 63–64 & n.1.)

PAFA sued to enforce the lease. Grant challenged the notice’s timeliness and alleged environmental and tax breaches. The trial court granted PAFA summary judgment, and Grant appealed. The Superior Court, through Judge Montemuro on a panel with Judges Wieand and Hester, affirmed. The report contains no separate writing or express numerical vote. Its filing date is March 21, 1991; May 21 is the separately reported denial of reargument. (Id. at 62–63, 66.)

## Issue and governing rules
The principal question was whether mailing or receipt controlled exercise of the purchase option. Grant described the receipt date, five months and twenty-eight days before expiration, as a disputed material fact. The operative dates nevertheless presented a legal question about the effect of notice, rather than a factual contest for trial. (Id. at 64.)

Under then-applicable Pennsylvania Rule of Civil Procedure 1035, summary judgment required authorized record materials establishing no genuine dispute of material fact and entitlement to judgment as a matter of law. Courts viewed the record favorably to the nonmovant, resolved doubt against the movant, and identified factual disputes rather than deciding them. Controverted pleading allegations did not themselves supply proof: the court examined the authorized supporting and opposing material and uncontroverted allegations. Affidavits under Rule 1035(d) needed personal knowledge, admissible facts, and affirmative evidence of the affiant’s competence. (Id. at 63–64, 66.)

## Notice and the force of controlling precedent
The trial court found no lease term specifying when notice became effective. Section 801 prescribed a method of communication; it did not expressly declare dispatch or receipt the effective event. The court also recognized the general option rule, supported by Restatement (Second) §63 comment f and treatises: absent contrary intent, exercising a leasehold option becomes effective on receipt. PAFA’s result therefore cannot be explained simply by treating the contract as an express dispatch clause or by ignoring the ordinary distinction between options and other offers. (Id. at 64 & n.1.)

The controlling complication was the Pennsylvania Supreme Court’s decision in Chanoff v. Fiala. There, the buyer mailed a deposit check the day before an option expired, and the seller received it afterward. The Supreme Court applied the mailbox rule: when the use of mail is authorized or implied, posting an acceptance through normal channels completes acceptance. Registered-mail notice provisions supported authorization. (Id. at 64–65, discussing 440 Pa. 424, 428.)

Grant could point to factual differences. Chanoff involved a course of dealing that suggested lax attention to deadlines, a mailing one day before expiration, and the seller’s deposit of the check. The Superior Court found none of those differences part of the Supreme Court’s stated rationale. Its own recognition that option acceptance generally requires receipt did not authorize departure from the higher court’s pronouncement. It was constrained by Chanoff to hold PAFA’s January 31 mailing an effective exercise. The reasoning is consequently a jurisdiction-specific application of controlling precedent, including the authority relationship between Pennsylvania courts, rather than a claim that Restatement §63(b) generally adopts dispatch for option contracts. (404 Pa. Super. at 65–66.)

## Additional breach defenses and disposition
Grant alleged that an underground 10,000-gallon gasoline tank violated federal and local environmental requirements, and that delinquent city use and occupancy taxes breached the lease. The tank had been installed by predecessor lessee Mellon Bank. The record supplied no proof of legal action over the claimed environmental condition, only possible liability that might never materialize. The taxes involved a former tenant, not PAFA, and created no liability for Grant as landlord. These points described the evidentiary shortcomings of the defenses; the court did not make a general determination that underground tanks satisfy environmental law. (Id. at 66 & n.2.)

Grant’s own accusatory affidavits and an attorney’s affidavit resting on third-party hearsay failed Rule 1035(d)’s requirements. The court called them disqualified in every respect. They therefore established neither a material breach nor a disputed material fact preventing summary judgment. The exact disposition was “Order affirmed.” The opinion supplies no damages award or detailed conveyance decree, and the brief does not infer either from the affirmance of summary judgment for PAFA. (Id. at 66.)
