# Bel-Ray Company, Inc. v. Chemrite (Pty) Ltd.
181 F.3d 435 (3d Cir. June 28, 1999) | Contracts

## Facts and chronology
Bel-Ray, a New Jersey manufacturer of specialty lubricants, licensed confidential formulas, blending technology, and its trade name to South African distributor Chemrite. The January 1996 Trade Agreements covered distribution, manufacturing, and trade-name use. Each required arbitration in Wall Township, New Jersey of disputes relating to the agreement or its breach, under AAA rules and New Jersey substantive law. Each also required written consent to assignment. (437–438.)

After first representing that it had changed its name, Chemrite sold its lubricant business and expressly transferred the Trade Agreements to newly formed Lubritene in October 1996, then entered liquidation. Lubritene continued the business at the same location with the same personnel and management. Four Chemrite officers or directors became officers, directors, and shareholders of Lubritene. Bel-Ray was sent the asset-sale documents, and the parties continued trading under the existing agreements. Later negotiations produced conflicting affidavits about whether Bel-Ray’s representative said an enforceable agreement existed. (438–439.)

Bel-Ray then obtained internal board minutes describing a plan to continue trading without alerting it, transfer Lubritene’s other business to a new entity, leave Lubritene without recoverable assets, and later deny the agreements’ validity because Chemrite no longer existed. Bel-Ray alleged unfair competition, fraud, misappropriation, contractual breaches, and deceptive labeling of products. Those allegations supplied the dispute to be arbitrated; the appeal did not adjudicate their merits. (439–440.)

## Procedure and issues
Bel-Ray sued in New Jersey to compel Lubritene and the four individuals to arbitrate. They asserted lack of personal jurisdiction and counterclaimed for relief based on Bel-Ray’s related South African proceedings, including an injunction against compelling arbitration on a waiver theory. The individuals moved for affirmative relief before obtaining a jurisdictional ruling. The district court denied their motion, then granted Bel-Ray summary judgment compelling arbitration in August 1998. The Third Circuit reviewed that judgment de novo. The international arbitration action fell within the Convention’s implementing jurisdictional provision, 9 U.S.C. § 203. (440, 443–444.)

Lubritene argued that the assignments were ineffective without written consent and therefore it had no written arbitration agreement. The individuals separately argued that the district court lacked personal jurisdiction and that a corporation’s agreement could not bind them personally. These distinct questions produced different outcomes. (440–446.)

## Assignment rules and corporate holding
The court applied New Jersey contract law because Lubritene had neither raised South African contract law adequately nor supplied its content. Rule 44.1 authorizes independent judicial research but does not require it; parties ordinarily carry the burden of raising and establishing applicable foreign law. The information supplied about South African successor liability did not address the distinct assignment issue. (440–441 & n.2.)

New Jersey’s highest court had not resolved the anti-assignment question. Following its intermediate appellate court’s Garden State decision and Restatement § 322(2)(b), the Third Circuit distinguished a contractual restriction on the right to assign from a restriction on the power to effect an assignment. A covenant requiring consent ordinarily makes an unauthorized assignment a breach, potentially supporting damages, while leaving the transfer effective. To negate the power, the agreement must manifest that intention clearly—for example, by declaring a nonconforming assignment void or invalid, denying the assignee rights, or stating that the other party will not recognize it. (441–443.)

The Trade Agreements said that rights and obligations would not be assignable without written consent, but did not state that a nonconforming transfer would be void or ineffective. Their language therefore did not negate Chemrite’s power to transfer. The assignment bound Lubritene to the agreements, including the written arbitration clauses, and the district court properly required the company to arbitrate. This conclusion did not decide whether Chemrite owed damages for violating the covenant. Nor did it depend on resolving the conflicting recollections about the negotiations. (443.)

## Individual jurisdiction and arbitration
The individual appellants waived their personal-jurisdiction defense through their conduct, despite timely preserving it in their answer. They sought and litigated summary judgment for affirmative injunctive relief concerning the same dispute before obtaining a jurisdictional determination. Nothing in the record made treating that conduct as submission to jurisdiction unfair. Jurisdiction over them therefore existed. (443–444.)

Jurisdiction did not establish their contractual consent to arbitration. An agent acting for a disclosed principal ordinarily does not personally become a contracting party. Bel-Ray supplied no supported contract or agency basis for binding the individuals. Earlier cases allowing agents to enforce a signatory’s broad arbitration undertaking involved what the signatory had agreed to arbitrate; they did not authorize the corporation to surrender its agents’ own right to litigate their personal liability. A subtenant case likewise concerned derivative property rights, not an independent agreement to arbitrate personal liability. (444–445.)

The court also checked the usual nonsignatory theories—incorporation, assumption, agency, veil piercing or alter ego, and estoppel—and found none supported by this record. Without grounds to disregard Lubritene’s separate existence, the court could not find the direct benefit required for estoppel. A claim arising out of a corporate relationship alone did not bind the individuals. Bel-Ray could ask the district court to stay the litigation against them while its arbitration with Lubritene proceeded. (445–446.)

## Exact judgment and source limits
Judge Stapleton wrote for a panel with Rendell and Aldisert; no dissent or separate writing is reported. The court’s mandate reversed the order compelling arbitration and remanded for entry of an order compelling only Lubritene to arbitrate. Thus, the company’s obligation survived, while the officers’ compelled arbitration was removed.
