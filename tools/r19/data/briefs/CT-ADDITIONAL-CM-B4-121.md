# City of North Kansas City v. Sharp
414 F.2d 359 (8th Cir. August 8, 1969) | Contracts

## Decision and posture

The Eighth Circuit affirmed a bench-trial judgment awarding engineer P. Clifford Sharp $131,262 plus interest for the City’s breach of an engineering contract. Judge Blackmun wrote for the panel of Blackmun, Gibson, and Heaney; there was no separate opinion. Rehearing was denied September 10, 1969. The court rejected challenges to municipal authorization, continued performance after a partner’s withdrawal, the asserted right to terminate after the preliminary phase, and damages, costs, and interest. (359–361, 369.)

## Contract and withdrawal chronology

Haskins, Riddle and Sharp had worked for the City before 1957. Haskins died in 1956; Riddle and Sharp continued under the existing name. They entered a written professional-engineering agreement with the City on November 12, 1957. The mayor signed, the city clerk attested, and the city attorney approved its form and legality. Preliminary services concerned sanitary-sewage studies, recommendations, costs, a report, bond-election assistance, and a federal grant. A second phase required design, bidding, and construction supervision. Paragraph 6 directed the engineers to prepare detailed documents when instructed by the City. The preliminary services were performed and paid for, the 1960 bond election succeeded, and the federal grant was obtained. (361.)

Ordelheide joined the firm in 1958 according to testimony; footnote 1 acknowledged the complaint’s inconsistent suggestion that he was already a partner in 1957 and explained that the difference did not affect the City’s position. In January 1961, the partners executed an agreement effective December 31, 1960, providing for Riddle’s withdrawal, transfer of his interest in partnership assets, assumption of liabilities by Sharp and Ordelheide, payments to Riddle, and freedom to compete. It neither mentioned nor purported to assign the City contract. Evidence showed that Riddle remained available to complete outstanding contracts and worked collectively with his former partners, although his willingness had not been communicated to the City. (361–362 & n. 1.)

On January 27, Sharp and Ordelheide notified clients that they had acquired Riddle’s interest, asserting that the partnership had not dissolved and would continue the City work. On February 1, the city attorney said dissolution terminated the agreement and that no design work was due without further City instruction. Counsel for the engineers disputed termination and offered continued performance. The council nevertheless resolved on February 21 that the agreement had ended and authorized negotiations with other engineers. When solicited for a new bid, Sharp declined because his firm already had a contract, again offering performance. (362–363 & n. 2.)

The trial judge found that the mayor favored another firm that would admit Riddle, at a higher fee than the original agreement. A council dispute caused that firm to withdraw and the mayor to resign. The City ultimately employed Black & Veatch, a firm unrelated to either Sharp or Riddle, to complete the remaining work. These were trial findings, rather than merely Sharp’s accusations, and supported rejection of the City’s stated justification. (363, 367.)

## Municipal authorization

Missouri required municipal contracts within lawful powers, consideration to be performed afterward, a dated writing, signatures by authorized parties or agents authorized in writing, and a council journal. Contractors were charged with notice of these mandatory restrictions. The City argued that Sharp had failed to plead and prove proper authorization, emphasizing the absence of an authorizing ordinance in his proof. (363–364.)

The appellate court did not dispense with those requirements. It found a sufficient prima facie showing from the combined record: facial regularity, the clerk’s contemporaneous letter stating approval by mayor and council, pretrial admissions that the City entered the contract, and the City’s later resolution recognizing it. The City conceded the issue had not been litigated below, offered no contrary evidence, and did not deny an ordinance or council journal existed. The showing placed on the City the burden to come forward with evidence, which it failed to do. (364–365.)

## Personal services and the continuing firm

The City argued that professional-service contracts were personal and that a partner’s withdrawal entitled the client to abrogate the agreement. It also criticized Sharp’s competence and relations with officials. The trial judge found neither principal reliance on Riddle nor an inability of Sharp to perform; the City’s own witnesses acknowledged his engineering ability, and testimony about interpersonal difficulties was weak or hearsay. (365–366.)

The appellate court assumed, without deciding, that professional engineers could fall within the personal-service classification asserted by the City. That assumption did not answer this case. Under Missouri’s Uniform Partnership Act, dissolution changed the partners’ relation but did not terminate the partnership until winding up. Existing contracts ordinarily survived, and partners remained responsible for completing outstanding engagements. The court recognized that the City’s rights to demand performance did not automatically establish reciprocal rights of the remaining partners, then evaluated the actual circumstances. (366.)

The critical engineers remained available and capable of working collectively; the withdrawal agreement did not purport to assign this contract; and an unchallenged finding showed Riddle functioned collectively even into 1963. The City’s account of supervisory discontinuity was undermined by those facts and by its soliciting the continuing firm for a new bid. The underlying mayor–Riddle dealings further supported the trial result. The holding therefore depended on continued availability and capacity, rather than an unrestricted rule permitting replacement of personally selected professionals. This is the qualification emphasized by the casebook’s account. (366–367; assigned printed 1220.)

## Timing of the second phase

The City characterized the contract as severable, arguing that separately compensated phases and the words “when so instructed” let it terminate after preliminary work and seek cheaper performance elsewhere. The court instead read that language as fixing when design work should begin. It protected the City from ordering plans for a project lacking a successful bond vote or federal grant; both contingencies had been favorably resolved. A single agreement covered a single project, with continuous paragraph numbering and compensation provisions following the services, and grant administration itself extended into the later work. (367–368.)

Once the City proceeded with construction, it remained obligated to employ the original engineers. The court expressly left open whether passing the bond issue and obtaining the grant required the City to proceed at all. Timing protection was not a power to substitute a different engineer after deciding to carry out the project. (368.)

## Damages, costs, and interest

Both sides accepted lost profits as the measure; the dispute concerned net profit. Black & Veatch’s gross profit after actual project costs was $168,038.69. The trial judge declined to deduct its $12,535.75 project-manager salary from the estimate of Sharp’s profit because Sharp would perform that work himself for about ten hours weekly. Although the judge incorrectly described Black & Veatch as a corporation rather than a partnership, the relevant finding about Sharp’s available time and ability was not clearly erroneous. The estimated 27 percent allocation for operating expenses also had record support. The appellate judges might have chosen differently but found no basis to reverse. (368–369.)

A municipal verified-claims statute did not defeat costs or interest here. Its purpose was to give the City an opportunity to investigate and settle; the City already possessed the pertinent information and opportunity, and the statute did not apply universally to every judicial claim. The opinion’s paragraph numbered 3 introduces the objection as one to interest but expressly discusses denial of costs; the next paragraph separately resolves interest. Interest also fell within the complaint’s request for all proper relief. The judgment was affirmed in full. (369.)
