Hawkins v. McGee
84 N.H. 114, 146 A. 641 (1929)
Citation, court, and assigned scope
The Supreme Court of New Hampshire decided the case on June 4, 1929. Justice Branch
wrote the opinion; Justice Marble did not sit, and the participating judges concurred. The
course scan prints the formation discussion at pages 2–3 and the damages discussion at
pages 11–12. This brief combines those two excerpts and the remainder of the judicial
opinion. Reporter pinpoints below refer to the New Hampshire Reports pagination
reproduced in the online opinion.
Parties and their objectives
Hawkins was the patient who sought contractual compensation for a surgeon's allegedly
guaranteed result. McGee was the defendant physician. Hawkins's theory depended on
proving an undertaking to produce a specified condition of the hand. McGee disputed
whether his words could create that obligation and challenged the instructions governing
both contract formation and damages. The appeal therefore concerns a promise about the
result of treatment, rather than a general guarantee that every unsuccessful operation
constitutes malpractice. (84 N.H. at 115–19.)
Material facts
An electric-wire burn approximately nine years before the relevant treatment had left scar
tissue on the palm of Hawkins's right hand. McGee operated by removing a substantial
quantity of scar tissue and grafting skin from Hawkins's chest. The promised improvement
and the actual postoperative condition supplied the comparison needed to measure
contractual loss. The opinion describes the graft and resulting condition without making the
familiar classroom nickname for the hand the basis of its rule. (Id. at 115, 118.)
The evidence included two different kinds of statements. In answer to an inquiry about
hospitalization, McGee predicted three or four days in the hospital and a return to work a few
days later. The court treated those estimates as opinions about duration and recovery.
Separately, witnesses attributed to him a guarantee of a completely good or perfect hand.
The alleged guarantee, made before the decision to operate and in Hawkins's presence, was
the substantial basis for the contract claim. The difference between an estimate and an
undertaking had to be assessed in context. (Id. at 115–16.)
There was evidence that McGee had repeatedly solicited the opportunity to perform the
operation. Hawkins's counsel suggested in cross-examination that McGee wanted experience
in skin grafting. The court treated that as a theory the jury could accept if supported by its
assessment of the evidence; it did not independently find an experimental motive as an
established historical fact. If accepted, it helped explain why a physician might make
unusually strong assurances to secure consent. The evidence also permitted the conclusion
that Hawkins and his father took the alleged guarantee seriously. (Id. at 116.)

Procedural history and review posture
The formation question was submitted to a jury. On damages, the trial judge instructed the
jury to consider the patient's operative pain and suffering and injury beyond the preexisting
condition. McGee objected. The trial court subsequently set aside the verdict under its
assessment of damages and regarded an award exceeding $500 as excessive. Because the
Supreme Court rejected the legal measure underlying both the instruction and that
assessment, it did not decide whether the evidence otherwise supported the trial judge's
$500 assessment. (Id. at 116–18.)
McGee also requested instructions that would have required an identical subjective
understanding by physician and patient and that would have barred liability unless a further
operation could not correct the condition. The Supreme Court reviewed the legal adequacy of
those requests. It declined to address disputes about plaintiff's counsel's argument that were
unlikely to recur at another trial. The reported disposition is a new trial, not an appellate
calculation of Hawkins's final recovery. (Id. at 118–19.)
Issues
Could the evidence of the physician's words and surrounding conduct support a finding that
he undertook a contractual obligation to achieve a specified result? If so, should damages
restore the patient's preoperative position, reimburse the burden of undergoing treatment, or
compensate for the difference between the promised result and the actual result? The
ancillary instructional issues ask whether uncommunicated reservations defeat assent and
whether the prospect of corrective surgery necessarily defeats recovery. (Id. at 115–19.)
Rules and holdings
A statement framed as a guarantee can create a contractual undertaking when its objective
meaning and context permit the recipient reasonably to take it as an inducement to consent.
The evidence here justified submitting that question to the jury. Estimates of hospitalization
and recovery time, however, were predictions rather than promises on this record. A
speaker's undisclosed mental reservation does not control the legal meaning of an objectively
manifested undertaking. (Id. at 115–16, 119.)
If a good or perfect hand was promised, the governing compensation is the difference
between the value to the patient of the promised hand and the value of the actual hand,
together with incidental consequences within the parties' contemplation. Ordinary pain
necessarily incident to the agreed operation is part of what the patient undertook to bear to
obtain the promised result. It is not a separate measure of the failure to deliver that result.
Deterioration matters within the promised-versus-actual comparison; it should not be added
again as an independent, duplicative item. (Id. at 117–18.)
Actual judgment
The Supreme Court ordered a new trial. It sustained the legal possibility of the contract claim
while rejecting the damages instruction. It did not direct payment of $500, affirm an amount
awarded by the jury, or decide a final dollar value for the promised hand. Marble's
nonparticipation and the concurrence of the other judges are expressly recorded. (Id. at
118–19.)

Reasoning
The physician's language must be evaluated with the transaction
The court first separated statements that could reasonably bear a promissory meaning from
ordinary medical forecasts. Whether particular language is capable of the asserted
contractual interpretation is initially a legal question for the judge. Once that threshold is
met, the jury can decide whether the words were spoken and what the relevant
circumstances establish. The alleged explicit guarantee cleared that threshold; the
short-term recovery predictions did not. This protects a distinction between reassurance and
a deliberately assumed obligation without immunizing all professional statements from
contract law. (Id. at 115–16.)
The physician relied on the uncertainty inherent in surgery to argue that no reasonable
person would understand a guarantee literally. The court did not decide whether that
argument would be conclusive in every case lacking countervailing circumstances. Here, the
alleged solicitation and the possible incentive to obtain experience provided a factual setting
in which a result warranty was plausible. The reasoning is conditional on the jury's
evaluation, which is why a brief should not turn the patient's theory of motive into a finding
by the reviewing court. (Id. at 116.)
Contract damages are measured against performance of the undertaking
The promised improvement defines the protected interest. A comparison between the
preoperative and postoperative hand would miss the loss when an operation fails to improve
the hand but does not make it worse. The promised-versus-actual measure recognizes that
failure as a breach with potentially compensable consequences. Conversely, when the
operation worsens the hand, the same measure already captures the deterioration as part of
the total shortfall. Adding a second award for deterioration would count some harm twice.
(Id. at 117–18.)
The court supported that measure by analogy to a machine warranted to achieve a specified
function. In both situations the recipient bargains for an identified performance, and
damages compensate for its absence rather than merely returning what the recipient
supplied. The analogy addresses valuation and the content of the promise; it does not
establish that medical treatment is governed by sales legislation. The court also limited
incidental losses to consequences fairly within contemplation, preserving a separate
constraint on recovery beyond the basic difference in value. (Id.)
The expected burden of treatment is not itself a loss from breach
The patient would have experienced the ordinary pain of the operation even if the physician
had achieved the promised result. That pain was part of the cost the patient agreed to bear.
Treating it as an additional compensable consequence of nonperformance would put the
patient in a position different from the one promised by the bargain. The opinion's statement
concerns pain necessarily incident to the contemplated operation. It should not be converted
into an unqualified rule excluding every physical or psychological consequence from every
contract action. (Id. at 118; compare Sullivan's separate analysis.)

The rejected instructions would have imposed requirements the claim did not
contain
A request demanding that both parties internally understand the guarantee in the same way
ignored the external standard of assent. If the doctor objectively guaranteed the result and
the patient relied on that manifestation, a private reservation would not undo it. Likewise,
the requested corrective-surgery instruction was inconsistent with evidence that McGee had
refused another operation. The evidence could support an amount covering such an
operation even on the physician's own proposed theory. The court did not require Hawkins to
establish that all possible future medical correction was impossible. (Id. at 118–19.)
Separate opinions
There is no separate concurrence or dissent in the report. The concluding notation states that
Marble did not sit and that the others concurred. A nonparticipating judge should not be
counted as joining the judgment. (Id. at 119.)
Use in class and limits of the decision
For formation, identify the exact alleged words, the setting in which they were used, and the
recipient's understanding. For damages, state the promised condition, the actual condition,
and the expenses or suffering that would have occurred even with complete performance.
The relevant baseline changes the answer: the value of an unimproved hand can fall short of
a promised perfect hand even without additional injury. This is an analytical application of
the opinion's measure, not a new factual finding about Hawkins.
Keep the result distinct from its familiar doctrinal lesson. The court allowed the jury to
consider the contract theory and required a legally different assessment of damages at a new
trial. The brief also preserves the full opinion's discussion of the $500 assessment, objective
assent, corrective surgery, and nonparticipation, which would disappear in a summary
limited to the two classroom excerpts. No subsequent-treatment survey is implied by this
historical brief.
