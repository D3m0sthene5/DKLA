# National Tire Dealers & Retreaders Association, Inc. v. Brinegar

**Course:** Legislation, Regulation & the State  
**Citation:** 491 F.2d 31 (D.C. Cir. 1974)  
**Court and date:** United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit, January 8, 1974  
**Opinions:** Judge Wilkey's opinion, joined by Judge Tamm; Judge Spottswood W. Robinson III concurred separately in the disposition on practicability grounds.  
**Assigned source:** LRS November pages 1–165 scan, PDF pages 18–24, printed pages 592–598; Robinson's judicial concurrence is excerpted within editorial Notes on PDF pages 24–25, printed pages 598–599.  
**Brief ID:** LRS-MAX-TIRE

## Case orientation

The court partially vacated a permanent-labeling requirement for retreaded tires because the agency did not adequately connect the extra permanence requirement to safety or justify its economic practicability. Congress had expressly required permanent labels for some information, however, and those portions survived. Robinson agreed that the practicability analysis failed but considered the permanent labels' safety value sufficiently apparent. The decision therefore distinguishes a supported need for information from the costs and benefits of a particular means of supplying it.

## Facts and agency action

The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration adopted Standard No. 117 under the National Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety Act of 1966. Paragraph S6.3.2 required pneumatic passenger tires retreaded on or after February 1, 1974 to have specified information permanently molded into or onto a sidewall: size, maximum permissible inflation pressure, maximum load, actual number of plies or ply rating, tube type, and the applicable bias-belted or radial construction designation. (Opinion introduction; notes 1–3.)

Retreaders reused tire casings of many different sizes and constructions. Removing the old tread could remove markings on the shoulder. Approximately one-third of existing casings bore the necessary information where it would survive buffing. A new-tire labeling amendment, effective July 1, 1973, placed labels where they would remain through retreading, but it would take time for new casings to replace existing inventory. (Part III; notes 30–33.)

Using only suitably labeled casings could sharply reduce retread output. Relabeling others involved frequently changing information plates in hot molds. Industry comments described large numbers of possible combinations, burn hazards, increased expense, and unsuccessful labels. One company's experiment reported only 80% effectiveness and an additional cost exceeding $2.50 per retread, approximately 30%. Those were industry submissions raising concerns, not court-certified cost figures applicable to every producer. (Part III; notes 35–39.)

NHTSA believed methods already used for tire identification and recordkeeping could be adapted, and that sorting casings into groups could reduce plate changes. Its denial of reconsideration invoked all available information and agency expertise, while disclaiming an obligation to connect particular records to particular determinations. The challenger's central position was that information could be supplied on an affixed label at sale without imposing these permanent-labeling burdens.

## Procedural posture and statutory framework

The association petitioned for direct review of the standard. The agency had used informal notice-and-comment rulemaking, including a technical conference and several proposals and reconsideration stages. The association did not challenge the use of informal rather than formal procedures. The court applied the review approach established in *Automotive Parts*: review of legislative rulemaking materials remains searching for arbitrariness and irrationality, even though it differs from reviewing adjudicatory findings. This was not mandatory formal substantial-evidence review. (Introduction; notes 10–13.)

Section 103 required safety standards to be practicable, meet the need for motor vehicle safety, and be stated objectively. It also required consideration of reasonableness, practicability, and appropriateness for the equipment. Legislative history made clear that practicability included economic factors, reasonable cost, feasibility, and adequate lead time, rather than technological possibility alone. (Part III; notes 26–29.)

Section 201 separately specified permanent and conspicuous tire labeling, including the actual number of plies and maximum permissible load. Those express legislative choices constrained both the agency and the court. The distinction between actual ply number and a ply rating is consequential: Congress expressly specified the former, while the latter was among the discretionary requirements vacated.

## The majority's safety analysis

The court accepted that tire size, load, inflation, and construction information helped prevent mismatching, overloading, and overinflation. The contested point was permanence. An affixed label could inform the original purchaser at sale. Permanence could matter later if the tire changed vehicles, was resold, or had to be matched with replacements after a temporary label disappeared. (Part II; notes 17–21.)

The Secretary mentioned transfers between wheels and vehicles but supplied no record illustrations showing how frequent or important the relevant later transactions were. The court offered possible resale and replacement scenarios but found their frequency and significance unexplained. The government's acknowledgment that an expert could identify many tire characteristics by inspection further weakened the unexplained necessity of permanence. The majority did not find that labels in general lacked a safety relationship. It found the additional permanent-labeling relationship too remote on this record to justify the substantial asserted burden.

Congress had nonetheless specified permanent labels for actual ply number and maximum load. The court preserved those requirements even though its policy discussion could logically apply to them too. APA review of an agency's discretionary choice did not authorize the court to reweigh Congress's express requirement. The opinion's statement that no administrative-procedure test applied to an act of Congress was not a ruling that statutes were immune from every form of constitutional review. (Part II; notes 22–24.)

## Practicability: unsupported assurances did not answer the record

The agency could draw upon expertise and information in its files. But concrete comments about relabeling burdens required a persuasive explanation, record support, or an inherently plausible response. The court did not automatically accept interested parties' predictions. It found that those submissions raised serious doubts to which the agency responded inadequately. (Part III.)

The recordkeeping analogy was incomplete. Retreader identification generally remained constant across tires, and the week of production could be changed weekly when molds were cool. The proposed safety-label characteristics varied among individual casings. An existing method for one kind of information did not establish feasibility for the much more variable information required here. Likewise, sorting casings seemed plausible but lacked support showing whether sorting would be practicable and reasonably priced. (Part III; notes 40–41.)

The agency's refusal to identify which record materials supported its determinations made the problem more acute. Expertise could not overcome this record's contrary indications through an unsupported assertion alone. Under APA § 706(2)(A), the discretionary permanent-labeling requirements were arbitrary. This conclusion concerned the present record; it did not establish that no agency could ever support these requirements through additional analysis and evidence. (Part III; notes 42–46.)

## Robinson's concurrence

Robinson agreed with the partial vacatur because the Secretary had not adequately considered economic practicability. He did not share the majority's doubts about whether permanent safety information mattered. Transfers between vehicles and wheels made its continued availability a matter of common experience, sufficient to support the safety connection under the narrow review approach. Technological feasibility itself was also undisputed. (Concurrence, 491 F.2d at 41–43; concurrence notes 1–2.)

The missing analysis was economic. Approximately thirty million tires were retreaded each year, and the labeling requirement would affect an estimated twenty million lacking suitable existing labels. Numerous characteristic combinations required changes of labeling slugs. Those operational demands affected producers and motorists who depended on affordable retreads. Statutory practicability therefore required examination of cost and feasibility. Robinson found no acceptable support for the Secretary's conclusion on that question. His concurrence is not an endorsement of the majority's entire safety rationale. (Concurrence notes 3–11.)

## Judgment, statutory qualifications, and exam use

The court vacated the portion requiring permanent labels of size, maximum inflation pressure, ply rating, tube type, and applicable bias-belted or radial construction. It preserved the express congressional requirements of actual ply number and maximum load. The precise disposition was **vacated in part**, rather than wholesale invalidation of Standard No. 117. (Introduction and final paragraph of Part III.)

Notes 6 and 45 add that Congress also mandated permanent labeling of ply material composition, which NHTSA had omitted after deciding it was insufficiently related to safety. The agency could not substitute that policy judgment for Congress's express command. The surviving obligations could remain difficult and costly, but involved fewer items than the full challenged rule. The court did not itself manufacture a new regulatory text.

For an exam, separate the safety value of providing information, the additional value of permanence, and the economic feasibility of providing it in the chosen form. Then identify which choices Congress made directly and which it delegated. *Automotive Parts* and this case applied the same general review approach but reached different results because one record adequately explained and supported the tradeoffs and the other did not. Robinson's narrower agreement should be stated when identifying the rationale shared by all three judges.
