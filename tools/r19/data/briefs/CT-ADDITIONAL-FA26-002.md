# Properties of the Villages, Inc. v. Federal Trade Commission
2024 WL 3870380 (M.D. Fla. Aug. 15, 2024), No. 5:24-cv-316-TJC-PRL, preliminary injunction and incorporated bench ruling, ECF No. 59 | Contracts

## Facts, claims, and posture

Properties of the Villages was a real-estate broker in The Villages whose agents were subject to noncompete clauses. It challenged the FTC’s 2024 Non-Compete Clause Rule, which would prohibit nearly all existing and future employment noncompetes and was scheduled to take effect September 4. The broker filed suit June 21 and sought a preliminary injunction and stay July 2. The district court found federal-question jurisdiction, proper venue in the Ocala Division, and standing because the broker was subject to the rule it challenged. The decision addressed interim protection before a final merits determination. (Transcript 4–6.)

The four counts asserted no substantive rulemaking authority over unfair competition, an excessive exercise of any such authority, impermissible retroactivity, and a Commerce Clause violation. Judge Timothy J. Corrigan specifically noted that the complaint did not allege arbitrary and capricious rulemaking. After briefing and amici submissions, he announced his reasons from the bench rather than delay a ruling until a polished written opinion could be prepared. The August 15 written order incorporated that ruling and attached the complete excerpt transcribing the judge’s decision. This excerpt supplies the judicial reasons; it is not merely counsel’s description of them. (Order 1–2; Transcript 3–6.)

## Standard and the arguments that did not establish likely success

The broker had to clearly establish a substantial likelihood of success, imminent irreparable injury, favorable balance of harms, and consistency with the public interest. With the government opposing relief, the latter two factors merged. A plausible or nonfrivolous legal theory alone would not establish likely success. The court applied Loper Bright’s requirement of independent statutory judgment while recognizing that a lawful delegation still had to be respected. It treated the standards for the requested preliminary injunction and stay as essentially the same. (Transcript 6–8.)

The court initially rejected the contention that the FTC lacked any substantive competition-rulemaking power. Sections 5 and 6(g), read together, coupled the directive to prevent unfair methods of competition with rulemaking authority. The broker argued that section 6(g)’s location amid ministerial powers, lack of penalty and procedural provisions, the more detailed unfair-or-deceptive-practices regime, and fifty years without comparable rulemaking made a substantive grant implausible. Those arguments had some force but did not demonstrate a substantial likelihood of prevailing on the general authority question. Nothing limited section 6 to procedural rules. The 1975 amendment preserved competition-rulemaking authority, the 1980 amendments contemplated substantial economic effects, and National Petroleum Refiners and JS&A had upheld substantive authority. The court also agreed with ATS that preventing unfair competition could require action before an unlawful practice occurred. (Transcript 9–13.)

The judge likewise found insufficient likelihood of success on the broker’s standalone interstate-commerce, separation-of-powers, and nondelegation theories. He was not persuaded that invoking the Sherman Act, traditional state regulation, or retroactivity independently established probable success. These were preliminary assessments, not final judgments comprehensively resolving every constitutional claim. They also did not settle whether this particular regulation fit within the general rulemaking authority the court was willing to recognize. (Transcript 13–14.)

## Why this rule presented a major question

The decisive distinction was between some substantive rulemaking authority and authority for this rule. The court read the major questions decisions as requiring clear congressional authorization for extraordinary agency assertions of economic and political power. It discussed both a constitutional clear-statement understanding and a contextual method of discerning statutory meaning. Either approach required skepticism toward a consequential power claim even when the agency had a plausible textual basis. Major questions analysis did not prohibit Congress from delegating such power; it required Congress to make that choice sufficiently clear. (Transcript 14–17.)

The economic scale was substantial by the FTC’s own estimates: approximately thirty million workers, or one fifth of the workforce, had affected noncompetes, and anticipated increased wages alone ranged from $400 billion to $488 billion over ten years. The judge acknowledged that higher wages might benefit workers. The point was the magnitude of transferred value and the regulation’s economic consequences, not a judicial finding that increased wages were undesirable. Aggregate compliance costs and other large financial effects further supported the extraordinary scale. (Transcript 17–18.)

The rule also displaced a politically contested field long governed by common-law contracts and state legislation. States had adopted varying restrictions, including outright bans in some jurisdictions, while attempts at federal legislation had not produced comparable regulation. The court regarded the nationwide rule as a consequential expansion of federal regulatory power. It recognized a countervailing factor: competition was within the FTC’s mission, and its statute anticipated rules with substantial monetary effects. On balance, however, the ban’s breadth and its reach to existing agreements made a major question substantially likely. An agency’s general mission did not dispense with the need for authority proportional to the specific assertion. (Transcript 18–19.)

Sections 5 and 6(g) did not clearly authorize that assertion in the court’s view. Section 5 addressed prevention through adjudication; section 6(g)’s rulemaking language shared space with classification amid reports and investigative powers. The FTC had not previously attempted competition-rulemaking of this magnitude. Its recent noncompete consent decrees, announced immediately before the proposed rule, did not turn the comprehensive ban into a historically ordinary incremental step. The judge qualified his discussion of earlier enforcement by noting a 1963 Snap-on matter he understood had been reversed. He did not silently treat the existence of that earlier proceeding as proof of a longstanding nationwide noncompete-regulation program. (Transcript 20–21.)

The court thus agreed with ATS that the major questions doctrine applied only to extraordinary cases and that competition regulation belonged to the FTC’s core mandate, but disagreed that the Commission had exercised its powers in this scope and manner before. General rulemaking language, in its text, placement, context, and history, fell short of the clear authorization needed. The broker established likely success on Count II, the excess-of-authority claim. The ruling did not determine the permissible limits of other FTC competition rules, such as a rule confined to hourly workers or a particular industry, and did not decide noncompetes’ ideal role as a policy matter. (Transcript 21–23.)

## Irreparable harm, equities, and exact relief

The court rejected the FTC’s contention that the broker forfeited an irreparable-harm claim by waiting to sue. The rule was not yet operative, and the short interval before its effective date made the timing understandable. Compliance would require reviewing contracts, deciding how to revise agreements, and adjusting business arrangements. Even disregarding the contested affidavit attached to the reply, the court found that such costs followed as a matter of common sense and had been recognized in the FTC’s own rulemaking. They would be more than de minimis and not readily recoverable from the government if the rule ultimately proved invalid. The associated disruptions supported an imminent injury finding under Eleventh Circuit authority. This reasoning differed from ATS’s treatment of ordinary expenses under Third Circuit precedent; the decisions should not be collapsed into one judicial harm finding. (Transcript 23–25.)

The merged equities and public-interest factors also favored preserving the status quo. Although courts ordinarily hesitate to interfere with the government’s implementation of legislation, the broker had shown that the rule was likely outside the statute’s authorization. The FTC would not be substantially harmed by interim nonenforcement against this plaintiff pending a final validity decision. The judge expressly rejected a general stay or nationwide injunction. (Transcript 25–26.)

The August 15 order granted the motion only to the stated extent: the FTC and its agents were enjoined from implementing or enforcing the rule against Properties of the Villages until further court order. No bond was required. The order did not set aside the regulation for everyone, grant final merits judgment, or award damages. Judge Corrigan decided alone; the order and incorporated oral ruling contain no judicial footnotes, concurrence, dissent, or panel vote. (Order 1–2; Transcript 26.)
