# T. B. Harms Co. v. Eliscu
339 F.2d 823 (2d Cir. 1964) | Civil Procedure

## Copyright arrangements and the competing ownership claims

The dispute concerned renewal rights in four songs written for the 1933 film *Flying Down to Rio*. Composer Vincent Youmans agreed with RKO Studios to assign certain recording and film-related rights for the original and renewal terms. RKO would hire lyric writers and obtain publishing rights for Youmans, who would pay customary royalties. Subject to those arrangements, Youmans could assign the music’s and lyrics’ publication and small performing rights. RKO hired Gus Kahn and Edward Eliscu as lyricists. Max Dreyfus, the principal stockholder of T. B. Harms Company, acquired Youmans’s retained rights and was designated to receive rights in the lyrics. The complaint alleged that Eliscu also assigned his original and renewal rights to Dreyfus by a June 30, 1933 agreement. Eliscu denied that crucial allegation. OpenJurist judicial ¶ 2.

As the original copyrights neared expiration, renewal applications were made by Youmans’s children, Kahn’s widow and children, and Eliscu. The Youmans and Kahn successors assigned their renewal rights to Harms. Eliscu instead recorded a February 1962 assignment to Ross Jungnickel, Inc., expressly subject to a judicial determination that he owned the rights he purported to assign. His lawyer notified ASCAP and the agent handling mechanical-recording fees that Eliscu claimed a half interest and that payments ignoring it would be made at the payors’ risk. He also demanded an accounting from Harms. Eliscu then sued in New York state court for a declaration of a one-third interest and an accounting. The differing fractional descriptions are part of the opinion’s factual account, rather than judicial findings establishing his share. ¶ 3.

Harms responded with a federal suit for equitable and declaratory relief against Eliscu and Jungnickel. It invoked 28 U.S.C. § 1338, which confers federal jurisdiction over actions arising under the copyright laws. Diversity was unavailable: Harms was incorporated in New York, and the defendants’ citizenship was concededly New York. Defendants challenged both jurisdiction and the sufficiency of the claim. Judge Weinfeld dismissed for lack of federal jurisdiction, and Harms appealed. ¶¶ 1, 4.

## The question: a federally created property right or a federal dispute?

Judge Friendly, writing for a panel including Kaufman and Anderson, agreed with the dismissal but explained why absence of infringement alone was not a complete jurisdictional test. Copyright infringement involves conduct reserved to the copyright owner by the federal statute. Conduct that threatens the economic value of a copyright is not necessarily infringement. Neither defendant had used or threatened to use the songs; they were asserting ownership through judicial proceedings, recorded instruments, and notices to interested parties. Thus, this was not a pleaded infringement suit. ¶ 5.

Nevertheless, the statute speaks of claims arising under copyright law, not merely infringement. A noninfringement ownership claim might require resolution of a genuine federal question. The task was to identify what, if anything, made this ownership dispute arise under the statute instead of ordinary contract law. ¶ 6.

## Statutory jurisdiction is narrower than Article III’s federal ingredient

Friendly distinguished Congress’s jurisdictional grants from the constitutional judicial power. *Osborn v. Bank of the United States* described an Article III federal ingredient sufficient even when antecedent and uncontested. The Supreme Court had given “arising under” a narrower meaning in statutes allocating cases to lower federal courts. Otherwise, every dispute over land originally granted through a federal patent could become a federal-question case even when the actual controversy turned entirely on local law or disputed facts. ¶ 7.

Patent and copyright ownership cases followed that narrower approach. A federal grant of property did not make every later title or contractual controversy nationally significant. *Wilson v. Sanford* and related decisions recognized that disputes about licenses and contracts could depend on ordinary equitable and contract principles. An expansive interpretation of § 1338 would have an additional consequence: because the jurisdiction at issue was exclusive, it would exclude state courts from disputes with little distinctively federal content. ¶¶ 8–10.

## Three possible paths to copyright jurisdiction

The first path was a remedy or cause of action supplied by federal copyright law. Holmes’s formulation—that a suit arises under the law creating its cause of action—readily explains infringement jurisdiction, even when an infringement case requires only factual determinations. But the applicable copyright statutes did not expressly create an action to enforce or rescind assignments or generally determine who owned assigned rights. The provision authorizing assignment by a written instrument did not itself impose the sort of federal duty that would support the proposed implied remedy. Friendly left room for materially different circumstances, such as a state’s refusal to enforce federally valid assignments, but Harms pleaded no such dispute. ¶¶ 11–12.

The second path was a state-created claim that required construction or application of federal law. Holmes’s formula was useful for inclusion but could not exclude every claim created by state law. *Smith v. Kansas City Title & Trust Co.* illustrated that point. More directly, *De Sylva v. Ballentine* adjudicated ownership of renewal terms without diversity or infringement because it involved substantial questions interpreting the Copyright Act. Footnote 1 explained why that exercise of jurisdiction could not sensibly be dismissed as unnoticed: the jurisdictional issue had been raised in the lower court. ¶ 13 & n.1.

Harms’s complaint presented no comparable interpretive issue. The central controversy was whether Eliscu had actually executed the alleged assignment, possibly together with interpreting the parties’ 1933 contract. Invoking the Act’s work-for-hire provision did not establish a statutory question: footnote 2 explained that the contract governed the allocation and the complaint did not reveal why construing that provision mattered. A copyright subject matter plus a disputed signature was therefore insufficient. ¶ 13 & n.2.

The third, more tentative path was a distinctive federal policy requiring federal principles to govern the claim. Friendly discussed federal common law and decisions in which a dominant federal interest displaced what otherwise looked like local law. He also cautioned that one would need to consider whether such jurisdiction rested on § 1331, rather than § 1338, with the jurisdictional consequences applicable at that time. Here no identified policy of the Copyright Act required a federal rule for this particular contract dispute. A general desire to ensure that property belongs to its true owner was too general. ¶¶ 14–15.

The court’s resulting formulation thus covered a remedy expressly granted by the Act, a claim requiring its construction, or, at least more doubtfully, a distinctive copyright policy requiring federal principles. It did not create federal jurisdiction over all controversies involving copyrights. ¶ 15.

## Alternative dismissal analysis and disposition

Friendly addressed earlier circuit ownership decisions that had apparently assumed jurisdiction. One also involved diversity; another had been presented as infringement litigation. He did not determine whether the latter complaint independently satisfied the newly articulated criteria. Those unexplained assumptions did not establish jurisdiction over Harms’s complaint. ¶ 16.

The court also considered *Bell v. Hood*: a sufficiently substantial attempted federal claim can confer jurisdiction even if it ultimately fails on the merits. Friendly assumed, without deciding, that failure to state a claim might have been the better dismissal ground for the asserted federal claims. The disposition still stood. Any resulting ability to hear related state claims did not require exercising it after the federal claim disappeared before trial, particularly when a prior state action seeking the same relief was pending. Footnote 3 added that neither dismissal characterization determined whether Eliscu signed the assignment or what his RKO contract meant. ¶ 17 & n.3.

The judgment was affirmed. Harms lost this federal proceeding; the court did not declare Eliscu the true owner, resolve the disputed assignment, or award either side copyright royalties. Friendly authored the panel opinion, with no concurrence or dissent reproduced. ¶ 18.
