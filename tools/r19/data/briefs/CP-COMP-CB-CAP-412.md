# Dairy Queen, Inc. v. Wood

**369 U.S. 469 (1962).** The Supreme Court decided the case on April 30, 1962. Justice Black wrote the Court’s opinion. Justice Harlan concurred, joined by Justice Douglas; Justice Stewart concurred in the result. Justices Frankfurter and White did not participate. The participating Justices agreed on the judgment, but not all joined Black’s reasoning. The assigned account is CivPro physical page 540, printed pages 1061–1063.

## Facts and procedural path

The dispute concerned the exclusive right to use the Dairy Queen trademark in portions of Pennsylvania under a December 1949 licensing agreement. The licensee undertook to pay approximately $150,000: a small initial payment, followed by half its receipts from sales and franchises involving the mark, subject to minimum annual payments. The petitioning corporation obtained its rights by assignment. The original licensees joined the trademark owners’ suit because they remained potentially responsible for the payments and claimed their own royalties. The Court restricted its analysis to the trademark owners’ claims because the other plaintiffs presented no different issue relevant to the jury question. (473–474 & n.9.)

In August 1960, the owners notified the licensee that its failure to pay constituted a material breach and that its franchise would be canceled unless the default was remedied. The complaint alleged more than $60,000 unpaid, continued operation after cancellation, trademark infringement, unstable finances, and threatened irreparable injury. It sought temporary and permanent injunctions against further dealings in the franchise and trademark; an accounting and judgment for the money owed; and an injunction against collecting money from stores pending that accounting. These were allegations and requested remedies, not established findings of breach, infringement, or financial instability. (474–475 & n.10.)

The corporation denied breach and alleged that a January 1955 oral modification had removed the minimum-payment requirement, leaving only the obligation to remit half its receipts. It also pleaded laches, estoppel arising from the owners’ delay while it invested in the business, and antitrust violations. Its answer included a timely Rule 38 jury demand. The district court struck that demand because it regarded the action as either purely equitable or as presenting legal issues merely incidental to equitable issues. The Third Circuit denied a petition for mandamus without an opinion. The Supreme Court reviewed that denial. (470, 475–476 & nn.1–2, 11.)

## Issue and governing distinction

Could a court deny a timely jury demand on disputed legal issues because the complaint predominantly sought equitable relief and styled its monetary demand as an accounting? The Court held that it could not do so here. The monetary demand was legal under every plausible construction, and the legal factual issues had to be tried by a jury before final resolution of equitable claims sharing those issues. The Court did not decide which party would ultimately prevail on the contract or trademark merits. (473, 476–480.)

The federal procedural merger of law and equity permitted legal and equitable claims to be joined in one action. It did not extinguish the jury right attaching to legal issues. The Court traced that distinction through Scott v. Neely and Rules 18 and 38: the old requirement of separate proceedings had changed, while the constitutional protection remained. Trying equity first could effectively eliminate that protection if the judge’s factual findings bound the later legal determination. Beacon Theatres therefore limited that practice to imperative circumstances the Court could not anticipate given the flexible federal procedures. This case presented no such circumstances. Calling the legal issues “incidental” could not avoid their jury entitlement. (470–473 & nn.3–8.)

## Why the monetary claim remained legal

The complaint could be understood as demanding contractual arrears through termination plus trademark damages thereafter, as invoking both theories over a broader period, or as resting on one theory alone. Black found it unnecessary to settle the ambiguity. Collection of a contractual debt was a traditional legal action; damages for trademark infringement were likewise cognizable at law. Every plausible reading therefore included a legal demand for money. Note 13 rejected as particularly unlikely the concurrence’s reading that the complaint sought only an infringement accounting, emphasizing the express allegation of money due under the contract. The disagreement concerned characterization of the pleading, not whether these monetary issues could be withheld from the jury. (476–477 & nn.12–15.)

The owners relied on their choice of the word “accounting.” That label could not determine the constitutional jury right. An equitable accounting on a cause otherwise cognizable at law required an inadequate legal remedy—for example, accounts so complicated that only equity could satisfactorily resolve them. Procedural mechanisms supporting a jury, including the then-existing Rule 53 power to appoint a master in exceptional circumstances, made such a showing more difficult. Note 18 warned that even this limited use of a master should be uncommon. Note 19 explained that procedural advances could reduce the need for traditional equitable remedies rather than expand equity’s power to displace juries. (477–478 & nn.16–19.)

This was not a case requiring that exceptional remedy. A properly instructed jury could determine the recovery under contract, trademark, or combined theories. Consulting business records to calculate damages did not itself make the legal remedy inadequate. The Court thus rejected an inference from the need to inspect accounts to the absence of a jury right. (478–479.)

The asserted defenses did not transform the legal claim either. In particular, the alleged oral modification concerned what agreement actually governed the parties. The Court treated it as an ordinary legal question about the contract’s terms, not an equitable request for novation or reformation. On the debt claim, the jury could determine the contract, whether it was breached, and damages if any. The opinion identified that defense as sufficient to illustrate why factual legal issues were present; it did not adjudicate all defenses or establish that the oral modification had occurred. (479.)

## Sequence, judgment, and separate views

Because the breach-related factual questions were shared with the requested equitable relief, the legal claims had to be determined before the court’s **final** disposition of the equitable claims. The qualification matters: note 20 expressly preserved the district court’s power to grant temporary relief pending final adjudication. Such relief had already been granted, and its propriety was not before the Supreme Court. Dairy Queen therefore does not announce that a jury must act before every provisional injunction in a mixed action. (479 & n.20.)

The Supreme Court **reversed the Third Circuit’s judgment and remanded** for proceedings consistent with its opinion. It explained that the appellate court should have corrected the district judge’s error by granting mandamus. The mandate was about protecting the jury trial, not awarding the plaintiffs money or resolving trademark liability. (480.)

Harlan, joined by Douglas, was more inclined to read the complaint as seeking an accounting for trademark infringement rather than contractual damages. Nonetheless, the accounting sought did not involve a claim exclusively cognizable in equity or accounts too complicated for a jury. A jury could calculate infringement damages under proper instructions just as courts of law handled analogous copyright and patent claims. The action therefore joined legal and equitable prayers, and the legal claim retained its constitutional jury right. His sole starred note clarified that apart from the damages demand, the complaint sought equitable relief. Stewart’s concurrence in the result supplied no separate reasoning. The report records Frankfurter’s and White’s nonparticipation. (480–481.)
