# Cargo of the Brig Aurora v. United States

## Parties

The United States sought forfeiture of merchandise imported aboard the brig Aurora. Robert Burnside, described as a citizen of Orleans, claimed the goods as his property and appealed their condemnation. The proceeding was directed against the cargo, which explains the vessel-related caption; the controversy concerned the lawfulness of the importation and the cargo's eligibility for an exemption. Burnside's assertion of ownership and his own citizenship did not conclusively establish the national ownership of the merchandise. 11 U.S. at 382, 387–388.

## Material Facts

The Nonintercourse Act of March 1, 1809, prohibited specified imports from Great Britain and France after May 20, 1809, and provided for forfeiture of goods imported in violation. After extensions, that legislation expired on May 1, 1810. A statute enacted that day supplied a conditional revival mechanism. If either country revoked or modified its restrictions so that they ceased to violate American neutral commerce, the President was to proclaim that fact. If the other country failed to follow within three months, designated provisions of the earlier act would revive against it. Congress thus specified both the relevant international event and its domestic legal consequences. Id. at 382–384. 

On November 2, 1810, the President proclaimed that France had made the required change. The Aurora cleared Liverpool on December 11, sailed on December 16, and arrived in New Orleans between February 2 and February 20, 1811. The proclamation had become known in Liverpool on December 13. The cargo was seized on February 20 under the restrictions asserted to have revived against Great Britain on February 2, three months after the proclamation. The sequence made the statute's effective date consequential to this particular importation. Id. at 382, 384–385. 

Congress subsequently enacted an exemption on March 2, 1811, covering qualifying vessels wholly owned by United States citizens that had departed a British port before February 2 and qualifying wholly American-owned merchandise imported in those vessels. Burnside needed to establish the ownership condition. The bill of lading said the goods were shipped on account of a United States citizen and consigned to Burnside but did not identify that owner. Testimony identified a Liverpool shipper without establishing on whose account he acted. The Court found the ownership evidence insufficient. The full opinion supplies these evidentiary details; the assigned excerpt compresses them into an editorial statement. Id. at 384, 387–388; casebook p. 46.

## Procedural History

The District Court for the District of Orleans condemned the cargo on a forfeiture libel, the pleading initiating the proceeding against the goods. Burnside appealed to the Supreme Court. He challenged the restrictions' revival and effective date, invoked the American-ownership exemption, and objected that the government's libel had not alleged the absence of American ownership. The Court therefore addressed statutory authority and timing together with proof of an exemption and the sufficiency of the pleading. Id. at 382, 387–389. 

The assigned excerpt also reproduces competing arguments by counsel. Burnside's advocate contended that making revival depend on a presidential proclamation transferred legislative power. The government's advocate answered that Congress had prescribed evidence of a fact upon which its law would operate. Those statements are arguments; Johnson's ensuing explanation is the Court's reasoning. Casebook p. 46.

## Issue

Could Congress make revival of the expired import restrictions depend on a future event declared by presidential proclamation? If revival was authorized, did the restrictions resume on February 2, 1811, or remain suspended until a renewed May 20 starting date? Separately, had Burnside proved the statutory American-ownership exemption, and was the government's pleading defective because it did not negate that exemption? 11 U.S. at 387–389.

## Rule and Holding

Congress could exercise its legislative discretion to revive an earlier law expressly or conditionally. The earlier enactment's expiration provision did not prevent Congress from extending its operation upon a later combination of events. The Court rejected the challenge to this conditional revival arrangement. Its holding concerns Congress's specified statutory condition and consequence; it does not recognize an independent presidential power to revive expired legislation. Id. at 388. 

Revival restored the earlier act with the force it possessed when it expired, rather than restarting the original waiting period. The restrictions consequently operated against Great Britain from February 2, 1811. Burnside failed to establish American ownership sufficient for the exemption, and the libel did not have to plead the absence of a fact constituting his defense or an exception to the statute. Id. at 388–389.

## Judgment

The Court rejected the challenges to the cargo's condemnation: the restrictions applied when the goods arrived, the ownership exemption was unproved, and the pleading objection failed. The authenticated report reprint expressly closes the case with ‘Sentence affirmed.’ The cargo’s condemnation was affirmed. Justia’s text omits that closing sentence; it is present in the scanned judicial report. Id. at 388–389.

## Reasoning

Johnson located the decision to revive the law in Congress. A legislature could have restored the restrictions directly; it could also specify circumstances upon which restoration would occur. The expiration clause in the original act limited that enactment's duration but did not exhaust Congress's subsequent power over the subject. The 1810 statute supplied the new legislative authorization, and the President's proclamation operated within that authorization. Id. at 388. 

The timing question required interpreting what Congress meant by revival with full force and effect. When the earlier act expired, its initial May 20 waiting period had already passed. Restoring the act as it then stood therefore restored an immediately operative prohibition. Carrying the old calendar date forward into a new year would postpone the restrictions despite the later statute's express three-month mechanism. The Court found that interpretation inconsistent with the words Congress used and fixed February 2 as the operative date. Id. at 388–389. 

The exemption failed because readily obtainable evidence had not been produced. Burnside could have supplied correspondence or testimony identifying the owner for whose account the merchandise was shipped. An unnamed citizen in a bill of lading and a consignment to Burnside left that question unanswered. Witnesses who identified the Liverpool shipper were not shown to have established the beneficial ownership. The Court treated the absence of accessible proof as supporting the conclusion that the property was not American. The full opinion thus rests on evidentiary insufficiency, rather than simply declaring that shipment from Britain necessarily defeated American ownership. Id. at 388. 

The pleading objection sought to put the ownership exception into the government's affirmative allegations. The Court instead treated American ownership as a defense or exception that the claimant had to establish. The libel could state the prohibited importation without anticipating and negating that ground for relief. This procedural holding explains why failure to plead the exception did not rescue goods whose claimant had failed to prove it. Id. at 389.

## Notes

The assigned placement emphasizes conditional legislation. The useful analytical sequence is to identify the policy Congress enacted, the fact or event made relevant, the official assigned to ascertain or proclaim it, and the consequence the statute itself attaches. Here the consequence was revival of designated restrictions after a specified interval. That account explains the decision without importing the later terminology of an intelligible principle into the 1813 opinion. Casebook pp. 45–47; 11 U.S. at 384, 388–389. 

A sound account also preserves the forfeiture setting. Even a successful delegation defense would not answer whether a particular cargo qualified for an exception, and an ownership defense depended on proof rather than the claimant's nationality alone. The ownership and pleading discussions above supplement the shortened assigned excerpt from the full judicial report. Id. at 387–389.
