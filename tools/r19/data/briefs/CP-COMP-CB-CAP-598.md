# DIRECTV, Inc. v. Imburgia

**Courses:** Civil Procedure; Contracts

## Citation

DIRECTV, Inc. v. Imburgia, 577 U.S. 47–72, 136 S. Ct. 463 (2015). Supreme Court of the United States, No. 14–462. Argued October 6, 2015; decided December 14, 2015. Justice Breyer delivered the Court's opinion, joined by Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Scalia, Kennedy, Alito, and Kagan. Justice Thomas dissented separately. Justice Ginsburg dissented, joined by Justice Sotomayor. The judgment was 6–3. Assigned accounts: Civil Procedure casebook p. 1401 and note 4 at pp. 1403–1404, repository PDF pp. 705–706; Contracts C&M p. 718, third repository volume, PDF p. 61. These are two accounts of the same Supreme Court judgment under parallel reporter citations.

## Parties and material facts

DIRECTV supplied television service under a consumer agreement. Amy Imburgia and Kathy Greiner were California customers who brought a putative class action in 2008 seeking damages for early termination fees they alleged violated California law, including the Consumers Legal Remedies Act. The Supreme Court decided arbitration enforceability, rather than whether those charges actually violated consumer law. 577 U.S. at 50, 60.

Section 9 required binding arbitration of asserted claims and barred joining or consolidating claims in arbitration. It also provided that the whole arbitration section would be unenforceable if the customer's state's law made the class-arbitration waiver unenforceable. Section 10 specified that the Federal Arbitration Act governed section 9. Those two provisions created the interpretive dispute: did the state-law reference incorporate California's consumer protections independently of federal preemption? Id. at 50.

When the 2007 version of this agreement was adopted and the customers sued, California's Discover Bank rule made certain consumer class-arbitration waivers unconscionable. The rule concerned adhesive consumer agreements involving predictably small damages and specified circumstances. In 2011, Concepcion held that the FAA preempted that rule. Ginsburg emphasized DIRECTV's earlier representation that it would not seek arbitration of California customers' disputes and its subsequent effort to compel arbitration after Concepcion. Id. at 50–51, 60, 66.

## Procedural history

The California trial court denied DIRECTV's request to compel arbitration. The Court of Appeal affirmed, reasoning that the parties could choose California law without its federal preemption and had done so. It regarded the state-law invalidation condition as a specific qualification of the more general FAA provision and applied the rule construing ambiguous language against the drafter. Its decision is reported at 225 Cal. App. 4th 338, 170 Cal. Rptr. 3d 190 (2014). Id. at 51–53.

The state appellate court discussed the CLRA provisions prohibiting waiver of the right to bring class claims. The Supreme Court treated that relevant application as embodying the Discover Bank rule, noting California's recognition of Concepcion's effect and the parties' agreement on that point. The California Supreme Court declined discretionary review. DIRECTV sought certiorari, pointing to a contrary Ninth Circuit interpretation of the same language in Murphy v. DirecTV. The Supreme Court granted review of the California judgment. Id. at 51–53.

## Issue

Did the California court's interpretation of the contractual state-law condition violate the FAA's requirement to treat arbitration agreements on the same footing as other contracts, even assuming that interpretation correctly stated California contract law? Id. at 53–55.

## Rule and holding

FAA section 2 makes covered written arbitration agreements enforceable subject to grounds applicable to contracts generally. State contract interpretation ordinarily belongs to state courts, but the FAA preempts a construction that singles out arbitration contracts for unfavorable treatment. The Supreme Court concluded that California courts would not interpret comparable state-law references in other kinds of contracts to include law already invalidated by superior federal law. The construction here therefore failed the equal-treatment requirement and was preempted. Id. at 49, 54–59.

This was not a holding that parties lack power to select otherwise preempted rules by agreement. The Court expressly acknowledged substantial freedom to select governing rules, including the Discover Bank rule independently of Concepcion. It held that the particular state-court interpretation of this language did not comply with the FAA. Id. at 53–55.

## Judgment

The Supreme Court reversed the California Court of Appeal and remanded for proceedings consistent with its opinion. It directed that the arbitration agreement be enforced. It did not award damages, decide the merits of the early termination charges, or itself issue an arbitral award. Id. at 58–59.

## Reasoning

Binding federal precedent supplied the starting point. State judges could disagree with Concepcion but could not deny its controlling federal effect. That premise alone did not resolve the case, because the parties could contractually choose rules that federal law would otherwise displace. The Court therefore assumed the state court correctly interpreted California law and examined whether that interpretation respected the FAA. It tested the reasons the state court actually offered, rather than hypothetical grounds that might have supported the same outcome. Id. at 53–54.

Breyer gave six connected reasons for finding unequal treatment. First, absent an indication to the contrary, a contractual reference to state law ordinarily means valid state law. Neither the respondents nor the dissent supplied a comparable contract decision reading such language to include law already held invalid. The majority did not find the phrase ambiguous. Second, California's general contract principles incorporated the state's reserved power to change the law retroactively, and judicial statutory construction ordinarily operates retroactively. The contracting parties' likely understanding in 2007 therefore did not settle the later legal question. Id. at 55–56.

Third, the appellate court supplied no general explanation that would apply equally to contracts affected by federal labor, pension, antidiscrimination, or constitutional law. Its approach appeared confined to arbitration. Fourth, its framing of the question expressly concerned FAA preemption rather than a general doctrine about incorporating displaced law. Fifth, its statements that the waiver remained unenforceable under California law treated the invalidated rule as retaining independent force, an approach the Court considered unlikely outside arbitration. Id. at 56–57.

Sixth, neither the specific-over-general canon nor construction against the drafter established that the same reading would apply elsewhere. The first canon did not answer whether the state-law reference incorporated invalid law. The second required limits, and the absence of comparable nonarbitration applications undermined the asserted neutrality of this interpretation. Taken together, these points showed discrimination against arbitration rather than application of a generally applicable contract rule. Id. at 57–59.

## Thomas's dissent

Thomas would affirm because he continued to interpret the FAA as inapplicable to state-court proceedings. Under that view the statute did not require this California court to compel arbitration. His single-paragraph dissent rested on the statute's forum reach, not Ginsburg's analysis of contractual consent. Id. at 59.

## Ginsburg and Sotomayor's dissent

Ginsburg regarded the state court's interpretation as both reasonable and correct. Arbitration depends on consent; the FAA ordinarily requires enforcement of the agreement the parties made. DIRECTV chose a conditional state-law reference in a standardized agreement, and the customers had neither drafted the provision nor reason to anticipate Concepcion four years later. The California court permissibly gave the customers the benefit of ambiguity under ordinary contract principles. Id. at 59–62, 65–67.

She distinguished federal compulsion from contractual incorporation. Concepcion prevented a state from imposing class procedures against the parties' agreement, but parties could still choose California consumer protections. Her first footnote explained that FAA preemption depends on contractual intent in a way other categories of federal preemption do not. Her second distinguished California's unchanged legislative CLRA policy from the judicial Discover Bank rule and emphasized that consumer protections continued to operate when parties selected judicial resolution. Id. at 62–64 & nn.1–2.

The particular wording mattered. Other versions of DIRECTV's agreement conditioned severability directly on preserving the class-arbitration ban. The 2007 agreement instead referred to the customer's state law. Treating that formulation as equivalent to a direct federal-law condition gave the drafter the advantage of an avoidable ambiguity and disregarded contemporaneous expectations. Ginsburg also considered the presumption favoring arbitration inappropriate at this threshold dispute over whether an enforceable agreement existed. Id. at 64–67.

Her broader objection concerned practical access to relief. Small claims often produce no realistic individual proceeding, while take-it-or-leave-it terms prevent customers from bargaining over class remedies. Footnote 3 surveyed prior arbitration and effective-vindication decisions; footnote 4 discussed the CFPB's 2015 study of consumer arbitration. These are dissenting criticisms and predictions, not a majority holding abolishing every effective-vindication objection. Id. at 67–70 & nn.3–4.

Finally, Ginsburg contrasted the Court's approach with the FAA's original commercial setting and with European consumer protections. The EU discussion at official p. 71 is the passage reproduced in the Civil Procedure casebook's note 4. It forms part of her dissent, not the Court's governing rule or a present-day comparative-law verification. She and Sotomayor would affirm the California judgment. Id. at 70–72.

## Assigned notes and source reconciliation

The Civil Procedure account accurately identifies the contract's state-law contingency, the effect of Concepcion, and the separate dissents. Its adjoining note 4 reproduces the dissent's comparative consumer-arbitration discussion with the correct official pinpoint, p. 71. The surrounding seven notes raise arbitrator bias, precedent, international arbitration, repeat-player advantages, empirical outcomes, regulatory enforcement, and delegation of arbitrability. Those policy questions and other cases are contextual material; they are not additional factual findings or holdings in DIRECTV. Casebook pp. 1401–1406, repository pp. 705–707.

Contracts p. 718 compresses the decision into preemption of California law despite the contractual condition. The operative analysis is more precise: Concepcion had already invalidated the relevant rule, and DIRECTV tested whether the California court's interpretation of the agreement treated arbitration equally. The brief preserves the majority's acknowledgement of contractual choice and the dissent's disagreement about this actual agreement. The following Contracts discussion qualifies broader arbitration doctrine and begins a separate Gateway account; it is not a further DIRECTV holding.
