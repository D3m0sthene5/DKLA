# Harrington v. Purdue Pharma L.P.

**Citation and court:** 603 U.S. 204 (2024), Supreme Court of the United States, No. 23–124, decided June 27, 2024. Justice Gorsuch wrote for five Justices, joined by Thomas, Alito, Barrett, and Jackson. Justice Kavanaugh dissented, joined by Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Sotomayor and Kagan. The complete preliminary report includes seven Court notes and eight dissent notes; all were read, together with the complete dissent.

**Assigned reading:** Civil Procedure Supplement, printed pages 510–511, physical PDF page 344, the complete own-caption account between the preceding Jarkesy discussion and the Carrera heading. Text and original scan checked. The full official preliminary report, pages 204–278, supplies the complete decision and separate opinion. Justia corroborates the identity, date, lineup, and reversal/remand. Pinpoints use the official report; Justia still displays a blank United States Reports page and uses slip-opinion pinpoints.

**Facts and proposed plan.** Purdue marketed OxyContin as having a time-release formula that reduced addiction risks, expanded its promotion beyond traditional opioid applications, and earned approximately $34 billion in revenue between 1996 and 2019. Members of the Sackler family owned and controlled the company and participated in its business and marketing decisions. In 2007 a Purdue affiliate pleaded guilty to felony misbranding. Thousands of civil suits followed, including against family members. The majority recounted roughly $11 billion in distributions to the Sacklers between 2008 and 2016, draining approximately 75% of Purdue's assets. These were the historical figures in the record, not damages awarded here. 603 U.S. at 209–211.

Purdue filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in 2019; the Sacklers did not. They initially proposed contributing $4.325 billion over a decade in exchange for settlement of estate claims against them and an order releasing and permanently enjoining victims' current and future opioid-related claims. The latter would cover negligence, fraud, and willful misconduct claims and reach many family members and controlled entities, without every affected claimant's consent. Id. at 211–212.

The plan would reorganize Purdue as a public-benefit company and compensate individual victims at base amounts of $3,500 up to $48,000 before fees and expenses, with larger payments spread over years. Most returned ballots favored the plan, but fewer than 20% of eligible creditors voted, and thousands opposed it. The U.S. Trustee, certain governments, and victims objected. Id. at 212–213. Thus, overwhelming support among voters and universal claimant consent were different propositions.

**Procedural history.** The bankruptcy court confirmed the plan. The district court vacated, concluding that the Code did not authorize extinguishing claims against nondebtors without claimants' consent. While appealing, the Sacklers increased their proposed contribution by $1.175 to $1.675 billion, producing the approximately $5.5–$6 billion figure described in the supplement; eight objecting States and the District of Columbia withdrew objections. Other objections persisted. A divided Second Circuit revived the modified plan. Judge Wesley separately criticized the releases' statutory authority. The Supreme Court stayed the decision and treated the stay application as a certiorari petition to resolve a circuit split. Id. at 213–214 & n.1. The modified contribution was a proposed settlement term, not money awarded or a completed payment ordered by the Supreme Court.

**Issue and holding.** Does Chapter 11 authorize a release and injunction effectively discharging claims against a nondebtor without the affected claimants' consent? No. Neither §1123(b)(6)'s catchall nor the Code's other provisions supplied the authority claimed for the Sackler release. Id. at 214–227. This was a statutory decision about the nonconsensual nondebtor release, rather than an adjudication of the merits of opioid victims' tort claims.

**Text and context.** A debtor's estate generally includes virtually all of its assets; confirmation binds the debtor and its creditors and discharges certain debtor liabilities. Discharge ordinarily benefits the debtor and does not affect another entity's liability. The Sacklers sought substantially comparable protection without filing themselves or committing anything close to all of their assets. Id. at 214–215.

The first five optional-plan paragraphs of §1123(b) concern the debtor's claims, property, contracts, or relations with creditors. Paragraph (6) permits other appropriate provisions consistent with the Code. Applying ejusdem generis, the majority read the residual authority in light of the enumerated provisions. The catchall conferred additional powers, but not the radically different authority to extinguish victims' independent claims against nondebtors without consent. The word other tied the provision to its preceding context; appropriate was not permission for everything the Code did not expressly prohibit. Id. at 215–219.

Settlement of derivative claims under paragraph (3) did not establish authority over independent victim claims. A derivative claim substantively belongs to the corporation or estate, even when someone else asserts it. A claim against the Sacklers belonging to a victim does not become estate property merely because Purdue's conduct is a relevant factor. Id. at 219–220 & n.3. The majority accepted that bankruptcy addresses collective-action problems but denied that its general purpose eliminated statutory limits. Id. at 220–221 & n.4.

Note 2 also rejects §105(a) as an independent source: its authority to carry out Code provisions does not itself create the missing substantive power. Id. at 216–217 n.2. General equitable authority therefore did not cure the defect.

**Statutory scheme and history.** The Code ordinarily reserves discharge for a debtor submitting virtually all assets, subject to limitations concerning fraud, willful injury, and jury rights for personal-injury or wrongful-death claims. The proposed protection went beyond ordinary discharge while requiring less than ordinary debtor submission. Id. at 221–223. Congress expressly authorized certain third-party injunctions for asbestos bankruptcies in §524(g); that specificity illustrated how Congress could supply such power. Note 5 explains that the majority did not treat the asbestos provision as impairing authority already available elsewhere. Id. at 222–223 n.5.

Pre-Code practice generally reserved discharge to debtors surrendering their property. The majority found the text unambiguous and used history to confirm, rather than manufacture, its interpretation. Recent lower-court practice was divided, as the circuit split showed. Id. at 223–224 & n.6.

**Competing consequences and reserved questions.** Plan proponents predicted that losing the release would destroy victims' recovery. The Trustee argued that continued exposure could induce more favorable consensual settlements and warned against using bankruptcy to shield nondebtors from mass-tort liability. The majority left those competing policy judgments to Congress rather than choosing its statutory interpretation by the predicted recovery. Id. at 224–226. Note 7 presents competing arguments about indemnification, including the Trustee's reliance on a good-faith condition and statutory disallowance or subordination tools; it does not decide that every potential indemnification claim would be allowed or barred. Id. at 225 n.7.

The opinion expressly leaves consensual third-party releases undisturbed, does not define what counts as consent, does not pass upon plans fully satisfying nondebtor claims, and does not decide whether already effective and substantially consummated plans must be unwound. The plan here was stayed. Id. at 226–227. These reservations prevent turning the holding into a ban on every third-party settlement or an automatic reopening rule.

**Kavanaugh's dissent: statutory discretion and collective recovery.** The dissent would sustain the plan under §1123(b)(6)'s broad, context-dependent appropriate standard. Bankruptcy addresses creditors' race to exhaust limited assets, and nondebtor releases can preserve or expand a collective estate. Kavanaugh emphasized indemnified officers, shared insurance, substantial settlement contributions, and decades of mass-tort practice involving asbestos, Dalkon Shield, breast implants, the Boy Scouts, and religious entities. Id. at 227–244. He described seven lower-court safeguards: a close debtor relationship, intertwined claims, tailored scope, necessity to reorganization, substantial contribution, fair creditor payment, and overwhelming support. Those are the dissent's defense of the displaced approach, not the Supreme Court's adopted test. Id. at 241–243 & n.2.

Applied here, the dissent emphasized the Sacklers' indemnification agreement, Purdue's approximately $1.8 billion estate, the government's $2 billion superpriority claim, and a contribution expanding the overall estate to about $7 billion. It relied on bankruptcy findings that direct litigation and collection faced substantial obstacles and that the negotiated plan offered the best prospect of fair recovery. More than 95% of voting victims and creditors supported it, and all States had ultimately signed on. Id. at 244–256 & nn.3–4. These arguments disputed the majority's statutory conclusion; the Supreme Court did not award the plan's funds or find that no alternative recovery could ever occur.

**Dissent's textual response.** Kavanaugh disputed the majority's common genus. Paragraph (3) already allowed settlement of estate claims against nondebtors, affecting derivative creditor claims; paragraph (6), in his view, could also reach direct claims threatening the estate. Consensual releases, full-satisfaction releases, exculpation clauses, and Energy Resources illustrated broader authority. He argued that purpose should identify the relevant similarity: protecting the estate and securing equitable recovery. Id. at 256–268 & nn.5–6.

He further invoked the asbestos legislation's savings language, read §524(e) as stating that debtor discharge does not automatically relieve others rather than prohibiting an additional release, and distinguished a limited negotiated release from a blanket bankruptcy discharge. He viewed post-1978 practice and related-to jurisdiction, including third-party litigation stays, as more informative than superseded earlier practice. Id. at 268–276 & nn.7–8. He predicted severe losses, delay, and renewed races to recovery and urged congressional action. Id. at 276–278. Those were dissenting interpretations and predictions, not findings adopted by the five-Justice Court.

**Exact disposition.** The Supreme Court **reversed the Second Circuit's judgment and remanded for further proceedings consistent with the opinion**. Id. at 227. It did not affirm confirmation, enter tort judgments against the Sacklers, fix a replacement contribution, or decide the ultimate form of a lawful reorganization.

**Course significance.** Mass-tort coordination and creditor support cannot substitute for statutory authority to extinguish independently held claims. Keep estate claims, victim claims, consent, and debtor discharge distinct. The assigned account contrasts Gorsuch's statutory limits with Kavanaugh's collective-recovery rationale; the full decision adds the precise reservations and confirms that both sides' future-recovery assessments were arguments.
