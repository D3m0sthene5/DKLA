# Ortiz v. Fibreboard Corp.

**Souter, J., for seven Justices: Rehnquist, O'Connor, Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, and Ginsburg joined. Rehnquist also concurred, joined by Scalia and Kennedy. Breyer dissented, joined by Stevens.**

## The actual dispute and the proposed solution

Fibreboard manufactured asbestos products from the 1920s until 1971. Decades later it faced thousands of personal-injury claims and potentially many more from people whose exposure had not yet produced disease. Its own assets were much smaller than its projected liability, but two old insurance policies offered a possible source of substantial recovery. Continental's policy covered a period from May 1957 to March 1959 and had per-person and per-occurrence limits without an aggregate limit. Pacific's disputed policy covered an earlier period. California litigation over those policies had produced a favorable trial judgment for Fibreboard, but the insurers' appeal threatened to eliminate or greatly diminish that recovery. The settlement therefore involved both an asbestos-liability problem and a disputed insurance asset. 527 U.S. at 822–825.

Before the global settlement, Fibreboard had settled substantial numbers of individual claims. Some arrangements paid part immediately and deferred the balance pending favorable resolution of the insurance litigation; others assigned insurance rights to claimants. During the 1993 negotiations, counsel representing existing claimants arranged separate settlements for approximately 45,000 inventory claims. At least some of those agreements made full payment contingent on approval of the global settlement or favorable resolution of the insurance dispute. Those lawyers also negotiated for the proposed settlement class, creating an incentive to secure a deal that would unlock payment for their existing clients. The distinction between these inventory plaintiffs and the class was crucial to the Court's later analysis. Id. at 823–825, 852–855.

With appellate argument in the coverage case approaching, negotiations reached agreement on August 26, 1993. The global settlement would establish a $1.535 billion trust: $1.525 billion from the insurers and $10 million attributed to Fibreboard. Only $500,000 of that last amount came from Fibreboard's own assets; the rest came from other insurance. At class counsel's insistence, a separate backup Trilateral Settlement Agreement provided $2 billion from the insurers if the global settlement failed. That independent agreement had been approved and was not itself before the Supreme Court. Id. at 825–828 & nn. 4, 8.

The class principally included people with unfiled or withdrawn claims and people who might develop future injuries, together with related claimants. It excluded people whose actions were pending and certain people who had already settled while retaining rights to sue for later malignancy. Trust claimants would pass through settlement, mediation, and arbitration procedures before pursuing judicial recovery against the trust. Litigation recovery was capped at $500,000, with no punitive damages or prejudgment interest and payment over five to ten years; settled claims were payable over three years. The later chance to sue the trust was therefore different from a right to reject the class settlement and sue Fibreboard on ordinary terms. Id. at 826–827 & n. 5, 847 n. 23.

## Procedure, competing positions, and the question decided

The Eastern District of Texas certified a mandatory settlement class under Rule 23(b)(1)(B), finding a limited fund and approving the settlement after an eight-day fairness hearing. It relied on the risk of losing insurance coverage and on the company's assets and settlement proceeds as inadequate to meet projected claims. The Fifth Circuit affirmed. After Amchem, the Supreme Court returned the case for reconsideration; the Fifth Circuit again sustained certification, reasoning that the mandatory-class setting differed from Amchem's opt-out class and that the allocation did not discriminate among injury groups. Id. at 827–830.

Objectors challenged both the fund and the representation: the supposed limit resulted from negotiated payment figures, many similarly situated claimants were excluded, counsel represented competing groups, and Fibreboard retained virtually all its own value. Settlement proponents emphasized the danger that the insurance asset would disappear, the practical need for one binding resolution, common interests in maximizing recovery, and the costs avoided by settling. They also argued that separate actions might resolve insurance issues in ways impairing others' recovery. The Court did not treat the possible economic advantages as a substitute for the certification requirements. Id. at 848–864 & n. 33.

The Court decided that this settlement could not be certified as a mandatory limited-fund class. It did not decide whether Rule 23(b)(1)(B) could ever aggregate unliquidated mass tort claims. Certification was logically antecedent to the Article III objections in this case, and those objections remained unresolved. The Court likewise did not finally adjudicate the asserted constitutional violations or the difficult state-law and choice-of-law questions. Its reasoning used those concerns to support caution in interpreting the Rule. Id. at 831, 844–848, 864–865.

## The historical rule and why necessity matters

A limited-fund class permits one proceeding to bind people who cannot all recover in full from the same inadequate resource. Separate early recoveries could consume the fund and effectively defeat later claims. The traditional examples were concrete funds such as trust assets, insurance proceeds, or sale proceeds. Because this form of class action is mandatory, absent members lack the inherent opt-out right that Rule 23(b)(3) provides. Historical safeguards therefore mattered to the justification for binding them. Id. at 833–837 & n. 13.

The Court identified three characteristics of the traditional limited-fund cases. First, definite maximum amounts for the claims and available fund demonstrated insufficiency. Second, the whole inadequate fund went toward the claims rather than being withheld for the defendant's benefit. Third, claimants sharing the recovery theory were included and treated equitably among themselves. These features were presumptively necessary, and a proponent departing from them had to justify the departure; they were more than a list of circumstances that happened to be sufficient in old cases. Id. at 838–842.

The Rule's history favored preserving those protections. The Advisory Committee's principal development for mass tort litigation was the opt-out provision, not an unrestricted expansion of mandatory limited-fund classes. The Rules Enabling Act prevents procedural rules from altering substantive rights. Mandatory settlement of damages claims also raises jury-trial and due-process concerns because it can extinguish legal claims without the absent person's consent. The Court drew on those concerns without holding that every mandatory limited-fund settlement is unconstitutional. It separately reserved the necessity and sufficiency of notice for such a class. Id. at 842–848 & nn. 19–24.

## Application: the negotiated fund was not independently proved

The lower courts accepted negotiated insurance figures as establishing the fund's limit. But an agreed payment amount establishes what parties will pay under that agreement, not necessarily the maximum resource available to claimants. The district court needed an independent evidentiary basis for the limit and insufficiency, with findings and an opportunity for opponents to challenge the evidence. A later fairness hearing could not replace those certification safeguards. The Court did not choose between competing lower-court formulations of the degree of threatened exhaustion because this certification failed under either. Id. at 848–851 & nn. 26–29.

The policies' value might conceivably be discounted for the risk of losing coverage litigation. Even assuming that approach could support a limited fund, the courts had not independently valued that asset. Settlement value could sometimes be useful evidence after genuinely disinterested bargaining, but that assumption was unavailable here. Counsel negotiating for the class also represented inventory clients whose payments depended on a successful global deal or coverage resolution. That conflict rewarded reaching an approvable settlement rather than necessarily obtaining the best settlement for the class. The backup agreement did not cure the problem: it was linked to the global negotiations, and the insurers insisted on a shared payment constraint. The Court did not find the policies necessarily sufficient to pay everyone; it held that their insufficiency had not been independently established. Id. at 851–853, 862–863.

## Application: inclusion and representation could not be repaired by fairness

A mandatory limited-fund class should include the competing unsatisfied claims against that fund. Here, approximately 45,000 inventory plaintiffs and more than 53,000 pending claimants were outside it. Counsel had helped remove a substantial group during the very negotiations designed to obtain a binding resolution. Inventory claimants also appeared to receive more favorable immediate payments and terms than class members. Even proof of comparable benefits would not itself supply the missing structural protection of independent representation. Id. at 854–855.

Within the class, present and future claimants had different interests in immediate payment versus preserving resources. They required homogeneous subclasses with separate representation. People exposed before the 1959 expiration of Continental's policy also had more valuable insured claims than those exposed afterward. Treating those groups identically was an allocation decision, not proof that their interests were identical. The shared desire to obtain insurance proceeds could not eliminate these conflicts. Rule 23(a)'s adequate-representation requirement and the equitable justification for mandatory treatment required protection before certification; the subsequent Rule 23(e) fairness assessment could not swallow those requirements. Id. at 856–859 & nn. 31–33.

Fibreboard's retention of nearly all its net worth raised another serious difficulty. A regime that denied ordinary withdrawal, capped damages, and delayed recovery looked difficult to justify as necessary while allowing the defendant to retain the purportedly available resource. The Court expressly did not decide whether that defect alone would invalidate certification, how close to insolvency a defendant must be brought, or whether savings in transaction costs could justify a credit in a different case. Those were identified questions, not independently resolved holdings. Id. at 859–861 & nn. 34–35.

## Judgment and separate writings

The Court **reversed the Fifth Circuit's judgment and remanded** for proceedings consistent with its opinion. The seven-Justice majority concluded that the fund was not independently shown to be limited and that exclusions and conflicting representation defeated the required protections. Its mandate did not award tort damages, finally decide insurance coverage, or invalidate the independent backup settlement. Id. at 864–865.

Rehnquist, joined by Scalia and Kennedy, agreed that the majority correctly stated existing law. He also acknowledged the district court's efforts and the enormous transaction costs, arguing that the asbestos crisis called for a legislative solution rather than judicial redesign of Rule 23. His concurrence occupies page 865.

Breyer, joined by Stevens, would sustain the Fifth Circuit's judgment. He emphasized the extraordinary number of claims, delays, and costs, and argued for deference to the district court's detailed findings and practical experience. He considered the disputed insurance asset a genuine limited fund: its value could disappear after the impending California decision, and an opt-out settlement would undermine insurers' willingness to resolve the dispute. He accepted the backup agreement as arm's-length valuation between Fibreboard and its insurers; regarded the representation, allocation, and absence of subclasses as defensible on this record; and found substantial satisfaction of the whole-fund requirement because settlement savings exceeded Fibreboard's retained value. This differed from the majority's insistence on independent valuation and structural protections despite urgency. Id. at 865–883.

Breyer anticipated that respondents would likely defeat the remaining objections but expressly declined to decide them definitively, including notice, standing-related matters, and the asserted statutory and constitutional barriers. Those statements are predictions in dissent, not holdings of the Court. Id. at 883–884.

## Reading the assignment accurately

The assignment substantially compresses the majority, omitting much of the negotiation history, procedural detail, and both complete separate writings. Its lettered note identifies the later renumbering of the subclass provision as Rule 23(c)(5); that editorial update is distinct from the 1999 opinion's reference to Rule 23(c)(4)(B). The shared notes discuss legislation, constructive bankruptcy, and settlement policy. They should not turn the majority's reserved questions about all-assets contribution or every possible mass tort limited fund into categorical holdings. This brief describes the decision at its historical date. Full-source reading and the independent-review record accompany the brief in the register.
