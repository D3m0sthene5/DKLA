# Waldon v. Marshall

**Y.B. Mich. 43 Edw. III, fol. 33, pl. 38 (Common Bench, conventionally dated 1370). Civil Procedure.** Assigned account: CivPro physical 305, printed 576. Available judicial source: the edited translation in Harvard Ames Foundation materials, VII–23 (physical 7), captioned *Waldon v. Marshall [Dalton v. Bereton]*. The complete original Year Book report has not been recovered. This brief explains the whole available translated passage and the assigned account; its explicit omissions prevent full-source certification.

The available translation describes a horse owner, called William Waldon in the report, suing a practitioner called J. Marshall. The writ alleged that the practitioner took the owner’s horse in hand to cure its illness, then conducted the cure so negligently that the horse died. Those are the pleaded circumstances supporting the challenge to the writ. The translated passage supplies no established fee, particular treatment, medical diagnosis, jury findings, or amount recovered. Its important procedural result is that the writ was adjudged sufficient, not that a damages judgment for the horse’s value is reported. (Harvard VII–23, opening and final Waldon paragraph.)

The first objection concerned how the alleged wrong had been expressed. Kirton argued that the writ used the language of a breach of the peace, although the declaration described negligent treatment causing death. The judges initially thought the writ defective on that basis. But when the writ itself was read, it did not contain the supposed breach-of-peace allegation. The objection therefore failed and the writ was held good. The sequence distinguishes an advocate’s characterization of the document from what the document actually said; it does not hold that inconsistent language was present but immaterial. (Harvard VII–23, first Kirton objection and following judicial paragraph.)

Kirton then argued that an undertaking to cure belonged in covenant. Belknap answered that covenant was unavailable without a deed and that the plaintiff instead sought a special writ suited to negligent performance of the cure. The problem was the fit between the factual wrong and the available form of action. A promise alone did not supply the formal instrument required for covenant, while a traditional direct-force trespass did not accurately express the way the horse allegedly died. The translated argument thus explains why the plaintiff sought case as a route to remedy for a badly performed undertaking. It does not establish that every informal promise, including a promise never acted upon, was then enforceable by this form. (Harvard VII–23, second Kirton objection and Belknap response.)

Kirton proposed a general trespass writ alleging that the defendant killed the horse. Belknap rejected that route because the horse had not been killed by force: it had died through the practitioner’s failure in the cure. The available passage then says the writ was adjudged good. This is the reported pleading determination. It should not be enlarged into findings that the practitioner actually was negligent, a guarantee of successful treatment, or a final adjudication of damages. The translation has an omission before the disposition and another immediately after it, so the full subsequent procedural record cannot be reconstructed from this source. (Harvard VII–23, final exchanges and disposition.)

The textbook uses the case as an illustration of an undertaking performed badly, in its explanation of the development of case beyond the older procedural categories. It follows with Cavendish’s specialty discussion from the distinct Surgeon’s Case. The two accounts share a historical teaching context but are separate reports and have different available outcomes: Waldon’s translated writ was sustained, whereas the complete recovered Surgeon’s Case report ends with abatement for an omitted place. The adjacent quotation should not be assigned to Waldon or used to supply an unreported damages result. (Assigned physical 305–306, printed 576.)

Identity also requires care. The Harvard caption supplies Dalton v. Bereton as an alternative and its editorial source note attributes the identification of William de Dalton of York and William de Bereton, marshal, to Professor Palmer. That note is scholarly identification, not a separately read original plea roll. It is preserved without substituting those personal names for every occurrence in the translated report or conflating another dated record with the assigned Michaelmas report. The second editorial note translates the writ’s Latin description of taking the horse in hand and negligent cure. Both notes were read; neither cures the explicit omissions in the translated report. No judicial footnotes, separate judicial opinion, or numerical vote are supplied in the recovered passage. The entire available source page and the whole assigned original scan were checked, but the complete original report and any omitted proceedings remain a source barrier.
