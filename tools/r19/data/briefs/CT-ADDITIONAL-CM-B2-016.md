# Farone v. Bag’n Baggage, Ltd.
165 S.W.3d 795 (Tex. App.—Eastland 2005) | Contracts

## Court and outcome
Justice Jim R. Wright wrote for the Eastland Court of Appeals panel with Chief Justice Arnot and Justice McCall. The court affirmed a defense summary judgment on Robert Farone’s employment-contract and stock-option compensation claims. The employment claim failed because continuation of the original two-year agreement lacked a further writing satisfying the statute of frauds; without an enforceable restriction, he remained subject to employment at will. The separate promise to compensate him for stock options lacked essential terms. There are no judicial notes or separate writings in the full report. (797, 800–802.)

## Contract, corporate conversion, and discharge
Caribbean Marine bought the closely held Bag’n Baggage corporation in 1983. On June 17, 1985, Bag’n Baggage agreed to employ Farone as president for 24 months, subject to earlier termination for death, permanent disability, or specified breaches. Material breach permitted termination on 30 days’ written notice, with accrued compensation paid. Listed grounds included willful misconduct, failure to perform responsibilities, felony conviction, resignation or retirement, and other conduct that the board determined in good faith was detrimental. The agreement contained no renewal clause. (797, 799.)

A simultaneous written option agreement allowed him to purchase ten percent of Bag’n Baggage’s shares within three years, with early expiration if employment ended for reasons other than death or disability. He exercised options for five percent. When the business was converted into a partnership, he accepted Caribbean Marine stock of equal value in exchange for those shares and bought a one-percent limited partnership interest for $10,000. He alleged that he told Caribbean Marine president Bill Bowen that conversion could dilute his existing equity and destroy his remaining options. Bowen allegedly responded that he would be compensated for the remaining options when Bag’n Baggage was sold or Farone left. That alleged undertaking was oral. (797–798, 801.)

Bowen died in 1996, and Patrick Sullivan succeeded him. Most of the board became dissatisfied with Farone’s performance, voted to discharge him in December 2000, and informed him on January 11, 2001. He had continued working long after the written employment term expired but had no successive written employment agreement. His suit sought damages for breach of the employment arrangement and for the alleged option-compensation promise; other claims were settled and were not part of this appeal. (798, 800.)

## Summary-judgment posture
The defendants obtained partial summary judgment, which became final after the parties resolved the other issues. Although their motion invoked both traditional and no-evidence rules, it primarily used traditional summary-judgment terminology; the appellate court applied Rule 166a(c), not the no-evidence rule. The movants therefore had to demonstrate no genuine material factual dispute and entitlement to judgment as a matter of law. Evidence favorable to Farone was taken as true, with reasonable inferences and doubts resolved in his favor. A defendant could prevail by disproving an essential element or establishing an affirmative defense. Because the trial court did not specify its grounds, any meritorious theory advanced could sustain the judgment. This was not a jury finding that Bowen never spoke the alleged words. (797–798, 802.)

## Renewal and the one-year provision
Texas Business and Commerce Code § 26.01 requires a signed writing for an agreement not to be performed within one year. The original employment term was two years. Its provisions for death, disability, and performance failures did not make full performance possible within one year: the court distinguished those methods of termination or excusable nonperformance from completion of the promised term. It thus held the original agreement subject to the statute. (799–800.)

For purposes of the appeal, the court assumed without deciding that the initial contract unequivocally limited discharge to the specified conditions. That assumption did not extend the restriction indefinitely. An original agreement that could not be performed within a year could not be enforced through successive implied renewals without further writings. The court acknowledged that implied agreements to continue may have existed but held them unenforceable under the statute. The original writing did not itself authorize renewal or memorialize those later arrangements. (800–801.)

The employee’s authorities did not resolve that problem. They involved an original one-year term, monthly extensions of noncompetition arrangements, oral promises too indefinite to alter at-will employment, or employment lasting only while performance remained satisfactory. Farone’s contract prescribed a two-year term with designated causes for early termination, rather than employment merely for as long as the employer remained satisfied. Those distinctions prevented treating the contract as outside the statute. Without an enforceable continuing limitation, the at-will presumption allowed discharge for good cause, bad cause, or no cause. The court rejected his first two issues and did not need to reach the third. (799–801.)

## Indefinite option-compensation promise
The option claim failed for a different reason. Accepting the alleged assurance that Farone would be compensated, the summary-judgment evidence supplied no agreement on the price, whether compensation would be cash or equity, how equity would be valued, or whether any equity would come from Caribbean Marine or the new partnership. These were essential matters of obligation, not merely omitted administrative details. Courts favor validating agreements but cannot create a contract the parties did not make. A promise using the word “compensated” did not alone establish definite and ascertainable terms. (801–802.)

The court rejected the fourth and fifth issues on that basis and did not reach the sixth. It also did not decide the seventh issue’s objection to the specificity of a no-evidence motion, because its decision rested on the traditional summary-judgment standard. The final judgment was affirmed; neither a renewed employment restriction nor option damages were awarded. (802.)

## Assigned comparison and historical scope
The casebook presents Farone alongside Rash’s later interpretation of it. The full Farone opinion uses broad language about renewal of an original agreement within the statute. The later federal panel in Rash construed that language in light of renewal of a term exceeding one year and distinguished a month-to-month or indefinite continuation. That later characterization is preserved in the separate Rash brief rather than retroactively inserted as Farone’s own express qualification. The complete Farone report and judicial text, including its quoted contract and final issue-specific disposition, and the whole assigned comparison were read.
