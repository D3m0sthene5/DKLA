# Marbury v. Madison
5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137 (1803) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Decision and setting

On February 24, 1803, the Supreme Court discharged the rule requiring Secretary of State James Madison to show cause why a writ of mandamus should not issue. Chief Justice Marshall concluded that William Marbury had a completed appointment and a legal right to his commission, and that withholding it violated that right. He nevertheless concluded that the Supreme Court could not entertain this original mandamus proceeding: the statutory authority as the Court understood it exceeded Article III’s original-jurisdiction allocation. The Court issued no writ and did not order Madison to deliver the commission. This was an original application, not an appeal in which a lower judgment was affirmed or reversed. The report contains Marshall’s opinion for the Court and no separate judicial writing or numerical vote tally. (153–154, 162–173, 173–180.)

The assigned executive-authority account emphasizes the difference between political discretion and ministerial duties affecting individual rights. The complete opinion also supplies the appointment analysis, the statutory and constitutional jurisdiction dispute, and the explanation of judicial review that the short account compresses. Its ministerial-duty conclusions must be read with its ultimate absence of original jurisdiction; the assigned editors expressly characterize the earlier discussion as technically dicta. (Assigned October PDF 20/printed 264.)

## Appointment and factual record

The December 1801 applications were brought by Marbury, Dennis Ramsay, Robert Townsend Hooe, and William Harper, represented by Charles Lee, formerly Attorney General. Their supporting affidavits stated that President John Adams nominated them to be District of Columbia justices of the peace, the Senate consented, Adams signed their commissions, and the Secretary of State affixed the United States seal. They sought their commissions from Madison after the change of administration, but the commissions were withheld. Requests for satisfactory information from the State Department and for Senate documentation also failed. The later opinion addresses Marbury’s individual right and application. (137–139, 153–154.)

The evidentiary proceedings mattered because the applicants could not obtain voluntary official affidavits. State Department clerks Jacob Wagner and Daniel Brent objected to being sworn on the ground that they were not obliged to disclose office transactions. Lee distinguished the Secretary’s confidential work as the President’s agent from public, legally prescribed duties concerning records and commissions. The Court ordered the witnesses sworn and their answers recorded, while allowing objections to particular questions. It did not recognize a blanket privilege shielding everything a department clerk knew. Wagner could not remember seeing these particular commissions and distinguished personal knowledge from information received from others. Brent supplied more direct evidence that commissions had been signed and sealed, including Marbury’s and Hooe’s. The report records uncertainties about the other applicants rather than making every applicant’s evidence identical. (139–144.)

Former acting Secretary of State Levi Lincoln also sought time to consider what he could disclose. The Court allowed him to decline questions implicating confidential communications or self-incrimination, but treated the existence of commissions in the office as a public matter. He could not say that the commissions reached Madison, and the Court treated their ultimate disposition as immaterial to the present application if they had never come into Madison’s possession. James Marshall’s affidavit described taking commissions for Alexandria officers on March 4, 1801, amid concern about disorder, and returning Hooe’s and Harper’s because he could not carry all the papers. A later affidavit by Hazen Kimball stated that he had seen Marbury’s and Hooe’s commissions signed on March 3. These details establish an evidentiary history, not a finding that every commission was personally delivered to Madison or that its later whereabouts were proved. (144–148, 153.)

## Application, arguments, and governing provisions

The Court had issued and served a rule to show cause. Madison showed no cause; the opinion does not describe a competing merits brief from him. Lee argued that a justice of the peace held an office different from one held at the President’s pleasure, that withholding the commission was an actionable ministerial failure, and that no adequate ordinary remedy existed. He relied on the distinction between the Secretary’s executive agency duties and statutory public duties concerning records, seals, and commissions. He also invoked prior mandamus proceedings and contended that the Supreme Court’s supervisory authority and the Judiciary Act supported relief. These are the applicant’s arguments, rather than admissions by the absent Secretary. (138–153.)

Section 13 of the Judiciary Act of 1789 authorized the Supreme Court to issue writs of mandamus, in cases warranted by legal principles and usages, to federal courts or persons holding office under United States authority. Madison fell within the latter description. Article III separately specifies original jurisdiction in cases affecting ambassadors, other public ministers and consuls, and cases in which a state is a party; the other enumerated cases fall within appellate jurisdiction subject to congressional exceptions and regulations. The Court had to decide whether Congress could make this direct demand against a federal officer an additional original proceeding. (173–176.)

Marshall organized the decision around three questions: whether Marbury had a right to the commission; whether the law afforded a remedy for its violation; and whether mandamus from this Court was the appropriate available remedy. Keeping those questions separate explains why conclusions favorable to Marbury did not produce a writ. (154.)

## Completion of the appointment

The Court distinguished nomination, appointment, and the commission evidencing an appointment. Nomination initiates the process; Senate consent enables the President to make the appointment; signing the commission is the last act required of the President. Affixing the seal completes the commission and conclusively evidences that the signature and appointment occurred. A commission was not invariably indispensable to an appointment, but a signed and sealed commission was decisive evidence in this record. (155–160.)

Delivery did not remain a discretionary executive step capable of undoing the completed appointment. Transmission was a matter of convenience, and a certified copy could serve the relevant evidentiary function. Acceptance likewise did not have to precede the existence of the appointment. The Court concluded that Marbury had been appointed to an office held for five years, which under its analysis was not removable at the executive’s will. His resulting interest was vested; withholding the commission violated an acquired legal right. This is the Court’s treatment of the particular statutory office in 1803, not a present-day rule that every office with a fixed term is protected against presidential removal. The September assignment itself cautions against that broader inference. (160–162; assigned September PDF 109/printed 179.)

## Legal injury, political discretion, and mandamus

The Court reasoned that civil liberty includes the ability to obtain protection of the laws when an individual is injured. Government could not function as a government of laws if the legal system supplied no remedy for this invasion of a vested right. But that premise did not make every executive act judicially controllable. When the Constitution entrusts political duties to the President and executive officers act as his agents in performing them, their conduct may involve discretion and responsibility to the country rather than a judicially enforceable individual entitlement. Courts cannot direct that political discretion. (162–166.)

A different analysis applies when law imposes a specific duty and individual rights depend upon its performance. The Secretary’s official rank does not insulate that duty from examination. The character of the act, rather than merely the officer’s title, determines whether judicial relief is appropriate. Marshall used such examples as a refusal to supply a legally available copy of an official record. Once the appointment was complete, providing Marbury’s commission or a copy was of the ministerial character the Court described. The earlier refusal to provide satisfactory information did not convert a public record duty into an unreviewable political decision. (166–170.)

The mandamus inquiry required both an appropriate object for the writ and the absence of another specific legal remedy. The Court identified the officer’s legally enforceable duty and Marbury’s right to the evidence of his appointment. It expressly acknowledged that no act of Congress specifically commanded delivery of the commission: the duty arose from the completed appointment, the vested right, and the character of the commission as evidence of that right. A brief should therefore not replace this analysis with an invented express statutory delivery command. An action of detinue seeking the paper or its monetary value was inadequate because the value of a public office was not ascertainable as an ordinary commodity, and Marbury sought the office and commission rather than equivalent damages. (170–173.)

## Jurisdiction and judicial review

These favorable conclusions could not supply jurisdiction. The Court read Article III’s division of original and appellate jurisdiction as an operative limitation, not merely a minimum that Congress could enlarge. Making that division optional would deprive the constitutional enumeration of effect. Madison was not an ambassador or other person whose status brought this proceeding within the Court’s specified original jurisdiction. Nor was the writ appellate merely because it would command a federal officer. Appellate jurisdiction revises an existing cause; Marbury’s direct demand originated a new proceeding, and the writ was not needed to aid the Court’s appellate jurisdiction. Section 13, insofar as it authorized this exercise of original jurisdiction, was therefore incompatible with the Constitution. The opinion does not eliminate every mandamus power, including writs properly associated with appellate jurisdiction. (173–176.)

The final reasoning asks why a court must give effect to the Constitution when an ordinary statute conflicts with it. The Constitution establishes limited governmental powers and is superior to ordinary legislative acts. If legislation could alter those limits through the ordinary process, the distinction between a limited and an unlimited constitution would disappear. Courts deciding a case must identify the governing law; when two rules conflict, they cannot apply both. Applying the statute while disregarding the Constitution would make the superior law subordinate to the act it limits. (176–178.)

Marshall reinforced that conclusion through Article III’s extension to cases arising under the Constitution, concrete constitutional restrictions that courts necessarily encounter, the judicial oath, and the Supremacy Clause’s treatment of federal statutes made in pursuance of the Constitution. His examples included export duties, bills of attainder and ex post facto laws, and the Constitution’s proof requirements for treason. The interpretive-duty proposition is part of this argument about a court resolving conflicting governing law in a case. The Parrillo-Shane assignment uses it to introduce later questions about agency interpretation and judicial scrutiny; this decision itself does not settle the modern administrative-deference framework. (178–180; assigned Parrillo-Shane PDF 47–48/printed 45–46.)

## Judgment and limits

The final operative order was that the rule must be discharged. Marbury received no mandamus from the Supreme Court despite the Court’s conclusions about appointment and injury. There is no separate opinion in the reproduced report. Because the report does not provide an attendance list or numerical vote tally, this brief does not infer one from its single opinion. (180.)
