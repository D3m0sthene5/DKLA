# J. C. Patel v. Telerent Leasing Corporation
574 So. 2d 3 (Miss. 1990) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
Presiding Justice Hawkins wrote the Mississippi Supreme Court’s December 19, 1990 opinion. Chief Justice Roy Noble Lee, Presiding Justice Dan M. Lee, and Justices Prather, Robertson, Sullivan, Anderson, Pittman, and Blass concurred, making the reported disposition unanimous. The court reversed the circuit court’s $24,445.44 judgment for Telerent and reinstated the county court judgment denying Telerent relief. It held that substantial evidence supported the county judge’s result and that the lessor’s material failure to install the equipment justified the motel owner’s withholding payment. (3, 6–8.)

## Lease-purchase agreement and incomplete installation
On April 25, 1985, Telerent agreed to provide thirty General Electric television sets and accessories for Jagdish C. Patel’s fifty-room Sonya Motor Inn in Jackson. The lease-purchase contract called for eighty-four monthly installments of $281.16, beginning by the first day of the month after completion of the lessor’s equipment work, and a nominal purchase option after those installments. Telerent had to change existing taps in all rooms and supply an in-line amplifier. It also undertook to replace or repair stolen or damaged sets upon prompt reports to police and Telerent. A typed clause made Patel responsible for servicing the equipment and keeping it working during the lease. An August addendum required theft alarms for all fifty rooms and increased rent $27.39 a month; a September addendum required insurance for the receivers. (4.)

Patel signed a completion certificate in June acknowledging delivery and satisfactory installation, and another on October 15 covering thirty pedestals, fifty taps, and fifty theft alarms. But he repeatedly complained that the distribution system remained incomplete and the televisions could not be used before October 15. Telerent’s November 11 letter acknowledged that he had refused its July bill because installation was incomplete and that the company had returned to extend the distribution system. It agreed to make the full-term lease effective November 1, with total monthly charges including alarm rental and taxes of $327.06. Nevertheless, it claimed four months’ earlier television use and demanded $1,345.78, including the new monthly payment, or threatened repossession. (4–5.)

Patel denied owing for the earlier period. On November 17 he tendered $272.55 for November, subtracting $54.51 for five reported stolen sets that had not been replaced. Telerent returned the check and demanded its larger sum within five days, threatening acceleration and removal. On December 4 Patel again sent the same check, reiterated his complaints, and added that the theft alarm malfunctioned and other equipment had not been properly mounted. These were substantive complaints and demands for performance, rather than an unexplained refusal to pay. (4–5.)

## Repossession and two different appellate assessments
Telerent obtained a county court replevin judgment giving it possession of twenty-two sets with radios, twenty-two locking pedestals, and the theft alarm system. At the subsequent public sale it was the only bidder, purchasing the sets for $110 each and the alarm system for $500, a total of $2,920. It then sued in county court for $24,445.44: the claimed remaining lease obligation and repossession and sale expenses, including attorneys’ fees, less the sale proceeds. The repossession judgment itself was not offered as a res judicata or collateral-estoppel bar to Patel’s assertion that Telerent had breached. (5–6.)

Only Telerent’s collection manager and Patel testified at the nonjury trial. The county judge found that Telerent had not performed the expressly typed requirement concerning room taps and the amplifier, that Patel had timely rejected and abandoned the contract, and that the lessor had failed a notice requirement. The December 22, 1986 judgment denied Telerent relief. On appeal, the circuit court applied North Carolina law, treated a completion certificate and engraving of the motel’s name on the sets as acceptance, held that Patel had not properly rejected, and rendered judgment for the lessor. Patel appealed that reversal to the Supreme Court. (5–6.)

## Governing Article 2 and deference to the actual factfinder
The Supreme Court treated the lease-purchase as the functional equivalent of a sale, supported by the nominal purchase price at the term’s end, and therefore applied Article 2. It observed that the parties could choose governing state law and that the relevant Mississippi and North Carolina UCC provisions were identical. It did not need to manufacture a conflicting rule between the two states. (6.)

The county court—not the circuit court sitting on appeal—was the factfinder. Its nonjury judgment was binding if supported by substantial evidence and not manifestly wrong, with necessary findings assumed in favor of its result. It could be sustained on a correct ground even if the judge had supplied a different legal reason. Patel’s letters and Telerent’s own November concession amply supported a finding that the system remained incomplete and unsatisfactory before October 15. The appellate courts could not simply disregard that evidence in favor of a certificate or the act of marking the receivers. (6.)

## Withholding for material breach despite acceptance
The court characterized inadequate installation as a material breach justifying withholding. Under § 2-717, a buyer who notified the seller could deduct damages resulting from a breach from a price due under the same contract. The official comments made two qualifications explicit: the breach and price had to arise under the same contract, and notice had to reasonably communicate the reason for withholding, though no special formality was required. Those provisions supplied the central legal explanation supporting the county court’s result. (6–7.)

The opinion also referred to § 2-609, allowing a written demand for adequate assurance when reasonable grounds for insecurity arose and commercially reasonable suspension of performance for which the agreed return had not yet been received. It regarded Patel’s requests for fulfillment of the remaining conditions as requests for further assurance for which he should not be penalized. It connected these Code remedies with pre-Code material-breach principles and Restatement § 237. It did not impose a general license to withhold arbitrary sums under unrelated agreements. (7.)

The circuit court’s acceptance analysis was not enough to negate the defense. The court relied on Missouri Bag Co. to show that a buyer could accept and use defective goods yet claim an offset against their price; even knowing acceptance did not necessarily waive merchantability obligations. It also cited Shreve Land Co.’s use of an analogous Code setoff provision and described that principle as general law. Thus, the Supreme Court sustained the favorable trial result through material breach, withholding, and recoupment principles rather than needing to adopt every aspect of the county judge’s characterization as rejection and abandonment. (7–8.)

## Disposition and assigned significance
The circuit judgment was reversed and the county judgment reinstated without a new-trial remand. Patel did not receive an affirmative damages award or a judicially specified refund. Telerent’s deficiency claim was denied. The assigned note on printed page 865 contrasts this right to withhold with the election-of-remedies problem in ARP Films; the contrast does not erase Patel’s particular sale-equivalent contract, same-contract breach, notice, and supporting trial findings. (8.)
