# The Evergreens v. Nunan

**141 F.2d 927 (2d Cir. 1944) · April 3, 1944**  
**Course:** Civil Procedure  
**Assigned account:** CivPro, physical PDF pages 659–660, printed pages 1307–1308, “The Evergreens Doctrine”; accompanying two editorial notes continue through printed 1310 on physical page 660.

Judge Learned Hand wrote for a panel with Judges Swan and Augustus N. Hand. The court affirmed a Tax Court order assessing income-tax deficiencies. Its preclusion holding concerned the purpose for which an earlier factual determination could be used in a later suit: the earlier decision could not conclusively supply an evidentiary premise from which a different ultimate fact would be inferred. The report contains no separate opinion or numerical vote notation. (927–931.)

## Facts and procedure

The Evergreens was a Brooklyn cemetery company that sold burial lots. For tax purposes, its gains on property dispositions depended on the amount realized minus the property's basis. The relevant basis was its value on March 1, 1913. Its property included fully improved and partially improved lots. During the tax years 1934 and 1935, it sold fully improved lots, and New York City took a large tract of partially improved lots for municipal purposes. This appeal concerned the partially improved property. (927–928.)

An earlier Board of Tax Appeals proceeding had assessed the company's income taxes for 1929 through 1933. The Board had determined a value for the fully improved lots and had also found that the cost of improving lots ranged from eight to twenty cents per square foot. In the later proceeding, the Tax Court treated the earlier fully improved valuation as preclusive for that class of property, fixing its basis at $1.55 per square foot. The Commissioner did not appeal that treatment. For the partially improved property, however, the Tax Court took evidence anew and fixed a basis of thirty-five cents per square foot. It refused to derive that value by subtracting the earlier improvement-cost finding from the earlier fully improved value. (927–928.)

The company sought review of the resulting deficiency order. It argued that the prior determination conclusively established not only the fully improved lots' value but also an indisputable starting point for the proposed subtraction. It additionally contended that the earlier tribunal must have determined the partially improved lots' value as a necessary step in reaching the fully improved valuation. The Tax Court's dissenting minority had agreed with that latter contention, but Hand found no support for it in the earlier record. (928.)

## Preliminary grounds limiting preclusion

The earlier tribunal's consideration of delays in disposing of all the company's lots did not establish that it first valued the partially improved property and then added improvement costs. Those were different analytical operations. Moreover, the earlier cost-of-improvements finding was not necessary to the determination of the fully improved lots' value. Accordingly, that finding did not itself create an estoppel. Expert testimony in the later proceeding also answered the company's challenge to the independent evidentiary support for the thirty-five-cent valuation. (928.)

These points matter because the court did not simply treat every finding in the earlier tax case as binding and then refuse to perform arithmetic. It first separated findings necessary to the first result from other findings and rejected the asserted earlier determination of the very property value now in dispute.

## The distinction between ultimate facts and mediate data

Hand described an ultimate fact as a fact that, together with other facts, triggers a legal right, duty, or sanction. A mediate datum is a fact from which the existence of such an ultimate fact can be inferred. The distinction describes a fact's role in the reasoning of a particular suit. The same proposition may occupy a different role when the later suit asks a different legal question. (928–929.)

The court separated two inquiries. One asks whether a finding that was only a mediate datum in the first suit can ever receive preclusive force. Authorities differed: some restricted preclusion to ultimate facts in the first action, while others treated necessary evidentiary findings as conclusive too. Hand expressly declined to choose between those approaches. The earlier Board proceeding had determined no mediate datum that was also necessary to its result and material to resolving that doctrinal conflict here. (928–929.)

The decisive inquiry instead concerned use in the **second** action. Could a fact determined earlier—whether ultimate or mediate there—become an indisputable evidentiary premise from which a different ultimate fact would now be deduced? The company wanted precisely that use: the fully improved lots' established value would become a premise for determining the partially improved lots' value. The court rejected it. Its concluding rule assumed without deciding that mediate findings in the first suit could establish ultimate facts in the second, but held that no earlier finding could conclusively establish a mediate datum in the second suit. (929–931.)

## Why the court limited that use

The requirement that a finding be necessary to the first judgment supplies a reason to expect genuine contest and effort: the outcome depends on resolving the issue. For that purpose, Hand thought the fact's place in a logical hierarchy was less significant than its importance to the original result. (929.)

But preclusion in later litigation raises a different concern. The original stakes may be modest, and the parties may have had little reason to anticipate the finding's future importance. Even making a finding conclusive as to a later ultimate fact can work harshly. Permitting it to serve as conclusive evidence for every rational inference in every future controversy would multiply those risks much further. Logical relevance could connect the original finding with consequences that neither party could calculate. Hand therefore refused to impose that unlimited evidentiary exposure without authority requiring it. (929.)

He considered making foreseeability of future controversies a general limit on preclusion, but expressly identified that as a possible reform, **not the law as it then stood**. The actual holding was the narrower restriction on using prior findings as conclusive mediate data in the second suit. (929–931.)

## Authorities, judicial note, and judgment

Hand found little authority squarely addressing that precise problem. He explained that a New Hampshire tax decision supporting the company's position had later been overruled. A British compensation decision concerning a medical certificate was distinguishable because the certificate was a statutorily conclusive step within the same proceeding, rather than a finding imported from an independent suit. He also discussed the earlier Restatement's evidentiary-facts treatment while recognizing that it might not decide the exact ultimate-to-mediate question before the panel. (929–930.)

The sole judicial footnote reproduces the Restatement's comment and illustration. In the illustration, a contract case's verdict based on conflicting evidence about a person's location does not prevent proof of that location in a later assault case. Hand observed that the location was a mediate datum in both proceedings and left open whether its status as an ultimate fact in the first would affect that illustration. This discussion supports the opinion's careful separation of questions rather than a claim that the Restatement had already resolved every aspect of its holding. (930 & n.1.)

The court **affirmed the Tax Court's order**. It did not direct a new valuation proceeding or hold that the company's subtraction method must be accepted. (931.)

## Assigned significance and source scope

The textbook's pyramid analogy makes the doctrine easier to visualize: evidentiary facts support intermediate inferences, which support the ultimate facts associated with liability or another legal consequence. Its account correctly focuses on the role of the fact in the second suit. Its simplified “unimproved” label corresponds to the actual opinion's “partially improved” property. The complete opinion additionally establishes that the earlier improvement-cost finding was unnecessary and that no prior partially improved valuation had been shown. Those qualifications belong alongside the book's arithmetic example.

Both accompanying textbook notes were read. They present later criticism of the ultimate/mediate terminology, a *Synanon Church* quotation favoring necessary issues important to the first litigation, and a treatise's possible full-and-fair-opportunity approach. These are assigned later commentary, not holdings or subsequent-law certifications supplied by Hand's 1944 opinion.
