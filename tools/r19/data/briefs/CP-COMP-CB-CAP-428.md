# J.E.B. v. Alabama ex rel. T.B.

**511 U.S. 127 (1994), decided April 19, 1994.** Civil Procedure. Assigned account: *CivPro.pdf*, physical pages 560–561, printed pages 1104–1106, including both attached editorial notes on printed page 1106. Pinpoints below refer to the complete United States Reports opinion.

Alabama brought a civil paternity and child-support action against J.E.B. on behalf of T.B., the child's mother. The constitutional dispute concerned how the State selected the jury, rather than whether the evidence established paternity. The Court held that intentional gender discrimination in peremptory challenges violates equal protection: gender cannot serve as a proxy for an individual juror's competence or impartiality. It reversed the Alabama Court of Civil Appeals and remanded to that court. It did not itself decide that J.E.B. was not the father or enter judgment extinguishing his support obligation. **129–130, 146.**

## The jury selection and procedural path

At the October 21, 1991 trial, the venire contained thirty-six people: twelve men and twenty-four women. Three cause challenges left thirty-three prospective jurors, including ten men. Alabama used nine of its ten peremptory challenges against men. J.E.B. used all but one of his challenges against women; his remaining challenge removed the last man. The resulting jury consisted entirely of women. The latter detail, discussed explicitly by Scalia, matters because the final jury composition did not result from the State's strikes alone. The constitutional objection nevertheless concerned the State's intentional use of gender in making its own challenges. **129–130, 157.**

Before the jury was empaneled, J.E.B. argued that the prohibition against race-based strikes established in *Batson* should also prohibit gender-based strikes. The trial court rejected that objection. The jury found him to be the father, and the court ordered support payments. His postjudgment motion was denied. The Alabama Court of Civil Appeals affirmed, and the Alabama Supreme Court denied certiorari. The United States Supreme Court took the case against a background of disagreement among courts over extending *Batson* to gender. **130–131.**

Alabama defended its challenges as a reasonable effort to obtain an impartial jury in a paternity case. It suggested that men might be more sympathetic to a man accused of fathering a child and women more sympathetic to the mother. It also argued that race and gender discrimination have different histories and should receive different treatment. The Court accepted that the histories were not identical, but concluded that both men and women have suffered the kind of stereotyped exclusion that equal protection forbids. **131–139.**

## The rule and its justification

Blackmun's opinion traced the history of excluding women from juries and the constitutional repudiation of governmental assumptions about their proper roles. The question was not simply whether women now appeared in substantial numbers on jury lists. Peremptory challenges could perpetuate the same group judgments within the selection process. Men also possess the protection against discrimination; Alabama could not justify its strikes merely by identifying a historically disadvantaged group different from the group it excluded here. **131–137, 141.**

Gender classifications require heightened scrutiny and an exceedingly persuasive justification. The Court did not decide that gender was inherently suspect in the same sense as race. Its reasoning did not depend on erasing that doctrinal distinction. Alabama's asserted interest in an impartial jury was legitimate, but selecting jurors through unsupported generalizations about male and female attitudes did not establish the necessary connection between means and end. The State had no legitimate interest in securing a jury predisposed to favor its side of the paternity dispute. **136–139; 137 nn.6, 8.**

The opinion also rejected the premise that a possible statistical relationship between gender and jury behavior would automatically justify discriminatory strikes. Using a person's gender as a substitute for evaluating that individual reinforces the very stereotypes the constitutional rule is designed to displace. A juror is not required to demonstrate that every member of the excluded group would have voted identically, nor is a litigant entitled to a jury selected to reflect expected group loyalties. **138–140; 139 n.11.**

The harms extend beyond the litigants. A person excluded because of gender loses the opportunity to participate in public adjudication on equal terms. Such exclusion diminishes the courts' legitimacy and brands the individual as unsuitable on a ground unrelated to individual qualification. The right is personal to the juror and does not disappear because men or women remain elsewhere on the eventual jury. Treating final numerical representation as a complete answer would overlook the discriminatory treatment of each person struck. **140–143; 142 n.13.**

## What remains permissible

The decision did not abolish peremptory challenges. Litigants may still remove individuals whom they consider unfavorable, provided gender is not used as the proxy for bias. A gender-neutral explanation need not establish a challenge for cause. But it must concern an actual individual ground rather than conceal intentional discrimination. A party alleging discriminatory challenges must first establish a prima facie case before the striking party must supply explanations. **143–145.**

The Court specifically recognized that an individual characteristic may be disproportionately associated with one gender without becoming an automatically forbidden ground for a strike. Employment in nursing or the military could be relevant if used without pretext; the prohibition does not mechanically invalidate every characteristic with an unequal gender distribution. Conversely, allowing express gender discrimination could facilitate pretexts for racial exclusion, particularly where race and gender intersect. Alabama's struck-jury procedure did not exempt the State from the constitutional restriction. **143 n.16, 144 n.17, 145 n.18.**

These qualifications preserve a distinction between an individual assessment and a categorical assumption. They do not create permission to offer a nominally neutral explanation that merely repackages a gender stereotype. Nor do the Court's reasons resolve every possible constitutional restriction on challenges based on characteristics other than race and gender.

## The separate writings and votes

Blackmun's Court opinion was joined by Stevens, O'Connor, Souter, and Ginsburg: five Justices. Kennedy concurred in the judgment, producing a **six-to-three judgment**. O'Connor also wrote separately. Rehnquist dissented individually, and Scalia wrote a dissent joined by Rehnquist and Thomas. **127, 146–163.**

O'Connor joined the Court's constitutional holding but warned that its practical costs would be substantial. Peremptory decisions often depend on intuition that is difficult to articulate; forcing explanations and collateral hearings could impair that traditional function. She distinguished the legal prohibition against using gender from a factual assertion that gender can never correlate with attitudes. She also maintained her disagreement with extending constitutional state-action treatment to private civil litigants and criminal defendants. The State itself brought this paternity action, so that objection did not prevent her joining here. Her hoped-for limitation to governmental challenges was a separate position, not an exception adopted by the Court. **146–151.**

Kennedy emphasized equal treatment of each person and the affront to individual dignity when government assigns a presumed viewpoint on the basis of gender. A representative pool supplies an opportunity for impartial decisionmaking; it does not authorize selection designed to obtain stereotyped viewpoints. Neither side has a right to jurors of a particular gender. His opinion concurred in the judgment without joining Blackmun's opinion. **151–154.**

Rehnquist accepted *Batson* while distinguishing gender classifications from racial classifications. He stressed different levels of scrutiny, different histories, and the reciprocal availability of peremptory challenges. He thought the traditional contribution of those challenges to fairness outweighed the Court's extension of the restriction. His separate starred note is part of that dissent. **154–156.**

Scalia argued that the Court overstated the case for treating gender as an impermissible selection criterion and undervalued peremptories. He stressed J.E.B.'s own use of strikes against women, disputed the asserted individual injury in a system of reciprocal strikes, and raised third-party-standing and harmlessness concerns. His reference to overwhelming proof of paternity was a dissenting argument, not a collective holding disposing of the constitutional error. He also objected to the increased litigation over reasons for strikes and defended the traditional appearance-of-fairness function of peremptories. **156–163; 157 n.1, 158–159 n.2, 161 n.3.**

## Assignment and source distinctions

The assigned narrative says Blackmun wrote “for himself and three others.” The original printed assignment confirms that wording, but the complete official report identifies **four joining Justices in addition to Blackmun**. O'Connor both joined the Court and wrote separately. The report therefore controls the five-member Court-opinion description and six-to-three judgment recorded here.

Both attached notes are editorial. The first points to scholarship about racial access to civil juries. The second asks about religion, disability, and gender or sexual status and cites commentary. Those questions are not additional holdings of *J.E.B.*, and the cited articles were not substituted for judicial authority. The following *Snyder v.
