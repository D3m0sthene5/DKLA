# Lansford-Coaldale Joint Water Authority v. Tonolli Corp.

**CP-119 · Civil Procedure · 4 F.3d 1209 (3d Cir. 1993) · September 17, 1993 · Becker, joined by Alito and Roth**

A trial judge’s prompt oral findings, including findings adopted verbatim from a party, remain the court’s findings and receive ordinary clear-error review. That principle does not excuse gaps in findings necessary to decide a separate claim. The Third Circuit upheld the finding that a lead-smelting site did not threaten the Authority’s water supply, but remanded distinct monitoring-cost claims because important corporate-control questions had not been addressed.

## Facts, claims, and proceedings

The Authority supplied public water in Carbon County, Pennsylvania. Tonolli Pennsylvania operated a lead-smelting facility approximately 3,100 feet from its production wells. Tonolli Canada had created and owned Tonolli Pennsylvania, but sold its shares in 1976 to IFIM, which also became Canada’s parent. Canada and Pennsylvania thereafter were sister corporations. After learning of hazardous-substance releases and a waste-disposal permit application, the Authority commissioned the AGES study in 1987. Its wells were normally upgradient from the industrial site; the study tested whether prolonged pumping could reverse groundwater movement and draw contamination toward them. 4 F.3d at 1212–1213, 1216–1218.

The Authority sued the three companies, ultimately including CERCLA claims for the prospective cost of replacing or treating its water supply and for monitoring and evaluation expenses. Pennsylvania became bankrupt and was dropped. Canada defended; IFIM never answered or appeared, and the Authority did not move for default judgment. The court bifurcated liability and damages. Within hours after the nine-day liability trial ended, it announced approximately 150 oral findings, most drawn verbatim from Canada’s proposed findings submitted before trial. It entered judgment for Canada and, without explanation, IFIM. The appeal concerned CERCLA, not the unappealed common-law negligence counts. Id. at 1213–1214 & n. 2.

## Rule 52 and standard of review

The Authority argued that the findings deserved less deference because the court spoke before obtaining a transcript, relied heavily on a party’s work, and requested proposed findings before rather than after trial. The court rejected those categorical objections. Legal components of mixed questions received plenary review; factual components received clear-error review. Rule 52(a) expressly permitted findings and conclusions stated orally and recorded in open court after the evidence closed. Prompt findings also promoted speedy adjudication and used the judge’s fresh recollection, although a carefully prepared written opinion might be preferable in many cases. Id. at 1214–1216 & nn. 3–4.

Adopting proposed findings did not change the standard. Under *Anderson v. Bessemer City*, the adopted findings were still those of the judge. The record showed informed, considered views, not unfamiliarity with the evidence or dependence on proposed findings as a substitute for judging. The court reserved that it might view such a different record differently. Note 5 expressly rejected reliance on *Roberts v. Ross*, explaining that *Anderson* and the circuit’s *Hayes* decision had superseded its less-deferential approach. Id. at 1215–1216 & n. 5.

**Assigned-source discrepancy:** The assigned PDF, at printed pages 1148–1149, places a lengthy passage criticizing postdecision findings directly after Tonolli’s groundwater holding and before Notes. That block is absent from the complete reported Tonolli opinion and duplicates the Roberts discussion identified in the assigned Notes. It must not be attributed to Becker as a contrary Tonolli holding. The actual opinion and retained note 5 support the ordinary clear-error standard just described.

## Applying deference to the groundwater dispute

The Authority’s expert read decreases in monitoring-well water levels and increased contaminants after a 72-hour pumping test as evidence of reversed flow. Canada’s hydrogeologist, Earl, offered a competing interpretation. The study lacked baseline trends necessary to isolate pumping from seasonal changes, tides, weather, other pumping, and other causes. He also identified a groundwater divide and a deep discharge-zone aquifer separating the sites, and possible alternative sources of contaminants. The district court credited that explanation and Earl’s greater hydrogeological experience. Id. at 1216–1218 & nn. 6–8.

The appellate court did not decide which expert it preferred afresh. Where two views of the evidence were permissible, the trial court’s choice was not clearly erroneous. Canada did not bear the burden of proving the exact alternative cause of every observed change; the Authority bore the burden of proving the threatened contamination. The finding of hydrogeological isolation therefore supported denial of replacement-water and treatment costs. Id.

## Separate monitoring-cost and corporate-liability rulings

Monitoring expenses could be recoverable even without actual contamination of the Authority’s property. The claimant still needed a covered defendant, a release or threatened release, resulting response costs, and costs necessary and consistent with the National Contingency Plan. Thus the failure to prove a continuing contamination threat did not itself dispose of the expenses reasonably incurred to investigate one. Id. at 1218–1220.

For corporate operator liability, this 1993 court adopted actual substantial participation and control rather than mere authority to control an affiliate. It distinguished investment oversight from active involvement in management; control, not ownership alone, could make a sister corporation an operator. Environmental decisionmaking was relevant, but pervasive management control could suffice without direct control over disposal decisions. These are the opinion’s historical CERCLA formulations, without a claim that they state present doctrine unchanged. Id. at 1220–1222 & nn. 10–13.

Many findings supported corporate separateness: separate equipment, personnel, records, finances, and arm’s-length services. But the court had not sufficiently addressed shared senior officers, a document describing unified management, their signatures on an environmental consent order, or the timing of hazardous releases relative to a Canada officer’s plant-management work and a bank’s period of control. A remand for findings was required. Owner liability, analyzed separately through veil piercing, was rejected: Canada had sold its shares, and the record did not justify disregarding corporate separateness. Id. at 1222–1225 & nn. 14–19.

The Authority’s failure to present evidence of necessity and NCP consistency could not defeat the claim when the court itself had reserved that evidence for an uncompleted damages phase. It would receive an opportunity to present such evidence if reached on remand; the appellate court did not decide whether the original bifurcation was erroneous. Id. at 1225–1226 & n. 20.

## Exact judgment and significance

The court affirmed judgment for Canada and IFIM on replacement-water and treatment costs. The common finding of no threatened contamination also prevented an inconsistent judgment against nonappearing IFIM under *Frow*. It vacated both defendants’ monitoring-and-evaluation-cost judgments and remanded. Canada’s possible control did not settle IFIM’s control; the trial court had to explain its IFIM ruling and consider a properly requested default judgment if sought. Finally, the court affirmed denial of Canada’s sanctions motions for lack of an abuse of discretion. Id. at 1226–1227. Becker wrote for a three-judge panel with no separate writing. The procedural distinction is between the permitted manner of producing findings and their substantive completeness: ordinary deference survives oral delivery and copied language, while unaddressed facts material to a distinct claim can still require remand.
