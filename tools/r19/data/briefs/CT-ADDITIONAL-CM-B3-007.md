# McCarthy Well Co. v. St. Peter Creamery, Inc.
410 N.W.2d 312 (Minn. 1987) | Contracts

## Court and disposition
The Minnesota Supreme Court decided the case on August 7, 1987, in an opinion by Justice Scott. The report contains no concurrence or dissent and does not give a numerical vote. Its formal disposition is “Affirmed in part; reversed in part.” The opinion sustains recovery of economic losses under negligence principles, refuses to enforce the unreadable exculpatory clause, and finds no reversible prejudice from the jury inquiry or late disclosures. It rejects the intermediate court's characterization of the clause as unconscionable and expressly leaves its alternative objection to the clause's breadth undecided. The published disposition does not separately direct a new trial or state a revised dollar judgment; those directions should not be added to the report. (410 N.W.2d at 312–313, 315–317.)

## Transaction, failures, and litigation
St. Peter Creamery engaged McCarthy Well to restore its artesian well to its original capacity. After a free inspection, the creamery's manager contacted the company in June 1979. McCarthy sent an acknowledgment-of-order form in August and began work two weeks later. It removed a copper liner, airlifted sand, televised the well, unsuccessfully attempted to remove a donut from the casing, and blasted with dynamite. Blasting increased water flow. In late October it installed a turbine pump. (Id. at 314.)

The November bill was $34,573.27, including $8,329.45 for the pump. The creamery paid only part, and McCarthy sued on March 12, 1980, ultimately seeking $23,422.02 plus interest. The shaft broke three days after suit and was repaired later that month; it broke again in April. Because the pump was under warranty, a subsequent $4,369 repair bill charged labor only. Following another failure in August, the creamery hired another company to install a pump. That pump failed in January 1981; inspection disclosed a casing hole, and the creamery dug a new well and installed another pump in July. (Id. at 313–314.)

The creamery counterclaimed, alleging negligent work and misrepresentation of the time required. A jury found both parties negligent, found $190,000 in creamery damages, and assigned 75 percent of the fault to McCarthy. These are actual jury findings, distinct from the pleaded allegations. McCarthy appealed the judgment and denial of a new trial. The Court of Appeals upheld the challenged rulings, and the Supreme Court addressed four issues. (Id. at 313–314.)

## Services, goods, and economic loss
McCarthy invoked Superwood's prohibition on recovering economic losses through negligence or strict products liability in commercial transactions, apart from personal injury or damage to other property. The Supreme Court explained that the prohibition rested on protecting the rights and remedies supplied by Article 2 of the U.C.C. For this doctrine, a commercial transaction meant one governed by that article. Without Article 2's remedial structure, that particular rationale for excluding tort recovery did not apply. This was a definition within the Superwood doctrine, not a declaration that an agreement between businesses is noncommercial for every purpose. (Id. at 314–315.)

For a mixed goods-and-services agreement, the court applied the predominant-purpose test. The object here was restoration of the well through extensive services, with the new pump one part of that work. Its identified cost was only $8,329.45 of the $34,573.27 bill. Footnote 1 expressly rejects treating purchase and installation of the pump as a separate transaction: the parties had one agreement performed over time. Because services predominated, Article 2 did not govern, and Superwood did not bar the creamery's negligence recovery. The court thus did not need to resolve the case by deciding whether the dried milk constituted “other property” under an Article 2 transaction. (Id. at 315 & n. 1.)

## Why the exculpatory term failed
The reverse of the contractor's form contained extensive terms, including a provision disclaiming any other damage or liability arising from its work. The trial court excluded that reverse side because it was illegible. The intermediate court affirmed on unconscionability grounds and alternatively because the provision was not confined to negligence. The Supreme Court declined to decide the breadth issue and refused to describe the clause as substantively unconscionable. Its sufficient ground was that the customer could not know the terms because they were unreadable. (Id. at 315–316.)

The physical presentation mattered. Two columns covered the entire reverse of an eleven-by-eight-and-one-half-inch page, leaving no room for amendments. Tiny print, apparently about three-point type, appeared on dark paper with no highlighting of sections or subsections. The exculpatory term lay in the middle of the dense text. Footnote 2 compares this type to roughly half the size of newspaper classified print and reports that the court itself resorted to enlargement to decipher it. The court suspected that larger original print had been reduced to fit the page. (Id. at 315–316 & n. 2.)

The defect was consequently not that clearly disclosed terms had excessively harsh consequences. It was the practical inability to identify those terms at all. The court refused enforcement as a matter of law, without deciding whether the same exculpatory language, presented readably, would have been enforceable. The assigned passage at printed page 669 correctly preserves that distinction between unreadability and the usual conception of unconscionability. (Id. at 316.)

## Trial procedure and prejudice
During voir dire the judge asked the panel whether anyone had an interest in Great American Insurance Company. No one responded. The governing rule permitted inquiry into interests in an interested insurer, and the scope rested in the trial judge's discretion. The record did not explain the reason for the question, but it was asked once and did not identify the company as McCarthy's insurer. The Supreme Court found no abuse of discretion. (Id. at 316.)

The creamery also disclosed more specific negligence grounds and two intended experts only five days before trial. The judge admitted the additional negligence evidence and permitted Joel Schaefer, but excluded Glen Anderson as an expert. The specific negligence subjects had already been explored in depositions; the record and defense counsel's trial performance showed no prejudicial surprise. Schaefer had been deposed as a fact witness, his eventual testimony had been explored, and the late expert designation did not appear to hinder cross-examination. Although interrogatory answers must be supplemented, drastic sanctions were tied to prejudice, and the remedies remained discretionary. Those rulings were affirmed. (Id. at 316–317.)
