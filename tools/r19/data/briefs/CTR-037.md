Swinton v. Whitinsville Savings Bank
Citation and Parties
Swinton v. Whitinsville Savings Bank, 311 Mass. 677, 42 N.E.2d 808 (1942). Massachusetts Supreme Judicial
Court; Justice Qua. Assigned excerpt: CB 510-511; notes, 511-512. Swinton was the purchaser and tort plaintiff.
The bank was the seller and defendant.
Alleged Material Facts
The bank sold Swinton a house in Newton for use as his family's home. The declaration alleged that the house was
infested with termites, the bank knew about the infestation and internal damage, and ordinary inspection would not
readily reveal the condition. Swinton claimed he did not know of the termites when purchasing and later incurred
substantial expenses for repair and termite control. Full-opinion addition: the sale occurred around September 12,
1938, and discovery was alleged around August 30, 1940. CB 510; 311 Mass. at 677-678.
The pleading described the bank's conduct as fraudulent concealment. But it did not allege a false statement,
misleading half-truth, affirmative obstruction of investigation, fiduciary relationship, or special relationship of trust.
The legal question arose from silence despite knowledge, rather than an allegation that the bank covered damaged
beams or lied in response to a direct question. CB 510-511.
Procedural History
The Superior Court sustained a demurrer to the declaration. Swinton appealed. A demurrer tests whether the pleaded
facts amount to a legally sufficient claim; it does not resolve disputed evidence after trial. The Supreme Judicial
Court affirmed the order and directed judgment for the bank. 311 Mass. at 677-679; CB 511.
Issue, Rule, and Holding
In an arm's-length real-estate sale, does a seller's knowing failure to disclose a concealed termite infestation, without
an additional duty-triggering circumstance, support the pleaded tort claim? The court held that bare nondisclosure
did not create liability under the Massachusetts rule it applied. Characterizing the silence as fraudulent added
nothing without supporting facts establishing a duty to speak. CB 510-511.
Reasoning
The court distinguished moral disapproval from the legal obligation to disclose. It acknowledged the appeal of
protecting a buyer from an unfamiliar hidden danger. But it feared that liability based solely on superior knowledge
of a defect would impose an expansive requirement that each party reveal information affecting the other's
evaluation of a transaction. CB 510-511; 311 Mass. at 678-679.
The bank had not, on the allegations, induced a misleading impression through a statement or active concealment.
Nor had it assumed a role requiring special loyalty or disclosure. Treating an arm's-length transaction as though it
carried that duty would substantially alter the rule the court understood its precedents to establish. The opinion
therefore treats silence differently from a lie or a half-truth.
The court also resisted making a special rule for termites based on how common the infestation was or how likely a
buyer was to discover it. Its reasoning sought a general administrable boundary. That policy choice is open to
criticism: the seller knew about a serious hidden condition and the buyer did not. A brief should preserve both the
rule and the tension, rather than turning the decision into approval of the seller's conduct.

Scope and Remedy
The disposition rejected the pleaded tort action. It did not award specific performance to the seller, adjudicate a
warranty claim, or conduct a separate rescission analysis. The assigned notes expressly ask how those alternative
remedial settings might differ. One should not report that the court held every possible remedy unavailable, nor
assume that switching the requested remedy would necessarily cure the absence of a duty or actionable
misrepresentation. CB 511-512.
The case also should not be presented as a universal contemporary rule for home sales. The assigned notes describe
decisions recognizing duties concerning latent defects, builder warranties, and statutory disclosure regimes. Their
existence shows why governing jurisdiction, type of sale, and applicable legislation matter. The packet uses Swinton
for its historical doctrinal position, not as a current nationwide seller's checklist. CB 511-512.
Applications and Comparison
Application: covered damage. If the seller actively masks termite damage, the facts no longer fit bare
nondisclosure. The inquiry becomes whether conduct prevented discovery or falsely represented the property's
condition.
Application: partial answer. If the seller says “we have never needed structural repairs” while withholding a
current infestation, analyze whether the answer creates a misleading impression. The duty can arise from what was
said and implied, even if there was no initial obligation to volunteer every fact.
Comparison with Kannavos. Both sellers knew adverse information. The decisive distinction is not simply that
one defect was more serious or more accessible. In Kannavos, affirmative marketing and representations about
rental operation made the omitted restrictions deceptive. In fact, the zoning problem was discoverable from public
records, yet the buyers received relief. CB 513-516.
