# United States v. Perkins
116 U.S. 483 (1886), decided January 25, 1886 | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## A naval cadet’s discharge and claim for continuing pay

Perkins entered the Naval Academy as a cadet-engineer in 1877 and graduated on June 10, 1881. On June 26, 1883, the Secretary of the Navy notified him that he was not needed to fill a vacancy arising during the preceding year and would be honorably discharged effective June 30. The letter offered one year’s sea pay under the August 5, 1882 statute governing cadet-midshipmen. Perkins protested that the discharge was unlawful, refused the payment, and treated himself as remaining in naval service. (483.)

He sued in the Court of Claims for $100 in salary accruing between June 30 and September 1, 1883, at the statutory rate of $600 annually under Revised Statutes § 1556. That court awarded the requested amount. The United States appealed. The Supreme Court described this case as differing from the just-decided *Redgrave* case in the particular that Perkins sought pay accruing after the asserted discharge. The actual opinion referred to that related decision rather than reproducing its statutory analysis. (483–484.)

## The government’s alternative removal theory

The question presented here was whether, even if the 1882 statute did not justify the discharge, the Secretary possessed an independent power to dismiss Perkins at will. The government contended that a cadet-engineer was not an officer within Revised Statutes § 1229. That provision prohibited dismissal of a military or naval officer in peacetime except pursuant to a court-martial sentence or its commutation. If Perkins was outside the officer category, the government argued, removal was implied in the Secretary’s appointment power. It also argued that a statutory restriction on removal impermissibly infringed the Executive’s constitutional prerogative. (484.)

## Inferior-officer status and Congress’s authority

Justice Matthews delivered the Court’s opinion by quoting and expressly adopting the Court of Claims’ reasoning. The lower court had treated the graduate cadet-engineer as already in naval service. Congress expressly vested appointment of cadet-engineers in the Secretary of the Navy. Under the Appointments Clause, an appointment of that kind made the appointee an inferior officer, rather than merely an employee. The claimed statutory protection therefore could not be avoided by denying Perkins officer status. (484.)

The opinion then separated the constitutional questions. Whether Congress could restrict removal of an officer appointed by the President with the Senate’s advice and consent did not arise and was expressly left undecided. Perkins had been appointed through Congress’s statutory vesting of inferior-officer appointment in a department head. Congress’s constitutional authority to select that appointing mechanism implied authority to limit, restrict, and regulate removal of the officers appointed under it. A department head did not possess an independent constitutional appointment prerogative over these offices. The head had to follow Congress’s enactment both in appointing and in matters incident to appointment. (484–485.)

That reasoning recognizes removal as incident to appointment without making the incident immune from statutory regulation. A default inference of at-will removal could not displace the protections Congress imposed in this setting. The decision neither determined the validity of restrictions on presidential removal of Senate-confirmed officers nor decided that all executive personnel occupy the same constitutional category. (484–485.)

## Application and judgment

Perkins had not failed an examination; had not been dismissed for misconduct under Revised Statutes § 1525; and had not been dismissed pursuant to a court-martial sentence or its commutation under § 1229. The Secretary’s asserted at-will authority supplied no alternative lawful basis for the discharge. Perkins remained in office and was entitled to its pay. The Court expressly adopted those conclusions and **affirmed the Court of Claims’ $100 judgment**. The opinion contains no separate writing, judicial footnote, or stated numerical vote. (483, 485.)
