# Cawley v. Weiner
236 N.Y. 357, 140 N.E. 724 (1923) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
The New York Court of Appeals, in Judge Crane’s opinion decided July 13, 1923, unanimously reversed the judgments for the builder and ordered a new trial, with costs to abide the event. Chief Judge Hiscock and Judges Hogan, Cardozo, Pound, McLaughlin, and Andrews concurred. The court identified two independently sufficient errors: excluding evidence of additional construction defects on an automatic waiver or estoppel theory, and awarding the full contract balance despite findings of defects without explaining or measuring an appropriate deduction. It did not determine the owners’ final liability or fix damages. (236 N.Y. 358–359, 363–364.)

## Construction contract and owners’ objections
Anthony B. Cawley agreed in writing on January 31, 1921, to erect and finish a dwelling and garage for the Weiners at Port Washington, Nassau County, for $8,500. Work proceeded through June 9; during construction the parties made five modifications with the owners’ request and consent. Around June 1 the owners entered the still-unfinished bungalow on their land and gave the builder a list of seventeen items they wanted changed or supplied. After claiming completion, Cawley filed a mechanic’s lien for the unpaid balance and sued to foreclose. His complaint alleged substantial performance, rather than complete performance. (359–360.)

At trial the owners sought to prove three defects outside their earlier list: a sagging floor, omission of a specified supporting arch beneath the living-room fireplace, and a half-inch crack in the first-story brick wall. They repeatedly sought to establish these conditions through cross-examination, direct evidence, and expert testimony. The trial judge excluded the proof over exceptions because the owners had not listed those objections earlier. He reasoned that, absent a showing that an omitted defect could not then have been discovered, the owners’ specification of some defects waived or estopped reliance on all others. These excluded defects were proposed proof, not appellate findings that the conditions existed. (360–361.)

## Proceedings and the limits of review
The Nassau County Court found substantial performance and awarded the builder the full amount claimed, less a small reduction concerning extras. The Appellate Division unanimously affirmed. The Court of Appeals accepted the binding factual findings but reviewed whether the evidentiary rulings were lawful and whether those findings could support the judgment. The owners argued that the judge had barred material defenses without the facts needed for waiver or estoppel and had allowed full payment notwithstanding proved departures from the specifications. The builder relied on the owners’ deliberate list and maintained that the substantially performed contract justified recovery. (358–359.)

## Occupancy and a partial defect list did not automatically surrender defenses
The court distinguished a house constructed on the owners’ land from a sale of goods. The owners could move into and use their house without thereby waiving construction defects. Nor did providing a list automatically estop them from proving other departures. The builder claimed no change of position induced by that list. The court separated actual waiver—acceptance with knowledge of defects—from estoppel, which required circumstances making it unfair for the owners to rely on their earlier omitted objections. (361–362.)

Quoting Williston, the court explained that specifying one objection could create estoppel if it operated as deception on which the contractor relied, preventing complete performance that otherwise would have occurred. It mattered whether the additional objections, if disclosed, could readily have been remedied. Simply omitting them did not establish those facts. The trial judge should have received the evidence and determined whether there was a factual waiver or estoppel, rather than treating the list itself as conclusive as a matter of law. The court did not rule out a factually established defense on retrial: once the owners’ evidence was admitted, the builder might prove circumstances supporting waiver or estoppel. Authorities invoked for the categorical exclusion had to be understood in their own factual settings. (362–363.)

## Substantial performance did not justify unexplained full payment
The second error involved defects the trial judge had actually found at the owners’ request. The builder substituted cement bricks for the specified No. 2 brick in the cesspool, regarding them as equivalent, omitted a bricked dome and a three-inch cement slab, and arranged drainage so that, when the cesspool reached the pipe’s height, drainage backed into the house. The dormer roof was six inches out of level, with its outer edge higher than its connection to the main roof; the sun parlor leaked around inward-opening windows. Those findings differed from the additional defects whose proof had been excluded. (363.)

Relying on Jacob & Youngs v. Kent and Spence v. Ham, the court required the builder to address these departures. They might or might not have been substantial modifications. The builder bore the burden of explaining their cause, including whether they resulted from the plans rather than defective workmanship. If they were defects properly compensated by an allowance, he should have proved reasonable remedial cost. The trial court had awarded the full contract price without a deduction or a finding explaining why none was warranted. That combination of findings and award could not stand. The court did not decide that every identified variation defeated substantial performance; it required the missing explanation and, where appropriate, measurement of an allowance. (363–364.)

## Exact disposition and assigned significance
Both lower judgments were reversed and a new trial granted, with costs dependent on the eventual result. The assigned account on printed page 852 focuses on successive objections: a builder’s practical desire for a definitive list does not itself establish a legal forfeiture of unlisted defenses. The complete judgment adds the second ground for reversal concerning the builder’s burden to account for defects already found. It leaves waiver, estoppel, the additional defects, and any appropriate monetary allowance to an evidentiary determination rather than deciding them against either party as fixed facts. (364.)
