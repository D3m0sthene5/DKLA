# Blumenthal v. Trump
949 F.3d 14 (D.C. Cir. 2020) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Facts and the asserted voting injury

Two hundred fifteen members of Congress—twenty-nine senators and 186 representatives—sued President Trump in his official capacity. They alleged that his financial interests in businesses dealing with foreign governments caused him to accept, or inevitably to accept, foreign emoluments without congressional consent. The Foreign Emoluments Clause restricts acceptance of foreign presents, emoluments, offices and titles by persons holding an office of profit or trust under the United States without the consent of Congress. The plaintiffs claimed that accepting the benefits first completely nullified their opportunity to vote on granting or withholding consent. These were allegations considered at the dismissal stage, not findings establishing prohibited transactions or resolving the Clause’s application to the President. (Official opinion 3–5 & n. 1; 10.)

The members argued that this Clause was distinctive because it required prior congressional approval and regulated private conduct. They maintained that Congress’s spending power could not effectively prevent conduct through the President’s private businesses, leaving no adequate legislative remedy for the denied voting opportunity. They sought declaratory and injunctive relief in a complaint filed June 14, 2017. The President responded that they lacked standing, lacked a cause of action, failed to state a claim, and could not constitutionally obtain an injunction against him in his official capacity. (Opinion 4–6.)

## How the interlocutory appeal reached the court

The district court separated standing from the other dismissal issues and first found standing. It later held that the members had an implied equitable cause of action and had stated a Foreign Emoluments Clause claim. The President’s certification requests for interlocutory review were denied. He then sought mandamus in the D.C. Circuit. That court denied the petition without prejudice but required immediate reconsideration of certification. The district court consequently certified both dismissal denials and stayed proceedings, and the Circuit permitted the interlocutory appeal under 28 U.S.C. § 1292(b). This was review of orders refusing to dismiss, not an appeal from a trial finding that the President had violated the Clause. (Opinion 6–7.)

A unanimous per curiam panel comprising Judges Henderson, Tatel and Griffith decided the case on February 7, 2020. It reviewed legal determinations de novo and assumed the truth of material factual allegations. It concluded that the members lacked Article III standing, **reversed the district court’s standing ruling and remanded with instructions to dismiss the complaint**. It separately **vacated as moot** the order finding a cause of action and a sufficient claim. The two distinct treatments of the district court’s orders should not be compressed into an affirmance or a merits rejection of the emoluments allegations. (Opinion 1–3, 7, 11–12.)

## Why the members lacked standing

The court began with Raines, which denied individual members standing to challenge the Line Item Veto Act based on diluted legislative power. The present members likewise alleged injury in their official roles, not a private right held personally. They had not been singled out: the alleged loss of power was shared with the other 320 members of Congress who did not join the suit. The number joining did not convert an institutional grievance into a personal injury. Bethune-Hill reinforced that individual members cannot assert institutional interests belonging to a legislative body, just as a single chamber cannot automatically assert interests assigned to the whole legislature. (Opinion 7–10.)

The plaintiffs conceded that they represented neither the House nor the Senate. Twenty-nine senators and 186 representatives were not a majority of either chamber, and therefore could not themselves approve or deny congressional consent. Their inability to act determinatively mattered; their cohort’s considerable size did not. For this standing inquiry the court treated their claim no differently from a single member’s claim. The district court’s suggestion that an institutional claim need not be brought by or for the institution could not survive Raines and Bethune-Hill. The Circuit stated that an institutional plaintiff could assert an institutional injury only if the injury also satisfied the requirement of being sufficiently concrete and particularized rather than abstract and widely dispersed. It did not hold that Congress itself automatically had standing in this controversy. (Opinion 9–11 & n. 4.)

Coleman’s narrow vote-nullification example did not change the outcome. As later explained in Raines, it concerned legislators whose votes would have sufficed to defeat or enact a specific legislative act but were completely nullified. These congressional plaintiffs did not possess such a determinative voting bloc. The court did not overrule Coleman, adjudicate a challenge by a majority or the whole institution, or decide the standing of different emoluments plaintiffs pursuing commercial, sovereign or proprietary injuries. Its note 2 identified those different pending cases without deciding them. Circuit precedents Chenoweth and Campbell further supported the result for individual members suing over executive action. (Opinion 7–11 & nn. 2–5.)

## Limits and assigned scope

The especially rigorous standing inquiry appropriate to a federal interbranch dispute did not require a wider constitutional ruling here. Raines and Bethune-Hill disposed of these plaintiffs’ claim, and the panel expressly declined to determine how Raines might apply elsewhere. The members remained free to press their arguments politically; the federal judiciary could not resolve this suit without an Article III case or controversy. The opinion did not decide the meaning of an emolument, the legality of any business transaction, presidential coverage under the Clause, availability of an equitable action to a proper plaintiff, or the permissibility of the requested presidential injunction. (Opinion 8–12.)
