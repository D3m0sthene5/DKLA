# Walker v. City of Hutchinson
352 U.S. 112 (1956) | Civil Procedure

## Decision, participation, and assignment

The Supreme Court decided Walker on December 10, 1956, after argument on October 15–16. Justice Black wrote the Court's opinion, which Justice Douglas announced. Justices Frankfurter and Burton dissented separately, and Justice Brennan did not participate: the resulting judgment was 6–2. The complete report includes six Court footnotes, two Frankfurter footnotes, and his appendix reproducing the amended petition. The assigned account is the Walker parenthetical in Mullane Note 4, Civil Procedure PDF page 148, printed page 246. [Complete official report](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-352/pdf/USREPORTS-352-112.pdf).

## A street project and a compensation hearing the owner missed

Lee Walker owned land in Hutchinson, Kansas. In 1954, the City instituted condemnation proceedings in the Reno County district court to obtain some of it for opening, widening, and extending city streets. Under section 26-201, the court appointed three commissioners to appraise the property and assess other damages caused by its appropriation. Section 26-202 required at least ten days' notice of the assessment hearing, but allowed either written notice or a single publication in the official city newspaper. The City used publication and gave Walker no written notice. 352 U.S. at 112–114 & nn.1–2.

The commissioners fixed compensation at $725, which was deposited with the city treasurer for Walker's benefit. An appeal from their award had to be taken within thirty days after their report was filed. Walker did not appeal within that period. He later brought an equitable action alleging that he had received no notice and had known nothing of the condemnation until the appeal period had expired. He challenged publication as insufficient under the Fourteenth Amendment and sought to restrain the City's entry onto his property, along with other appropriate relief. At 114.

The amended petition reproduced in Frankfurter's appendix supplies the concrete chronology behind those allegations. Walker alleged that he had owned the property since 1905, that the City filed its condemnation application on April 12, 1954, and that its only notice was an April 14 newspaper publication. He alleged that he did not learn of the effort to take the land until about mid-August. He described the proposed road as a bypass for the City's sports arena and asserted threatened entry and irreparable harm. Those are the petition's allegations, rather than independent Supreme Court findings about the project or the property's value. At 122–126, appendix paragraphs 2–14.

The trial court denied relief after a hearing because it considered the statutory publication constitutionally sufficient. The Kansas Supreme Court affirmed. The Supreme Court heard an appeal under the then-applicable section 1257(2). The question the majority considered necessary to decide was the adequacy of notice for the proceeding to fix compensation, rather than every aspect of the City's power to take the land. At 114–115.

## The majority's understanding of the pleading

The petition's request for an injunction raised a potential mismatch between the remedy requested and the notice issue. Judicial note 3 explains how the majority resolved it. The Kansas Supreme Court had addressed the constitutionality of section 26-202, governing the compensation proceeding. Kansas also required actual damage to maintain an equitable suit. The majority inferred that the state court had treated the pleading as sufficiently alleging monetary harm from lack of notice concerning compensation. It accepted that construction, rather than treating the injunction request as necessarily an attack only on the taking itself. At 114–115 n.3.

That interpretation mattered because a hearing on compensation is a constitutional safeguard independently of whether an owner can prevent the public project. The majority did not calculate a different award or hold that Walker's land must remain untouched. It considered whether the procedure adequately gave him a chance to contest the amount due. At 115–117.

## Why the newspaper method failed

An owner whose property is taken for public use must have a hearing on just compensation. Notice makes that hearing meaningful. Applying Mullane, the Court required a feasible method reasonably calculated to inform the affected person of the proceeding. It expressly rejected a rigid notice formula for every circumstance and acknowledged that missing or unknown people can make personal notice impracticable. At 115–116 & n.4.

But those practical obstacles were absent here. Walker's name was known to the City and appeared in official records. The Court found no compelling or even persuasive reason why direct notice could not be given. Newspaper publication rarely reaches the individual whose property rights are at stake; even a letter would have told Walker that his property was about to be taken and that he needed to appear to be heard about its value. The fact that the statute permitted publication did not establish that this method met the federal constitutional standard for this known owner. At 116.

Note 5 identifies an intervening legislative change. In 1955, Kansas amended the statute to require mailing a copy of the newspaper notice to an owner's last known residence unless diligent inquiry could not locate it. The Court cited that amendment as context; the notice Walker received in the 1954 proceeding was governed by the earlier procedure. The brief does not treat the historical amendment as a verification of today's Kansas law. At 116 n.5.

## The City's precedents did not decide this situation

The State principally relied on Huling, an 1889 condemnation decision sustaining publication for a nonresident owner. Walker was a Kansas resident, so the Court distinguished that setting and expressly declined to decide how far Mullane had undermined Huling's reasoning. This was not an unqualified overruling of all earlier condemnation-notice decisions. At 116.

Note 6 also answers reliance on North Laramie and Bragg. The first involved a statute requiring both publication and registered mailing of the newspaper to the owner. The second provided written notification and thirty days to appeal, thereby offering a full hearing in the reviewing court. Their actual protections did not conflict with the Court's conclusion about publication alone here. At 116–117 n.6.

Finally, the governmental character of condemnation did not diminish the owner's right to be heard. The majority saw no reason that meaningful notice about compensation would interfere with orderly acquisition of property. Without it, authorities could effectively set the amount one-sidedly. At 117.

## Frankfurter's dissent: what injury was actually pleaded?

Frankfurter did not resolve whether notice was constitutionally adequate for a properly presented compensation challenge. His objection concerned the case before the Court. In his reading, Walker sought to retain possession and permanently prevent trespass because the lack of notice invalidated the taking. Walker nowhere alleged that $725 was less than the fair value of the land or sought an injunction only until a compensation hearing could occur. A generic assertion of irreparable harm, following the allegation of threatened continuous entry, did not allege a monetary shortfall. At 117–120.

The full amended petition in Frankfurter's appendix lets the reader assess that disagreement. Its specific remedy was immediate and permanent restraint against entry; its final prayer also requested whatever other relief seemed just and equitable. The majority inferred a sufficient damages-related pleading from the state court's treatment. Frankfurter considered that inference unsupported because the Kansas court had upheld the statute without actually construing the pleading. At 120–126; Court n.3.

For Frankfurter, Article III required an actual controversy involving the particular alleged injury, rather than an abstract constitutional assessment. A state can take property before paying compensation if adequate payment arrangements exist. Without a claim that the compensation arrangements operated inadequately for Walker, a defective hearing notice did not itself establish that the taking was invalid. He would affirm, or at least return the case to Kansas for a construction of the pleading. He emphasized that the majority's ruling did not invalidate the taking itself or necessarily require an injunction if Walker's rights were protected. At 120–122.

His first footnote reproduces the additional statute governing when title and possession vest in the City. His second identifies the complete complaint appended at 122–126. These details explain why the legal sufficiency of compensation notice and the right to retain possession were different questions. At 118 n.1, 119 n.2.

## Burton's dissent: room for legislative choice

Burton addressed the substantive notice issue on the assumption that the case concerned the compensation hearing. Although just compensation was mandatory, he considered the length and method of notice largely within state legislative discretion, balancing the public's acquisition needs against the opportunity for a hearing. A provision could be undesirable or short without crossing the federal constitutional boundary. At 126–127.

He feared that striking down this established method would cast uncertainty over condemnation proceedings nationwide. He relied on Huling and other historical precedents, and on the Tenth Circuit's comparable Collins decision, in which the Supreme Court had denied certiorari the previous term. He would affirm. This was a defense of constitutional latitude for the State, distinct from Frankfurter's objection about the particular pleading and injury. At 127.

## Exact judgment and the assignment's lesson

The Court reversed the Kansas Supreme Court and remanded for further proceedings consistent with its opinion. Publication alone was inadequate for this known resident owner in the compensation proceeding. The Court did not award a higher sum, hold that the condemnation itself was invalid, or direct issuance of the permanent injunction sought in the petition. At 117, 122.

The assigned sentence accurately captures the known resident owner and newspaper-only notice. The full report adds the missed thirty-day compensation appeal, the majority's construction of the pleading, two distinct dissenting grounds, and the important separation between an opportunity to contest compensation and a right to stop the taking. Assigned printed 246; actual 114–127.
