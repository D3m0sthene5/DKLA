# Cummings v. General Motors Corp.

**Citation:** 365 F.3d 944 (2004). **Court and date:** Tenth Circuit, April 28, 2004, modified on partial grant of rehearing June 2, 2004; Nos. 02-6340 and 03-6209. **Opinion:** Paul Kelly, Jr.; McKay and Henry joined without separate writings. **Course:** Civil Procedure — initial disclosure, discovery requests, judgment as a matter of law, and relief from judgment. **Assigned:** CivPro.pdf, physical page 472, printed pages 918–919, Cummings paragraph and quotation. The caption calls the wife Trace; the opinion calls her Tracey.

## Facts and proceedings

Gregory Cummings drove a 1995 Pontiac Grand Am through a partially obscured stop sign at a rural Oklahoma T-intersection on September 13, 1998. The car crossed a ditch into a field. His wife sustained a vertebral compression fracture and paraplegia; the other passengers suffered minor injuries. Their children occupied the rear seats, including a three-month-old in a child seat behind the wife. The plaintiffs alleged defective seat and seat-belt design and inadequate warnings. GM disputed defect and causation, asserting that her posture and reclined position explained the injuries. (946–948.)

The parties disputed whether her seat was reclined about 25 degrees or 40–45 degrees and whether she was slouched or turned toward the children. Plaintiffs maintained that a rear-facing child seat prevented greater recline. GM offered evidence that the child seat was forward-facing. These were disputed trial positions, not appellate findings establishing either configuration. (947–948.)

Discovery disputes produced three motions to compel and several protective-order requests. The magistrate denied the plaintiffs' motions, granted GM protection and fees, and the district court affirmed on de novo review. At trial both sides moved under Rule 50(a). The plaintiffs' motion concerned GM's misuse defense and an instruction on that defense, rather than liability generally. Both motions were denied; the jury returned a defense verdict, and plaintiffs filed no post-verdict motion. (947–949.)

A month after the verdict, plaintiffs learned about six GM child-safety-seat acceleration-test videos already produced in unrelated litigation. They argued that the videos would undermine GM's forward-facing-seat theory because their infant had escaped injury. Seven months after discovering them, they sought relief under Rule 60(b)(3). The district court denied relief for delay and failure to establish misconduct. The Tenth Circuit consolidated appeals from the judgment and that denial. (948–949, 954–957.)

## Rule 50 holdings — historical preservation rule

**The plaintiffs' trial motion preserved the misuse issue, but did not preserve an ordinary sufficiency challenge to liability generally.** Liberal construction dispensed with technical precision, not adequate notice of the particular grounds. The court reviewed the broader liability challenge only for plain error amounting to a miscarriage of justice and found none. (949–951.)

Under then-binding Tenth Circuit precedent, failure to renew after verdict did not bar review of an issue properly raised before submission to the jury. The court nevertheless could grant only a new trial, rather than direct entry of judgment contrary to the verdict. Its sole judicial footnote acknowledged disagreement among circuits and explained its adherence to circuit precedent. This is an account of the 2004 decision. The textbook expressly records abrogation on other grounds by Unitherm Food Systems, Inc. v. Swift-Eckrich, Inc., 546 U.S. 394 (2006); the permissive Rule 50(b) review rule must not be presented as current law. (950–951 & 950 n.1; assigned 918–919.)

Reviewing the misuse issue de novo, the court found evidence supporting both sides and upheld submission to the jury. Oklahoma distinguished abnormal or unanticipated use from careless use for a proper purpose; contributory negligence was not a products-liability defense. The plaintiffs argued foreseeable recline, while GM relied on warnings and evidence that recline was intended for a parked car. Viewing evidence and reasonable inferences favorably to GM, the court could not resolve these competing positions as a matter of law. (951–952.)

## Discovery and initial disclosure

**The discovery rulings were affirmed under abuse-of-discretion review.** The plaintiffs identified no record support for their general allegation of inadequate responses. The court agreed that imprecise requests and notices had contributed to unnecessary disputes. GM had designated organizational deposition representatives whom the plaintiffs failed to depose. (952–953.)

Rule 30(b)(6) allowed a notice to the corporation, which selected its representatives. Plaintiffs could not use that procedure to compel a particular employee of their choice. For the employee at issue, who had not been designated, attendance had to be secured through a subpoena as for another nonparty witness. This procedural holding should not be expanded into a claim that corporations can refuse all testimony about their designs. (953.)

The magistrate had applied the 2000 amendments to Rule 26 without objection. Initial disclosure covered individuals with discoverable information whom the disclosing party might use to support its claims or defenses. It did not require automatic disclosure of every favorable or unfavorable witness or document the party did not intend to use. The narrower automatic obligation was distinct from responding to properly framed discovery requests. The court also upheld protection against proposed database searches found overly broad, duplicative, and unduly burdensome. (953–954.)

## Rule 60(b)(3): delay and absence of proven misconduct

**Filing within one year did not by itself make the motion timely.** The applicable rule also required a reasonable time. Plaintiffs waited seven months after learning of the videos. Vague references to other procedural channels and fear of Rule 11 sanctions did not explain why the inquiry needed that long. The district court could consider this unjustified delay in denying relief. (954–955.)

Independently, plaintiffs failed to establish misconduct. Relief required clear and convincing proof of fraud, misrepresentation, or misconduct that substantially interfered with full and fair preparation for and participation in trial. Failure to disclose properly requested material could qualify, but relevance alone did not establish a production duty. (955–956.)

The videos' relevance remained disputed: plaintiffs described infant-sized dummies, while GM described older child dummies, different seats, and different cars. **Assuming relevance**, the court held that the identified requests did not encompass these videos. The requests were either overly broad or directed to specified seat and belt components; the court had also limited discovery to specified vehicle model years and pertinent shared designs. GM did not plan to use the videos at trial, so the automatic-disclosure rule likewise imposed no duty to provide them. Cases involving concealment of clearly requested information did not control this record. The holding did not authorize withholding responsive material simply because it was unfavorable or would not be used. (956–957.)

## Disposition and textbook comparison

**Affirmed in all respects; all outstanding motions denied.** The court upheld the defense judgment, discovery rulings, and denial of Rule 60(b)(3) relief. It did not award a new trial, decide that the videos were irrelevant, or find that both plaintiffs sustained severe injuries. (957.)

The textbook focuses on the narrower automatic-disclosure obligation and how additional requests should follow initial disclosure. Its description compresses the litigation: only the wife suffered the severe injury described in the opinion, and the claims concerned seat design, restraints, and warnings. Its quotation at 953–954 comes from the discussion of witness identification and the 2000 rule; the later, document-specific analysis of the videos appears at 956–957. Keeping those discussions separate prevents confusion between automatic disclosure and production in response to a request. (Assigned 472/918–919.)
