# Oglebay Norton Co. v. Armco, Inc.
52 Ohio St. 3d 232, 556 N.E.2d 515 (1990) | Contracts

## A lasting shipping relationship and failed pricing machinery
The dispute arose from a 1957 agreement requiring Oglebay to maintain shipping capacity for Armco's iron ore transportation on the Great Lakes and requiring Armco to use that capacity. The agreement first looked to regular seasonal net contract rates recognized by leading iron ore shippers. If no such rate existed, the parties were to agree on a rate, taking into account rates for similar transportation charged by leading independent vessel operators. The parties thus designed two pricing mechanisms, rather than leaving the price entirely unexplained. Assigned casebook at 366; 52 Ohio St. 3d at 234–235.

The assigned introduction recounts four modifications during the next twenty-three years, each extending the relationship and requiring substantial investment by Oglebay. After the fourth extension, which carried the agreement through 2010, Oglebay began a $95 million capital-improvement program. Through 1983, the parties used a rate published in Skillings Mining Review. Following an industry downturn, they negotiated a satisfactory 1984 rate, but later could no longer agree. Oglebay sought declaratory relief in April 1986; Armco eventually counterclaimed for a declaration that the agreement was unenforceable. They continued to perform while the dispute was pending. These chronology details appear in the assigned introductory account; the judicial opinion supplies the reasoning reviewed below. Assigned casebook at 366.

By the relevant period, no new published benchmark was available. Armco also maintained that independent operators' rates were no longer publicly available, and the trial court had quashed subpoenas seeking that information from nonparties. Both designated mechanisms had therefore broken down. Armco argued that its consent to those mechanisms was not consent to a court-created price if they failed. The issue was whether the disappearance of the agreed means of calculating a term also destroyed the parties' underlying commitment. 52 Ohio St. 3d at 234–235.

## The lower courts' remedy and the issues on review
After trial, the court fixed $6.25 per gross ton for the 1986 season. For future seasons, it required the parties to notify it if they could not agree; it would appoint a mediator and require the chief executives to meet to establish a rate. The court of appeals affirmed. Armco challenged the finding of continuing contractual intent, the authority and evidentiary basis for the 1986 price, and the continuing equitable arrangement for negotiation and mediation through 2010. Assigned casebook at 366; 52 Ohio St. 3d at 234.

The Ohio Supreme Court described these as mixed questions of fact and law. Its treatment of the critical findings asked whether competent, credible evidence supported them. It did not simply select the price it preferred or decide that preserving every long-term agreement is desirable. The judicial per curiam opinion was joined by all seven listed justices: Chief Justice Moyer and Justices Sweeney, Holmes, Douglas, Wright, H. Brown, and Resnick. There was no separate opinion. Id. at 234–238.

## Evidence that the parties intended to remain bound
Armco's first argument was that neither party had agreed to remain bound when both pricing methods failed. The court treated that intention as a factual question. The trial record supported continuing commitment through the parties' long and close business relationship, including joint ventures, interlocking directorates, and Armco's stock ownership in Oglebay. Their agreement recognized Armco's vital and unique interest in having Oglebay's bulk vessel fleet dedicated to its needs, potentially involving up to 7.1 million gross tons of ore annually. Id. at 235.

Those facts supplied more than evidence that the parties had once wanted a transaction. They supported the trial court's finding that the dedicated capacity and ongoing shipping relationship were the central undertaking, and that the pricing provisions served that undertaking rather than operating as an unqualified condition on its existence. The Supreme Court upheld the finding because the trial court had ample evidence for it. It did not announce that a failed external benchmark always permits judicial substitution regardless of intent.

The capital investments described in the assigned introduction help explain why continuing commitment was commercially significant. Oglebay was adapting its vessels for the relationship rather than preparing one isolated shipment. The judicial holding nevertheless rests on the proven intent and contractual context, not a free-standing rule that investment alone gives a court authority to impose a bargain.

## Filling the price gap by a reasonable rate
Armco next contended that the court had no authority to impose a price outside the designated mechanisms. The Supreme Court approved the use of Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 33 and its comments. An otherwise binding bargain can receive a sufficiently definite meaning through usage, course of dealing, factual implication, or a term supplied by law. Comment e's treatment of an agreed market or third-party standard that is not set or recorded parallels UCC § 2-305. The court noted Ohio authority applying the statutory open-price principle by analogy to service contracts. This shipping-services case was therefore not treated as an ordinary sale of goods directly governed by the Code. Id. at 235–237.

The starting point remained the finding that the parties intended to be bound. On that premise, the failure of the calculation mechanisms left a gap capable of reasonable completion. Courts should not frustrate established contractual intent when they can fill such a gap fairly. This reasoning is materially different from imposing liability on parties who merely hoped to negotiate an agreement later.

The trial court's rate was $6 per gross ton plus twenty-five cents for use of self-unloading vessels. It considered the parties' extensive course of dealing, their respective detriment, comparative prices, and the depressed steel industry's economic circumstances. The appellate record contained several concrete reference points: an expert supported $7.44; Armco had paid $5 in 1985 when the published rate was $7.41; Oglebay quoted $5.66 for 1987; LTV Steel had paid $7.41 before bankruptcy renegotiations; and American Steamship quoted Armco $5.90 for 1986. The $6.25 rate fell within the range supported by that evidence. The Supreme Court therefore upheld it as a reasonable rate supported by competent, credible evidence. Id. at 236–237.

The result was not an authorization to choose any compromise between opposing demands. The evidence, the commercially relevant comparisons, and the parties' history supplied the basis for the judicially established price.

## Continuing equitable jurisdiction and mediation
Armco also challenged the court's authority to supervise pricing negotiations for future seasons. Restatement § 362 requires sufficiently certain terms for specific performance or an injunction, but its comments direct courts to use ordinary aids to interpretation and supplied terms before declaring certainty inadequate. The same context that permitted a reasonable price could thus help support an appropriate equitable order. Id. at 237.

Specific performance was justified because market changes and the agreement's long duration made an accurate award of future breach damages too speculative. The court also noted that a 1962 amendment had given Armco itself the right to seek specific performance of Oglebay's duties. The agreement's reciprocal context and unusual continuing relationship made it appropriate to preserve performance rather than try to estimate decades of lost shipping business. Id. at 237–238.

The court characterized required negotiation and mediation as a practical means of allowing the parties to work under their existing obligations. It neither added to nor subtracted from those significant obligations. The ruling approved a process for determining future seasonal prices; it did not fix $6.25 for every season through 2010 or give a mediator unrestricted authority to create different commercial commitments. Id. at 238.

The full judicial footnote also comments on the earlier subpoena ruling. The Supreme Court thought rate information under an appropriate protective order might have been useful and said the reasons for quashing instead were unclear. That observation explains the difficulty of obtaining comparative evidence. It is not a reversal of the subpoena ruling or an independent discovery holding. Id. at 235 n.4 in the preserved reporter reproduction; the Justia transcription renumbers it as note 1.

## Holding and judgment
The Ohio Supreme Court affirmed the court of appeals on all three questions: the supported finding of intent to remain bound, the reasonable 1986 rate, and continuing equitable jurisdiction with negotiation and mediation when the parties could not agree. The judgment was affirmed, preserving the trial court's arrangement. 52 Ohio St. 3d at 238.

For Contracts, the case demonstrates that uncertainty about an existing bargain's machinery differs from uncertainty about whether the parties made a binding bargain at all. Once the latter is established through the record, a reasonable price grounded in evidence can preserve their commitment, and an appropriately tailored equitable process can address recurring price gaps where long-term damages would be unreliable.
