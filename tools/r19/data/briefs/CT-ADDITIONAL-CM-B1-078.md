# Humble Oil & Refining Co. v. Westside Investment Corp.

428 S.W.2d 92 (Tex. 1968) | Contracts

## Decision and setting

The Texas Supreme Court held that Humble’s attempt to negotiate additional sale terms did not terminate its supported option. Humble later exercised the option unconditionally and on time, so the court reversed the judgments for Westside and rendered specific-performance relief for Humble. A separate broker’s commission claim required a trial and was remanded. Justice Smith wrote the reported opinion; the reporter labels it May 1, 1968, with rehearing denied on that same date. There is no separate writing or numerical voting lineup. All three judicial footnotes merely identify the abbreviated party names. 92–96 & nn.1–3 at 92.

## Option terms and performance

On April 5, 1963, Westside gave Humble an exclusive, irrevocable option to buy five subdivision lots outside San Antonio for a $35,000 purchase price. Humble paid $50 as consideration for the option. It could exercise by notice before 9 p.m. on June 4, 1963, accompanied by $1,750 earnest money paid then or within ten days. Those amounts would leave $33,200 due as the balance of the purchase price. The paid option was already a contract, distinct from the later purchase transaction it enabled. 93.

On May 2, Humble wrote that it exercised the option, but also asserted that Westside had agreed to extend gas, water, sewer, and electricity before closing. Its letter purported to amend the sale agreement accordingly and asked Westside to sign and return a copy accepting the amendment. On May 14, Humble sent a second communication exercising the option without qualification and allowing Westside to disregard the proposed amendment. It also paid $1,750 to the designated escrow agent, Commercial Abstract & Title Company. The parties agreed that this notice and earnest-money performance timely exercised the option unless the May 2 letter had already destroyed it. Most relevant facts were stipulated or established through summary-judgment affidavits. 93–94.

## Litigation and the seller’s argument

Humble sued on February 10, 1965, for specific performance. Realtor Marvin H. Mann intervened for $1,260 in brokerage charges. All three parties sought summary judgment. The trial court granted Westside’s motion and denied Humble’s and Mann’s; the court of civil appeals affirmed, 419 S.W.2d 448. 92–93.

Westside admitted the option’s existence but argued that Humble had rejected, repudiated, and terminated it. The May 2 letter, Westside reasoned, conditioned acceptance on new utility obligations and therefore rejected the original offer. The May 14 clarification allegedly confirmed that the earlier attempt had been qualified. The narrow formation question was whether that initial negotiation terminated the existing option, not whether the parties had agreed to the added utility term. 93–94.

## An option survives unsuccessful negotiation

The court distinguished the ordinary rule that a qualified acceptance rejects an offer from the consequences of a counterproposal under a paid option. An ordinary offer has not yet produced a contract. The supported option, however, is an independently completed agreement binding the optionor to keep the purchase opportunity open for a stated time. The holder creates the purchase obligation by satisfying that agreement’s conditions. The court’s extended quotation from James on Option Contracts explains why an ordinary rejection rule cannot automatically destroy rights already purchased under such a contract. 94.

The court also relied on *Cerbo v. Carabello*, where unsuccessful negotiation for a lower purchase price did not demonstrate abandonment of an existing option. It recognized that parties could abandon or modify an agreement through words or conduct; negotiation alone did not establish that result. Similarly, Humble had not surrendered its paid right to elect a purchase. It remained free to negotiate sale terms while preserving the existing option, and Westside remained obligated to honor that option through its specified period. The unqualified May 14 exercise therefore operated on a still-binding option. 94–95.

Corbin’s discussion reinforced the distinction between terminating an ordinary revocable offer and terminating contractual rights under a binding option. Westside’s authorities were distinguishable because they involved mere offers, no fixed option period, or no consideration. The court did not hold that every attempted acceptance containing new terms completes a purchase; it held that this attempt did not extinguish the paid option, which Humble subsequently exercised according to its terms. 95.

## The independent brokerage issue

The contract required Westside to pay brokerage charges and protect Humble from such claims. It named Clarence Jones Realty, which was to receive six percent of the gross sales price if the sale was completed. Mann alleged in his petition and affidavit that he was a licensed salesman associated with Jones when the option was negotiated, had procured the agreement, and was entitled under a continuing arrangement to sixty percent of the brokerage commission despite later leaving the firm. 95–96.

Westside did not deny those allegations, but they did not eliminate the unresolved issue concerning Mann’s entitlement. Jones Realty was not a party, and Westside had agreed to pay the firm the single six-percent commission. The supreme court therefore could not award Mann his claimed share on summary judgment. The judgment for Westside against him also could not stand: that separate cause required a new trial. 96.

## Exact disposition

The court severed Humble’s cause from Mann’s. It reversed the trial and appellate judgments concerning Humble and **rendered judgment** requiring Westside and its stipulated successors in interest, Ray Ellison and Boyce Gaskin, to specifically perform. They had to deliver a general warranty deed and a $35,000 owner’s title-insurance policy contemporaneously with Humble’s payment of the $33,200 balance. This was operative specific-performance relief, not merely permission to seek relief on remand. 96.

It separately reversed the judgments against Mann and **remanded his brokerage cause for a new trial**. All costs were assessed against Westside and its successors. The court did not award the $1,260 commission. 96.
