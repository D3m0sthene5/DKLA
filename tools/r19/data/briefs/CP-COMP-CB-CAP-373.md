# Watson v. Lowcountry Red Cross

**974 F.2d 482 (4th Cir. 1992).** Decided August 28, 1992; amended November 2, 1992. Hall wrote the court's opinion; Widener concurred in the result and much of the reasoning, with qualifications; Russell dissented. Assigned: Civil Procedure, physical PDF page 475, printed page 925, deposition by written questions.

## Facts and procedural posture

Cynthia Watson's premature infant Trevor received transfusions in 1985, tested positive for HIV in 1986, developed AIDS, and died in 1988. As administrator, she sued the Red Cross and the hospital for wrongful death. Of the six donors associated with the transfusions, only one was identified as possibly HIV-positive when donating. One negligence theory challenged the way employees screened that donor. Whether the prescribed screening procedure itself was adequate was an independent theory outside this appeal. 974 F.2d at 483–484, 488 n.12.

The nurses had been deposed but could not remember this donor years later. The Red Cross had supplied nonidentifying information and a redacted questionnaire. Watson requested the donor's identity or access through an intermediary. A magistrate proposed limited questioning through a court-appointed lawyer, and the district court adopted a protective arrangement. The Red Cross obtained a stay and permission for an interlocutory appeal under 28 U.S.C. § 1292(b). The question was whether the particular donor discovery should be prohibited under Rule 26(c) or the federal or South Carolina constitutions. This was neither a final negligence judgment nor unrestricted discovery of the donor's identity. Id. at 483–485.

The appellate proceedings had been held in abeyance pending resolution of federal jurisdiction over cases involving the Red Cross. Following the Supreme Court's charter-jurisdiction decision, the court vacated that abeyance order. The donation date remained disputed: February 27 on the donation card, February 26 in the hospital's inventory. The opinion used February 27 for convenience without deciding the factual dispute. Id. at 483–484 nn.1–2.

## Holding and governing approach

The court **affirmed the limited discovery order**. Under the discovery rules as then worded, relevant nonprivileged information could be obtained subject to protective measures against embarrassment, oppression, and undue burdens. The court separately reviewed factual findings for clear error, legal conclusions de novo, and the actual exercise of discovery discretion for abuse. Labeling all objections legal did not make every issue subject to de novo review. Id. at 485.

These nonparty written questions were properly treated as Rule 31 depositions, despite the lower court's use of the term interrogatories. Rule 31 as modified by a Rule 26(c) protective order supplied authority. They were not Rule 33 interrogatories addressed to a party. Id. at 484 n.3, 489 n.14.

## Reasoning

**Blood supply.** The district court had rejected the factual premise that this confidential discovery would dangerously reduce voluntary donations. Competing experts and courts expressed different predictions, but the Red Cross's expert provided no empirical support beyond a theory of behavior, while Watson's expert predicted no measurable adverse effect and possible improvements in safety. The appellate court found no clear error in rejecting that premise. It therefore did not balance Watson's need against a proven national blood-supply danger. It also rejected the argument that threatened litigation would rationally encourage volunteer donors to lie during screening; its reasoning expressly distinguished paid donors' incentives. Id. at 485–487 & nn.5–8.

**Privacy.** Hall accepted the Red Cross's standing to assert the donor's constitutional interests. He concluded both that the tightly confined disclosure did not violate those interests and, alternatively, that any minimal intrusion was outweighed by Watson's need and the public interest in compensation for injuries. The identity would reach only the court and the appointed lawyer, with sealed records and redacted verifications. The possibility of accidental publicity was insufficient in that setting. Embarrassment from answering the questions was distinguished from publicity of medical information. Broader disclosure was reserved; the Red Cross argued no broader privacy protection under the South Carolina Constitution than under the federal Constitution. Id. at 487–488 & nn.9–11.

**Need and relevance.** The donor was the one person who might remember what happened during screening. Questions about disease-risk knowledge, past deferrals, motivation, literacy, and education could illuminate the adequacy of the employees' actual screening. For example, inability to read could make oral explanation necessary. The court found no abuse of discretion in approving these questions under the historical discovery standard. It did not decide negligence or establish a categorical right to obtain every donor's medical history. Id. at 488–489.

## The actual protective order

The appendix reproduces the district court's order, signed February 6 and entered February 7, 1991. It required the Red Cross to deliver the donor's name and address privately to the judge within ten days. The clerk would seal the information, with opening prohibited absent express court approval. A lawyer appointed **at the plaintiff's expense**, potentially taxable later if applicable, would represent the donor; that lawyer's identity would also remain confidential. Approved questions would pass through counsel. Verified answers would be sealed, and the parties would receive copies with the verification redacted. Further dissemination required a court order. Id. at 489–492.

All eighteen questions and their subparts were read. They addressed lifetime donations; awareness that blood transmits disease; perceived risks; screening questions; suspension or rejection of prior donations; concern about HIV; the information supplied by staff; literature and the donor card; discussion of travel; the remembered date; literacy, education, and birth date; privacy during screening; motivations for donating; and prior incarceration. The order allowed Watson to **seek leave** for one follow-up set and required completion of donor discovery within forty-five days. Leave was not automatically granted. Id. at 490–492.

## Separate writings

**Widener, concurring:** He agreed with the result and much of Hall's opinion but rejected third-party constitutional standing, noting that the standing question had not been briefed. He also rejected any implication that a donor who transmits disease possesses a privacy right blocking disclosure about the disease or handling of the blood. The Rule 26 limitations already afforded at least the protection warranted; the extent of any such right need not be decided. His reasoning must not be substituted for Hall's approach. Id. at 492.

**Russell, dissenting:** He would prohibit discovery identifying the donor even to intermediaries. He regarded deterrence of donations as a common-sense risk that should not require a statistical experiment exposing donors to litigation. Judicial and legal staff, communications, and meetings could indirectly reveal the donor's involvement to others. Given the questionnaire, other donor information, and nurse depositions already available, the incremental discovery did not justify the risks to privacy and the blood supply. He also disputed that improved testing eliminated future analogous cases. Id. at 492–493.

## Disposition, textbook comparison, and verification

**Affirmed:** this protected discovery could proceed. No damages, negligence determination, or unrestricted public identification resulted. The textbook accurately presents selected questions as an example of written depositions and raises editorial questions about oral depositions and Rule 33. It omits the full protocol and separate writings. Its pinpoint to page 490 covers the appendix's opening questions; questions 9 and 13 continue on page 491. Its wording that the Red Cross could object is best read as describing submission and judicial approval, rather than an independent veto after approval.
