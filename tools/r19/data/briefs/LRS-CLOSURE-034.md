# Southern California District Council of Laborers v. Ordman
318 F. Supp. 633 (C.D. Cal. 1970) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Decision and procedural posture

On September 22, 1970, in an opinion corrected November 9, District Judge Harry Pregerson granted the labor organizations’ summary-judgment motion and denied the NLRB General Counsel’s motion. He declared that the six-month limitations period for the challenged collective-bargaining arrangement did not begin before employees existed who would be affected by it. The General Counsel therefore could not refuse to consider the charges on the asserted time-bar ground. The court expressly declined to compel issuance of an unfair-labor-practice complaint or to supervise discretionary enforcement decisions. This is a district-court judgment, not an appellate adoption of a general exception to prosecutorial discretion. (633–634, 636–637.)

## Facts and agency proceedings

The Southern California District Council and affiliated Local 1184 filed charges on January 2, 1970, asserting that Christiana Western Structures violated NLRA §§ 8(a)(1), (2), and (3). The company had signed a collective-bargaining agreement containing a union-security clause with United Furniture Workers Local 1010, which did not represent the employees covered by the arrangement. At signing, however, Christiana had no employees who would be subject to the agreement. Employees were hired later, and the charges were filed within six months of that hiring. The absence of employees at execution was conceded. (634–636.)

On January 27, the regional director dismissed because the charges had not been filed and served within six months of signing. General Counsel Ordman sustained that determination. The labor organizations then sought a declaration that the limitations period did not begin until employees were subject to the agreement and an order directing consideration of the charges on their merits. They moved for judgment on the pleadings and summary judgment; Ordman moved for summary judgment. The court heard argument on August 24. (634.)

## Limitations issue and competing positions

Ordman relied on *Local Lodge No. 1424 v. NLRB*, involving an agreement signed with a union lacking majority support among existing employees. In that case, the Board had treated continued maintenance of the agreement as a continuing violation despite the age of its execution. The Supreme Court rejected that approach because it could expose agreements and renewals to challenge throughout their lives, effectively eliminating the statutory protection. (634–635.)

Pregerson distinguished the critical fact: the employer in *Local Lodge* already had covered employees when it signed. They could immediately challenge the agreement and enjoy the full six months Congress allowed. Christiana’s arrangement initially affected no employees. No one had yet lost employee rights through it, and its existence might not be known outside the contracting parties. The court questioned whether execution alone could constitute the asserted unfair labor practice in that setting. (635.)

Starting the clock at signing could allow an employer to insulate a handpicked-union arrangement merely by waiting six months before hiring. Even hiring within six months would deprive employees of part of their opportunity to complain. That was different from permitting unlimited continuing challenges to an agreement already affecting workers. To avoid extinguishing statutory rights before anyone could exercise them, the court held that this period began when employees were hired who would be affected. These timely charges were not barred. (635–636.)

## Limited jurisdiction over the legal barrier

Ordman also challenged subject-matter jurisdiction. The court accepted the ordinary rule that it could not order the General Counsel to issue a complaint and would not review the daily exercise of enforcement discretion committed to his expertise. If he considered the merits and decided not to issue a complaint, this decision did not provide judicial review of that discretionary choice. (636.)

The court characterized this case differently: an incorrect legal construction had prevented consideration at all. Interpreting the limitations statute was a legal question rather than an administrative-policy judgment requiring specialized enforcement expertise. Correcting it was necessary to prevent the same erroneous barrier from foreclosing investigation of otherwise valid charges. Pregerson called the undisputed circumstances extreme and invoked precedents allowing limited intervention to prevent the obliteration of congressionally created rights. The sole judicial footnote supplied that jurisdictional rationale through *Switchmen’s Union*. (636 & n. 1.)

## Exact relief and assigned scope

The decree granted plaintiffs’ summary judgment, denied defendant’s summary judgment, and denied the judgment-on-the-pleadings motion after concluding that the summary ruling made its consideration unnecessary. It declared that the period did not commence until employees were affected and that § 10(b) did not bar consideration here. The opinion stated that no reason appeared why the General Counsel should not consider the merits. It did not order issuance of a complaint, decide the underlying violations, or impose liability on the employer. (636–637.)
