# Farnum v. Silvano
27 Mass. App. Ct. 536, 540 N.E.2d 202 (1989) | Contracts

## Decision and procedural setting

On June 30, 1989, the Massachusetts Appeals Court vacated the Probate Court's judgment upholding a house conveyance and remanded for a judgment of rescission with appropriate restitutionary accounting. Judge Kass wrote for a panel with Judges Warner and Fine; no separate writing appears. The court accepted the supported trial findings rather than retrying disputed facts, but held that the judge had applied an insufficient measure of contractual capacity. A moment in which the seller understood that a closing was taking place did not establish reasonable understanding of the transaction and its consequences. The buyer's knowledge of her impaired ability to act reasonably was decisive under Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 15(1)(b). (536, 538–541.)

## Sale, impaired condition, and the trial ruling

Viola Farnum was ninety when she conveyed her South Yarmouth house to twenty-four-year-old Joseph Silvano on July 14, 1986. He had mowed her lawn and performed other landscaping, and she trusted him. The purchase price was $64,900 for property appraised at $115,000; the agreement also included furniture and furnishings. At the closing Silvano obtained a $65,000 bank mortgage. Her nephew Harry Gove had warned him not to proceed because of the price's inadequacy; the court later emphasized that the warning also concerned her mental disability. Gove, subsequently her guardian, pursued rescission on her behalf in the action commenced April 6, 1987. (536–537, 540 & n.1.)

Farnum's competence had seriously declined since 1983. She wondered why deceased sisters did not contact her and where people in a nonexistent upstairs had gone. She had offered the house to a neighbor for $35,000; he declined because it was worth substantially more. She repeatedly locked herself out and broke in despite having left a key with a neighbor, concealed her cat from imagined police interest, expressed a wish to return to Cape Cod while already there, and became lost. Her sister and nephew helped pay bills and balance her checkbook. A May 1985 scan disclosed organic brain disease. Following several hospitalizations during the preceding three years, she was hospitalized for dementia and seizure disorder in January 1987 and then discharged to a nursing home. (537.)

Silvano selected and paid the lawyer who represented Farnum in preparing the deed and completing the conveyance. That lawyer and the bank's lawyer attended the closing in her home. She was cheerful, exchanged pleasantries, made coffee, and appeared aware of the event. The trial judge found that she could be coherent and then confused two minutes later, but was coherent or in a lucid interval when signing. After the closing, however, she told her sister and nephew she still owned the house. The Probate Court treated the lucid interval as establishing sufficient capacity and ruled for Silvano. (537–538.)

## Contractual capacity and knowledge of impairment

The appellate court distinguished testamentary capacity from contractual capacity. A person with a pathologically unsound mind may possess the ability to execute a will at a particular moment. A donor's choice among beneficiaries can also be arbitrary without defeating the disposition. A contract creates reciprocal obligations and requires reasonable comprehension of its nature, significance, and consequences, particularly where mental impairment is manifest. Transient awareness of the event is therefore insufficient. (538.)

The court invoked the two branches of Restatement § 15(1): inability, by reason of mental illness or defect, to understand the transaction's nature and consequences reasonably, or inability to act reasonably in relation to it where the other party has reason to know of the condition. It used Ortelere to show the difference between knowing that a selection or transfer is occurring and comprehending its consequences in the person's actual family and financial circumstances. In this case Farnum could know she was selling below value without understanding why disposing of a major asset so cheaply was unreasonable when rent, home-care, and nursing-home costs were approaching. (538–539.)

The supported findings established the missing contextual understanding. Her mental disease appeared in irrational conduct and was confirmed diagnostically; her physician did not regard her as competent to live alone, and her condition soon required hospitalization. Silvano was neither family nor someone who had cared for her over a long period. His asserted additional consideration—letting her remain in the house for some time—was unpersuasive because both the sale agreement and deed were silent on such an arrangement. The lawyer he procured concentrated on effecting the transaction, rather than serving as someone familiar with Farnum who made her overall interests the primary concern. In the face of growing support costs, the cut-rate transfer was irrational for her. (539–540.)

The decisive factor was Silvano's awareness of her inability to act reasonably. Her relatives, neighbors, and physician were concerned by conduct he knew or had reason to know. He knew the price was approximately half the home's value and had received the nephew's specific warning about unfairness and disability. The court thus held the deed voidable under § 15(1)(b), without resolving the alternative theories of fraud, undue influence, or constructive trust. The result did not establish that every unfavorable bargain by an elderly seller is invalid: it rested on this established mental impairment, this transaction's consequences, and this buyer's knowledge. (540.)

## Rescission and remaining accounting

Silvano was required to convey the property to Farnum's guardian in that capacity, in return for the consideration he had paid. The trial court was to restore the pretransaction position as fully as practicable. It should account for possession or rental value from July 14, 1986 through entry of revised judgment, and for benefits Farnum received, such as real-estate taxes Silvano paid. (540–541.)

Ordinarily Silvano's unrequested improvements and mortgage payments should not receive allowance. The court nevertheless cited an equitable exception permitting improvement credits when a particular case warrants them. It also observed that cases generally did not require interest on the returned price, while expressly declining to rule that such interest exceeded the trial judge's discretion in framing a fair restoration. Those details were not fixed dollar awards. The exact mandate vacated the judgment and remanded to the Probate Court for a rescission judgment consistent with these directions. (541.)
