Alaska Packers’ Ass’n v. Domenico
Citation
Alaska Packers’ Ass’n v. Domenico, 117 F. 99 (9th Cir. 1902), decided May 26, 1902. Judge Ross wrote the
opinion for a panel consisting of Judges Gilbert, Ross, and Hawley. Assigned excerpt: Farnsworth et al.,
Contracts: Cases and Materials, 10th ed. (C&M), pp. 470–473. This brief follows the assigned excerpt and
identifies additional facts from the full judicial opinion. The issue is whether a promise of higher pay for the
same already-promised services has consideration when obtained by a strategically timed refusal to work.
Parties
Alaska Packers’ Association operated a salmon-fishing and canning enterprise in Alaska. It was the
respondent in the workers’ admiralty action and the appellant in the Ninth Circuit. Domenico and the other
workers were the libelants, the admiralty term then used for plaintiffs, and the appellees. They sought the
additional compensation allegedly promised in Alaska; the Association contended that the new promise was
unenforceable and that it had paid what the original agreements required. C&M pp. 470–471; 117 F. at
99–105.
Material Facts
The workers agreed in San Francisco to sail to Pyramid Harbor, Alaska, and perform work as sailors and
fishermen. Their obligations included ordinary ship duties, loading and unloading, and other work requested
by the captain or the Association’s agent. The casebook’s introductory summary states compensation of $50
for the season plus two cents for each red salmon in whose catching a worker participated. The full opinion
additionally describes later shipping articles specifying $60 plus the same per-fish amount. The legally
important point is that the workers had binding agreements fixing their compensation before demanding $100
for the same season’s services. C&M p. 470; 117 F. at 99–102.
The Association had approximately $150,000 invested in its cannery. Fishing operations depended on a short
season in a remote location. After beginning their work, the workers stopped together on May 19 and
demanded $100 for their services. They threatened to stop altogether and return to San Francisco unless the
demand was met. The superintendent could not obtain replacement workers in the available time. Losing the
crew at that point threatened the operation of the enterprise, not merely the inconvenience of hiring someone
else. C&M pp. 470–471; 117 F. at 99–102.
The superintendent initially tried to persuade them to resume. The full opinion describes an agreement
prepared on May 22 that used the earlier terms with the compensation increased. The superintendent
expressly told the workers that he lacked authority to change their original contracts. The workers
nevertheless relied on the resulting promise and completed the work. When they returned to San Francisco,
the Association refused to recognize the increased compensation. The full opinion also describes payments
and releases under the original arrangements; the appellate court did not rest its decision on an independent
determination that those releases defeated the claims. C&M pp. 470–471; 117 F. at 99–105.
At trial, the workers tried to justify their demand by showing that the supplied fishing nets were rotten or
otherwise unusable. That allegation mattered because compensation partly depended on their catch, and
deficient equipment could have supported an argument that the employer had not supplied what the
performance required. The trial court, however, found the claim unsupported after hearing conflicting
evidence. It reasoned that the Association’s investment and its own dependence on the catch made provision
of unusable nets improbable. The Ninth Circuit accepted that factual finding. The alleged defect must

Procedural History
The workers sued in admiralty to recover the additional wages. The lower court entered judgment for them,
notwithstanding its finding against their defective-net explanation. Its decision was reported at 112 F. 554.
The Association appealed. The Ninth Circuit treated the material remaining issue as one of law: assuming
that the superintendent had authority and that the alleged new agreement was made, was that agreement
supported by consideration? It found this question sufficient to resolve the appeal and did not decide the
authority issue. C&M pp. 470–471; 117 F. at 99–105.
Issue
Does a promise to increase compensation become enforceable when workers, already bound to provide the
same services for a stated wage, refuse to continue without an increase at a time when the employer cannot
replace them? Can the employer’s agreement under those circumstances be treated as a voluntary rescission,
waiver, or basis for estoppel, particularly after the workers complete the promised work? C&M pp. 471–473.
Rule and Holding
A promise to perform the very services that a person is already legally bound to perform for the promisor
supplies no new consideration for an additional promise of payment. On the accepted facts, the workers
offered nothing beyond the performance already owed. Their increased-wage agreement therefore lacked
consideration. The court also rejected the contention that the circumstances established a voluntary
abandonment of the employer’s original rights or an estoppel preventing the employer from asserting the
defect. Completion of the existing duty did not cure it. C&M pp. 471–473; 117 F. at 102–105.
The holding concerns the pre-existing duty rule applied to this transaction. The coercive setting explains the
court’s refusal to recognize a voluntary replacement bargain, but the operative ground of decision was lack of
consideration. The opinion should not be restated as an application of the later Restatement §§ 175–176 or as
a holding that every requested wage increase is economic duress.
Judgment
The Ninth Circuit reversed and remanded with directions to enter judgment for the Association, the
respondent below, with costs. The workers could not recover the additional compensation under the alleged
modification. The casebook ends with reversal; the full opinion supplies the remand directions. 117 F. at 105.
Reasoning
The court began with the original obligation. The workers had knowingly undertaken to provide the relevant
services in Alaska for specified compensation. The later promise called for exactly those services, not
additional work, a longer season, new responsibilities, or surrender of a genuinely disputed claim. The
practical value of obtaining the performance did not change what was already legally owed. Under the rule
the court applied, the employer’s avoidance of disruption was not a new bargained-for legal benefit sufficient
to support the increase. C&M pp. 471–473.
The factual finding about the nets removed the workers’ asserted justification for stopping. The appellate
court declined to retry that contested factual issue because the trial judge had seen and heard the witnesses.
Its consideration analysis consequently proceeded on the premise that the refusal to perform lacked a valid

Timing and location supplied the court’s explanation of the bargain. The workers demanded the increase after
the Association had committed its capital and after replacement labor had become unavailable. Their
leverage arose from the Association’s dependence on their promised performance during a narrow operating
season. The court viewed the demand as exploiting that dependence rather than as a fresh exchange the
parties freely chose. Those facts helped explain why the court refused to infer voluntary rescission or waiver
from the superintendent’s apparent assent. C&M pp. 471–472.
The court assumed authority for purposes of the consideration question without finding that authority existed.
The superintendent’s express warning that he could not alter the original contract also undermined an
inference that the Association had knowingly and voluntarily relinquished its rights. The Association itself
did not learn of the breach until the expedition returned. The court therefore had no basis, on its view of the
record, to say that the employer freely excused the breach and substituted a new obligation. C&M p. 471.
The court’s discussion of King v. Railway Co. rejected estoppel based on completion of the work. A person
who obtains an additional promise through an unjustifiable refusal to do what is already owed cannot create a
protected reliance claim merely by eventually doing that work. Otherwise the party’s own breach would
become the foundation for preventing the other party from raising lack of consideration. The same conduct
that made the promise coercive also defeated the claim that its recipient should enforce it as a matter of
fairness. C&M p. 472.
The quoted reasoning of Lingenfelder v. Brewing Co. illustrated the same point with an architect demanding
extra compensation for already-promised services. Changing the label to a new contract did not identify new
performance. Enforcing the additional promise simply because the promisee had acquired situational
leverage would reward the refusal to honor the original agreement. The court treated that concern as
supporting the traditional rule, not as permission to rewrite the parties’ compensation whenever a court
considered the original bargain inadequate. C&M pp. 472–473.
There are thus two connected but distinct steps. The court first identified the unchanged legal duty, which
defeated consideration. It then rejected routes around that result because the record did not support voluntary
release, legitimate additional performance, or reliance deserving protection. The case’s force lies in that
combination; describing it only as a rule against pay increases loses the role of the accepted facts.
Separate Opinions
The reported appellate decision contains no separate concurrence or dissent. The quoted passages from other
courts are authorities used by Judge Ross, not separate opinions in this case.
Notes
Comparison with the assigned reforms. Restatement § 89 supplies possible grounds for enforcing some
modifications without new consideration, but those grounds must actually be established. A demand based
only on replacement difficulty does not itself demonstrate an unanticipated circumstance justifying a fair
adjustment. Section 89(c) likewise does not make every performance of an existing duty sufficient reliance to
enforce an opportunistic demand. These are applications of the later provisions, not holdings of Alaska
Packers. C&M pp. 473–477; Restatement § 89 & cmts. b, d.
