# Undersea Engineering & Construction Co. v. International Telephone & Telegraph Corp.
429 F.2d 543 (9th Cir. 1970) | Contracts

## Appellate court, adopted decision, and disposition
The Ninth Circuit's report gives June 18–22, 1970, and notes denial of rehearing on July 21. Judges Jertberg, Browning, and Hufstedler issued a per curiam opinion affirming summary judgment and the appealed disqualification and sanctions orders. It expressly adopted the district court's reasoning and reproduced that entire decision as an appendix. District Judge Martin Pence signed the underlying decision in Hawaii on August 26, 1968. The merits analysis below is therefore the district court's reasoning adopted on appeal, not a separately authored appellate reconstruction. There are no separate writings. (543–546, 552.)

The appellate court found no genuine dispute of material fact and held the defendants entitled to judgment as a matter of law. It also held the judicial-bias affidavit legally insufficient: filing an affidavit did not automatically disqualify a judge, who could determine its legal sufficiency. The hearing revealed frivolous, if not groundless and vexatious, grounds, and the costs and attorney-fee sanction lay within the district court's discretion. (544–545.)

## Project and the claims that preceded settlement
ITT held a Navy contract to install an offshore hydrophone system near Kauai. On June 10, 1966 it subcontracted work to Undersea; Shelton Engineering was Undersea's sub-subcontractor. Disputes developed over support services, delayed work, price increases, modifications, and performance quality. A different subcontractor, Healy-Tibbitts, operated a barge implicated in damage to a mooring installation built by Undersea. (546–547.)

The support-services dispute concerned whether ITT had promised to use Undersea for all needed vessels, divers, and related services, or could request only services it chose to use. Undersea claimed overhead and lost profit for services not used. A cable-laying vessel expected in August 1966 did not arrive until February 1967 for reasons outside ITT's control; Undersea sought standby charges and increased compensation because work shifted from calm summer weather to winter conditions. ITT rejected those demands. The parties also disputed a negotiated modification reducing the fixed-price portion to $170,000. Undersea acknowledged the negotiation but later conditioned acceptance on recognition of its other claims. ITT treated the modification as effective and asserted defective-work claims. These were disputed, unliquidated demands rather than established liquidated debts. (547.)

Undersea chose continued performance and negotiation while threatening litigation. Its vice president David Glickman, a California lawyer, demanded payment, threatened punitive damages, copied Navy officials, and disputed ITT's claimed setoffs. An August 1967 letter threatened approximately $200,000 in compensatory and $100 million in punitive damages, worldwide distribution of legal papers, and complaints to federal agencies. It nevertheless offered settlement for $108,000. ITT asserted that it already had paid $143,305 on the fixed-price work and listed substantial counterclaims. (548.)

## Release and later suit
In September 1967 ITT offered $40,000 to settle claims separate from the fixed-price portion and an additional $5,000 for that portion, conditioned on a release of all claims connected with the subcontract. Undersea accepted by cable and confirmed its release by an October 3 letter. ITT mailed the $45,000 check on October 5 with settlement language, and the check's endorsement identified final payment and release of further liability. These documents and their chronology were uncontested. (549.)

Undersea and Shelton sued in November 1967. Undersea asserted contract and alternative unjust-enrichment claims for support services and underwater work, a mooring-damage tort claim, and economic coercion to avoid the settlement. Shelton sought $88,290.15 in quasi-contract against ITT for work, materials, and advances furnished to Undersea. The court concluded that the unambiguous release encompassed Undersea's contract, restitution, and tort demands arising in connection with the subcontract, without resort to extrinsic evidence about scope. That made the asserted coercion the central route to avoiding the release. (546, 549.)

## Economic duress and the available alternative
Undersea alleged that ITT deliberately withheld payment, forced it into financial distress, and exploited impending bankruptcy to secure a cheap settlement. The court required facts showing involuntary acceptance, no reasonably available alternative, and circumstances resulting from ITT's coercive conduct. Assertions of financial difficulty or reluctance alone did not establish those elements. The record instead showed Undersea initiating negotiations, pressing its demands for almost six months, and retaining litigation as an available course. The court regarded settlement as its chosen alternative to suit, not its only reasonable choice. (549–551.)

The court rejected a proposed inference of coercion merely from relative size: ITT's multinational resources and Undersea's $25,000 capitalization did not make refusal of disputed claims wrongful by themselves. Even assuming bankruptcy motivated acceptance, the claimant had to connect that pressure to wrongful and oppressive acts of the opponent rather than its own necessities. The claims and counterclaims had remained unliquidated and disputed for months. The court also found an alleged isolated statement that ITT would break Undersea financially insufficient in the documented context. It did not find that every threat or financial disparity is harmless; it found that the undisputed record lacked facts necessary to establish duress here. (550–551.)

The assignment's observation that Undersea itself applied extensive pressure is accurate, but not the complete legal test. Its conduct supported the court's assessment of voluntary negotiation and available litigation. The release remained binding, and Undersea's connected claims against ITT were barred. (549–551.)

## Mooring damage and Shelton's distinct restitution claim
The adopted decision gave two grounds for rejecting the claim against Healy-Tibbitts. It discussed the then-applied joint-tortfeasor release rule and separately found no damages: Undersea had received the full $28,000 subcontract price for the mooring, and ITT replaced it at its own expense after discovering the damage resulted from Undersea's faulty construction. Note 6 expressly declined to accept the plaintiffs' characterization of the action as properly in admiralty; diversity furnished jurisdiction. The historical release discussion should not be transformed into a certification of current tort law. (551 & nn. 6–7.)

Shelton's unjust-enrichment theory ignored ITT's admitted total payment of about $247,000 to Undersea. It did not negate the inference that its work was included in work for which Undersea already collected payment. Independently, Shelton had an express subcontract with Undersea and no alleged contractual dealings with ITT. In the absence of legislation specifically permitting recovery, the court would not impose an implied payment obligation on the prime contractor merely because it benefited from the work. Note 8 mentions statutory mechanisms by comparison; Shelton established no such statutory claim. Its possible claim lay against Undersea. (551–552 & nn. 8–9.)

## Final result and source scope
The defendants received summary judgment on all claims. Because the settlement was intended to dispose of both claims and counterclaims, the district court also dismissed the defendants' counterclaims. The appellate court affirmed that judgment and all appealed orders, including rejection of disqualification and the related sanction. (545, 552.)
