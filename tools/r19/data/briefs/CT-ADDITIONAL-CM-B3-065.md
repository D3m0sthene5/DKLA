# Printing Center of Texas, Inc. v. Supermind Publishing Co., Inc.
669 S.W.2d 779 (Tex. App.—Houston [14th Dist.] 1984) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
Justice Cannon wrote for the Houston Fourteenth Court of Appeals, on a panel with Chief Justice J. Curtiss Brown and Justice Draughn, on March 15, 1984. The reporter notes rehearing denied April 19, 1984. The court affirmed a judgment returning the publisher’s $2,900 deposit and awarding attorneys’ fees after a jury found that the delivered books did not conform to the printing contract. No dissent or concurrence appears. The decision applied Article 2 on the parties’ assumed theory despite expressly doubting that this predominantly service transaction belonged under the UCC. (779, 782–786.)

## Transaction, evidence, and litigation
Supermind Publishing contracted with Printing Center for 5,000 copies of “Supermind Supermemory.” The July 31, 1981 bid proposal specified essential terms including quantity, trim size, paper, and cover. It called for thirty-pound white newsprint. A publisher witness testified that the printer showed a white sample but delivered books with gray pages. Other evidence concerned off-center cover art, crooked and wrinkled pages, and inadequate perforation of a pull-out page. The printer knew that the books were intended for public sale. (782, 784–785.)

The publisher rejected the delivered books and sought cancellation and a refund under UCC §§ 2.601 and 2.711. A jury found that the books failed in some respect to conform to the contract. The county court at law awarded the deposit, $3,000 in reasonable attorneys’ fees, and a combined $1,500 in additional fees through Supreme Court proceedings. The printer challenged the attorney-billing evidence, the support for the nonconformity finding, and the court’s authority to enter a judgment beyond its $5,000 jurisdictional limit. (782, 785–786.)

## Threshold qualification: Article 2 was assumed, not established
The court first observed that printing involved both goods—paper and ink—and services such as binding, typesetting, and proofing. Under the dominant-factor test, services appeared to be the essence of this transaction. If the common law governed, the jury’s finding of any nonconformity would not itself establish a right to rejection and refund under the UCC; the court identified damages as the common-law remedy and questioned the judgment’s support. But the printer had not assigned error to whether the verdict supported the judgment. That issue was waived. The court therefore proceeded on its expressly doubtful assumption that Article 2 applied so it could address the errors actually raised. The affirmance does not establish that every book-printing contract is a sale of goods. (782–783.)

## Conformity depends on the actual bargain
On the assumed UCC theory, perfect tender meant conformity to the contract in every respect, not abstract flawlessness. Substantial compliance was insufficient under § 2.601; the court rejected a contrary jury-submission holding in Del Monte Corp. v. Martin. Determining conformity required identifying the actual agreement, including express language and terms arising from course of dealing, usage of trade, or course of performance. Trade usage could explain or supplement, but not contradict, an express term, and its existence and scope required proof. (783–784.)

The white sample supplied evidence of an express warranty under § 2.313(a)(3) that the delivered paper would conform to that sample. The gray pages could therefore support the jury’s finding. The written proposal did not expressly address the other physical defects, and trade-usage evidence had not been supplied to supplement it. Nevertheless, the known purpose of public sale supported a requirement that the books be commercially acceptable. Under § 2.314’s merchantability standard, books had to pass without objection in the trade and be fit for ordinary use. The jury could reasonably regard crooked and wrinkled pages, off-center artwork, and deficient perforation as making the books unfit for sale. These grounds supplied sufficient support for nonconformity. (784–785.)

## Good faith qualifies rejection, but the printer did not establish bad faith
The court also explained that § 2.601 operated within the Code’s duty of good faith. Rejection was an act of performance, and a merchant’s good faith included honesty in fact and observance of reasonable commercial standards of fair dealing. A seller asserting bad-faith rejection bore the burden. Evidence that a buyer seized a minor defect in a falling market to escape an unwanted bargain might support bad faith; the court cited Neumiller Farms v. Cornett as an illustration. That hypothetical did not describe a proved falling market in this case. (784.)

Printing Center argued that the defects were minor and that Supermind acted in bad faith. The court did not regard the asserted defects as minor and found no evidence that the publisher’s primary motivation was escape from a bad bargain. The printer had also failed to request a jury issue on this defense, without conclusively establishing it by the evidence, and therefore waived it under the applicable procedural rule. The textbook labels the general good-faith illustration dictum; the opinion’s case-specific result additionally rests on evidentiary failure and failure to obtain a jury finding. (785.)

## Attorneys’ fees evidence and jurisdiction
The publisher introduced its lawyer’s billing letters through legal secretary Patricia Burrows as records custodian. She lacked personal knowledge of the listed work and had not been custodian when the bills were made. Those facts did not disqualify her under the then-applicable business-records statute: lack of personal knowledge affected weight and credibility rather than admissibility, and the statute did not require custody at the time of creation. The printer’s separate appellate argument that the bills lacked the foundation required for summaries of original records was not preserved by a sufficiently specific trial objection. The court therefore did not adjudicate that foundation argument’s merits. (783.)

Attorneys’ fees, including appellate fees, counted toward the jurisdictional amount. Nevertheless, jurisdiction was acquired through the original petition’s good-faith allegations seeking the $2,900 deposit, interest, and reasonable fees; that petition did not clearly demand an amount beyond the court’s limit. A later amendment requesting further fees accruing through prosecution of the suit did not divest jurisdiction already acquired. The printer’s assertion that the original jurisdictional allegations were fraudulent was waived because it had not pleaded and proved that fraud in the trial court. Accordingly, the judgment was not void on the grounds raised, even though the amended demand and ultimate award exceeded $5,000. (785–786.)

## Disposition and assigned significance
All asserted points were overruled, and the trial judgment was affirmed. The assigned account on printed pages 837–838 accurately uses the case to show the interaction of contractual conformity and good faith, and expressly preserves the court’s doubt about Article 2. The complete opinion additionally explains the dominant-factor issue’s waiver, the verdict’s support through sample warranty and merchantability, the waived bad-faith jury issue, and the fee and jurisdiction rulings. Its section numbers are the historical versions cited in 1984; the textbook’s bracketed replacements are editorial updates rather than words of the court.
