# World Duty Free Company Limited v. Republic of Kenya
ICSID Case No. ARB/00/7, Award of October 4, 2006, 46 I.L.M. 339 (2007) | Contracts

## Tribunal and decision
The ICSID tribunal comprised President Gilbert Guillaume, Andrew Rogers, QC, and V. V. Veeder, QC. All three signed the award; no separate writing is reproduced. James Crawford had initially been appointed but resigned in January 2001 after the claimant raised his relationship with Kenya’s law firm, and Veeder replaced him. The final award dismissed the company’s claim, required each side to bear its own legal costs, and divided arbitration costs equally. Former President Daniel arap Moi was not a party, was not legally represented, and was not heard as a witness; the tribunal expressly had no jurisdiction over him. This was not a criminal conviction of Moi or an award of damages against him. (Award ¶¶ 3, 6–11, 188–192.)

## Investment agreement and parties
On April 27, 1989, Kenya agreed with House of Perfume to construct, maintain, and operate duty-free complexes at Nairobi and Mombasa airports. The term was ten years with an option for another ten on the same conditions except renegotiated rent. The company owed $1 million annually for the two complexes. A May 11, 1990 amendment substituted World Duty Free, an Isle of Man company. Nasir Ibrahim Ali signed for the companies and managed the project; subsequent ownership changes left him with direct and indirect share interests. A lease signed August 25, 1995 by the Kenya Airports Authority acting for the government ran for ten years from July 1, 1990 on the agreement’s terms. These were related instruments, not a single newly executed contract in 1995. (Id. ¶¶ 62–65, 126.)

Article 9 consented to ICSID arbitration of disputes arising from the agreement or relating to investment under it and directed the tribunal to apply English law. Article 10(A), however, said Kenyan law governed construction of the agreement. The tribunal acknowledged the awkward combination but found no material difference on the particular issues: Kenyan contract law incorporated English common law subject to local modifications, and both sides accepted the relevant principles. The decision therefore did not need to choose one legal system to the complete exclusion of the other. (Id. ¶¶ 6, 158–165.)

## Alleged interference and requested relief
The company alleged that it had spent approximately $27 million developing the airport facilities, which were officially opened in 1990. It claimed that later political and private actors involved it without consent in false Goldenberg gold-export documentation, seized control and assets through forged ownership claims and court-appointed receivers, obstructed legal redress, and caused Ali’s unlawful arrest and deportation. It also alleged incorporation of a duplicate company and diversion of its lease. It attributed these acts to Kenya and sought restoration of the complexes with associated financial relief, or alternative compensation around $500 million, including lost profits and exemplary damages. These were the claimant’s allegations, not findings that every alleged expropriation, forgery, or prosecution irregularity occurred. (Id. ¶¶ 67–79, 88–93.)

Kenya denied contractual breaches and damage. Among other defenses, it said the ownership fight was a private dispute, challenged the attribution of Pattni’s conduct to the government, asserted limitation and rent-default issues, and disputed the claimant’s explanation of Ali’s deportation. The tribunal’s dispositive inquiry ultimately concerned the procurement bribe and enforceability, rather than deciding those competing accounts of subsequent breach and quantum. All pleaded claims depended on the investment agreement; no independent noncontractual proprietary or restitutionary claim remained before it. (Id. ¶¶ 80–87, 179, 186.)

## Procedural posture and threshold issues
The claimant commenced ICSID proceedings in June 2000. Early disputes concerned its removal and restoration on the Isle of Man companies register, authority of directors versus receivers to conduct the arbitration, and other preliminary objections. A Manx order of April 26, 2002 directed that the company be represented in this arbitration by its directors and their appointed lawyers, rather than the receivers. The tribunal joined remaining preliminary objections to the merits. Its procedural account also distinguished public discussion of the case from unauthorized release of closed-hearing records. (Id. ¶¶ 4–5, 9–40, 94–104; confidentiality decision reproduced at ¶ 16.)

After the claimant disclosed the payment in its December 2002 memorial, Kenya applied in March 2003 to dismiss on illegality grounds and, in its April 18, 2003 counter-memorial, expressly avoided the agreement. Following exchanges and postponements, the tribunal confined a preliminary merits hearing to three issues: whether Kenya was entitled to avoid and had avoided the contract, whether it had lost that right, and whether public policy barred the pleaded claims. Breach and damages were expressly excluded from that hearing. The January 18, 2006 hearing and subsequent submissions preceded the final award. Lord Mustill’s English-law opinion was treated as Kenya’s submitted legal material, not accepted as expert testimony establishing disputed facts. (Id. ¶¶ 41–61, 105–124, 163–164.)

## Payment and finding of bribery
Ali’s own written statement and hearing evidence supplied the decisive payment facts. While seeking permissions for the investment, he consulted intermediary Rashid Sajjad, who arranged an audience with Moi and said that a $2 million personal donation in cash was required for doing business with the government. Ali transferred $2 million for that purpose through the intermediary. His account described $500,000, converted into Kenyan shillings, being brought in a briefcase to the president’s residence; after the meeting he found corn in place of the money. He said the payment had appeared to him to be a bribe but that he was told it was lawful and necessary for the contract. He treated it as consideration paid for House of Perfume. The award does not say that the entire $2 million was visibly handed over in that single briefcase. (Id. ¶¶ 130–135.)

Kenya did not deny the payment circumstances; the parties disputed their characterization. The claimant invoked gifts of protocol and the Harambee tradition of collective contributions for public purposes. The tribunal rejected that explanation for concealed payments intended above all to obtain presidential approval for this investment. It found bribery procuring the 1989 agreement, rather than merely paying for an audience. This was a finding on the admitted and proved record, not an inference drawn solely from a legal presumption. (Id. ¶¶ 131–136, 166–167.)

## Transnational public policy
The tribunal distinguished a state’s narrow domestic public policy governing recognition of foreign awards from a genuinely transnational consensus applicable across legal systems. It warned that the latter required objective support rather than an arbitrator’s personal moral judgment. It considered criminal prohibitions, international anticorruption instruments, national decisions, and arbitral precedents. Some conventions postdated the transaction and bound only their parties from relevant dates; their significance was confirmation of an existing condemnation of bribery, not retroactive treaty liability. Kenya’s own anticorruption law already prohibited the relevant conduct in 1989. (Id. ¶¶ 138–147 & nn. 5–12.)

Its comparative arbitral discussion distinguished allegations not proved from corruption actually established. Widespread corrupt practices in a country or industry did not make procurement bribery legally acceptable. The tribunal concluded that contracts for corruption and contracts obtained through corruption could not sustain claims before it. It did not adopt every procedural disposition in every cited arbitration: for example, its discussion of Lagergren’s earlier jurisdictional refusal is precedent analysis, whereas this award independently states its own enforceability result and assumption about the arbitration clause. (Id. ¶¶ 148–157, 187; translations and authorities in nn. 13–20.)

## English and Kenyan public policy
The tribunal separately applied the public-policy principles of the chosen laws. Under its account of then-applicable English common law, the bar to a claim founded on illegality protected the public rather than rewarded the defendant’s virtue. It acknowledged that an illegal transaction could have legal or equitable proprietary consequences where title could be established without reliance on illegality, but no such separately pleaded claim required decision here. (Id. ¶¶ 160–162, 179, 186.)

It rejected an attempt to separate the company from Ali or House of Perfume: Ali was the driving force and alter ego of both successive contracting companies, and the claimant could not obtain a better position through substitution. The bribe was intrinsic to the entire transaction and intended to procure it. Its secrecy and omission from the written agreement did not establish a separate collateral bargain; severing it would disregard the very means by which the agreement had been secured. The tribunal rejected severability on these facts rather than needing to resolve every possible theoretical application of that doctrine. (Id. ¶¶ 167–175.)

The claimant also sought a balancing of comparative misconduct, emphasizing the president’s solicitation and later wrongdoing. The tribunal found no authority in the English law it was applying for the requested discretionary adjustment and said the facts would not favor the claimant even if discretion existed. There was no already-committed investment held hostage when Ali paid: he retained the choice whether to invest and freely chose to bribe. The tribunal acknowledged the disturbing feature of Kenya invoking its former president’s corruption and the apparent absence of recovery or prosecution against him. Its answer was that the public-policy bar protected citizens and would apply equally if a culpable Kenya were the claimant. Those observations do not adjudicate liability for all alleged subsequent state acts. (Id. ¶¶ 176–181.)

## Avoidance, knowledge, and affirmation
Avoidance was an additional contractual ground. A contract procured through bribery of the other party’s agent was voidable at that principal’s option, rather than necessarily void from inception. The principal had to take positive action but could lose the option through informed affirmation. The tribunal found that the payment was first made known to Kenya through the claimant’s December 2002 disclosure and that Kenya unequivocally and promptly avoided in April 2003. A prior contractual breach by the otherwise innocent principal did not itself eliminate the avoidance right. (Id. ¶¶ 163–165, 182–184; n. 22.)

For this agency and waiver analysis, Moi’s corrupt knowledge was not attributed to the state whose interests his conduct violated. The tribunal refused to equate the elected president with Kenya itself. Continued dealings without the relevant knowledge did not establish affirmation, waiver, or estoppel. It noted that consequences could follow avoidance, but they were not pleaded or decided here; returning the bribe was not among them. (Id. ¶¶ 169–172, 183–186.)

## Arbitration clause, final award, and costs
Neither side proved or argued that the bribe specifically procured Article 9’s arbitration agreement. The tribunal therefore operated on the assumption that that clause remained valid and effective for this proceeding and award. It did not dismiss for lack of arbitral jurisdiction merely because the underlying agreement was unenforceable. Its conclusions were that Kenya validly avoided, had not lost that right beforehand, and the claimant could not maintain its pleaded claims under transnational or applicable-law public policy. (Id. ¶¶ 187–188.)

The dispositive paragraph dismisses the claim. Each side bears all its own legal costs and one-half of tribunal and ICSID costs, without recovery from the other. Although Kenya prevailed on its defenses, the tribunal said public policy made ordinary successful-party cost shifting inappropriate. Kenya had requested dismissal with prejudice; the actual dispositive text says dismissed and should not be embellished with additional wording. There was no contractual damages award, restitution order, criminal penalty, or recovery of the bribe. (Id. ¶¶ 189–192.)
