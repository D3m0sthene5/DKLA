# Garcia v. General Motors Corp.

**195 Ariz. 510, 990 P.2d 1069 (Ct. App. 1999)** — May 6, 1999. Judge Berch wrote, with Judges Weisberg and Kleinschmidt concurring; there is no separate writing. Civil Procedure. Assigned: CivPro PDF 676, printed 1342, complete Note 4. The appeal concerns both preclusion and the law governing seatbelt evidence in a products-liability trial.

Arizona church-group members rented a van in Phoenix for a trip to a camp near Walla Walla, Washington. While passing through Idaho in 1993, their Arizona driver lost control; the van left the road and overturned. All passengers were injured. None wore the available functional seatbelts, and most were ejected. The parties disputed whether eight or nine of the eleven passengers were ejected, as note 1 records. Plaintiffs alleged a design defect in the General Motors van; the appellate court did not independently establish that allegation. One passenger, Ilsy Echeverria, promptly sued in Idaho federal court. The judge applied Idaho law and excluded evidence of her failure to wear a seatbelt. The case then settled before trial. (¶¶2–3; 513; n.1.)

The remaining passengers sued General Motors, the rental company, and Phoenix Tabernacle in Arizona in August 1995. They argued that the Idaho ruling barred General Motors from raising the seatbelt defense, and that Idaho law should govern even without preclusion. The Arizona trial court struck the defense and applied Idaho law. A jury returned a verdict against General Motors, and the trial court denied a new trial. General Motors appealed the choice-of-law and evidentiary rulings. The Court of Appeals reviewed the preclusion and choice-of-law issues de novo and reversed. (¶¶4–6, 19, 37.)

The court treated federal law as governing the effect of the prior federal ruling, while saying the outcome would be the same under Arizona law. It did not finally choose between the parties’ descriptions of the attempted preclusion as offensive or defensive. Note 2 explains that Parklane’s federal definition would make this offensive use and raise concerns about waiting on another passenger’s suit, while an Arizona precedent could characterize it differently. The decision rested on other deficiencies. Nor did lack of complete identity of plaintiffs alone defeat preclusion: note 3 recognizes that modern offensive estoppel does not always require mutuality. (¶¶7–9; 513–514; nn.2–3.)

The critical defects were finality and necessity. A consent judgment implementing a settlement does not necessarily make earlier evidentiary rulings essential to the judgment. Either side might have prevailed regardless of the seatbelt ruling; seatbelt nonuse affected allocation of damages, not whether General Motors caused the accident by designing a defective van. The ruling may have influenced settlement negotiations, but that was different from being necessary to an adjudicated liability judgment. (¶¶9–10; 514–515.)

The court acknowledged that finality for issue preclusion can extend beyond an ordinary appealable final judgment. It also expressly recognized that this choice-of-law ruling was not tentative or likely to change with developing trial evidence. Nevertheless, it was neither independently appealable nor independently reviewable. That distinguished a reviewable contempt order and prior summary-judgment cases in which settlement followed an opportunity for appellate review. Giving this ruling conclusive force could shield it from review and perpetuate error. The holding thus does not say that every interlocutory ruling is too provisional or that settlement always prevents preclusion. (¶¶11–14; 515.)

Settlement incentives reinforced the conclusion. Defendants facing several related suits might refuse early compromise if every adverse preliminary ruling would bind them later. The release expressly described a disputed claim and an intention to buy peace, not to concede issues. The court also worried that shared counsel could file one passenger’s case where a favorable choice-of-law ruling was likely and then export that ruling to others. It expressly **did not find** that this was counsel’s actual motive. Those considerations supported denying preclusion of this particular in-limine ruling, without announcing a general prohibition on offensive estoppel. (¶¶15–18; 515–516.)

The court next conducted its own conflicts analysis. Idaho law barred evidence of seatbelt nonuse in negligence cases; Arizona allowed that evidence in apportioning damages. Under Arizona’s most-significant-relationship approach, the place of injury supplied an initial preference, not an automatic rule. The court evaluated the Restatement’s contacts and policies with respect to this specific issue. The passengers’ domicile and the van-rental relationship centered in Arizona. Allegedly defective manufacture occurred in Michigan, rather than supplying a special Idaho contact. Idaho’s role as the place of injury was largely fortuitous. But the court did not merely count contacts: it evaluated their qualitative significance and the competing policies. (¶¶19–23; 516–518.)

Predictable legal expectations were less important here than in a consciously planned contract or will; passengers likely had not shaped their conduct around which state’s comparative-fault rule would govern an accident. Uniform outcomes among passengers did not override the reasons for rejecting preclusion. Either law was readily applicable, and interstate-system needs favored neither decisively. Both states supported seatbelt use, but Idaho favored full recovery without this evidence while Arizona limited recovery to damages attributable to others. Arizona had a substantial interest in its residents’ injury prevention, eventual medical care, rehabilitation, and support. Idaho had comparatively little interest in maximizing recovery for nonresident plaintiffs against nonresident defendants. The court concluded that Arizona had the more significant relationship to seatbelt use, even though applying its rule could reduce Arizona residents’ recovery. (¶¶24–36; 518–520.)

Notes 5–7 qualify that analysis. The comparative-fault Restatement provision plaintiffs invoked would still lead back to the same relationship test. Absence of a legal duty to wear a seatbelt did not prohibit considering a failure to take reasonable precautions when allocating injury damages. And the court recognized that initial medical providers and records might exist in Idaho; it did not declare that every medical contact occurred in Arizona. These qualifications keep the decision from becoming a simplistic residence-only rule. (nn.5–7.)

The court **reversed the trial judgment and remanded for proceedings consistent with the opinion**, with its opening expressly identifying a new trial. It allowed Arizona’s seatbelt defense to be considered; it did not itself fix a fault percentage, reduce damages by a specified amount, or absolve General Motors of defective design.
