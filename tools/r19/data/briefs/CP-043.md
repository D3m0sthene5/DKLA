Parties
Gaynell Reyno was the plaintiff as administratrix of the estates of five passengers killed in a Scottish airplane
crash. She was a California legal secretary employed by the lawyer bringing the action, had no personal
relationship with the decedents, and represented the interests of Scottish survivors. Piper Aircraft, the
Pennsylvania aircraft manufacturer, and Hartzell Propeller, the Ohio propeller manufacturer, were the
defendants and Supreme Court petitioners. The aircraft’s British owner, Scottish operator, and pilot’s estate
were potential third-party defendants absent from the American action. 454 U.S. at 238–240.
Material Facts
In July 1976, a Piper Aztec crashed during a charter flight from Blackpool to Perth in the Scottish highlands.
The pilot and five passengers died. They and their heirs were Scottish subjects and residents. The aircraft was
registered in Great Britain, owned and maintained by Air Navigation and Trading, and operated by McDonald
Aviation. Its wreckage was in England. These connections made British evidence important to the crash’s
cause and the survivors’ damages. Id. at 238–239.
A preliminary British investigation suggested mechanical failure after the plane entered a spin. A review board
later found no evidence of defective equipment and indicated that pilot error might have contributed: the
recently licensed commercial pilot flew below the minimum height prescribed by his operator’s manual. Those
were investigative conclusions relevant to the competing theories, not a Supreme Court determination of tort
liability. Manufacturing and design records, by contrast, remained in the United States. Id. at 239, 257–258.
Reyno sued on negligence and strict-liability theories. She acknowledged choosing an American forum for its
more favorable liability, damages, and capacity-to-sue rules. As described in the record at the time, Scottish
law did not recognize strict liability and limited wrongful-death recovery to relatives’ losses of support and
society. The passengers’ survivors also pursued separate litigation in the United Kingdom against the owner,
operator, and pilot’s estate. Piper and Hartzell agreed to submit to Scottish jurisdiction and waive available
limitations defenses. Id. at 239–242. The description of Scottish law is historical, not a statement of present
law.
Procedural History
Reyno obtained appointment in California probate court and filed in California state court. The defendants
removed the suit to the Central District of California. Piper sought transfer under § 1404(a); Hartzell
challenged personal jurisdiction and alternatively sought transfer. The district court quashed service on Hartzell
and transferred the litigation to the Middle District of Pennsylvania, where Hartzell was properly served. Id. at
240–241 & n.5.

The Pennsylvania district court then dismissed on forum non conveniens grounds after identifying Scotland as
an available alternative and balancing the private and public interests. It gave reduced deference to the chosen
forum because the real parties in interest were foreign. The Third Circuit reversed, both criticizing the
balancing and holding that an unfavorable change in applicable law would itself bar dismissal. The Supreme
Court reviewed the proper forum non conveniens standard and the resulting disposition. Id. at 241–247.
Issue
Does a foreign forum’s less favorable substantive law prevent forum non conveniens dismissal? If it does not,
how much deference is due a foreign plaintiff’s American forum choice, what showing of evidentiary
inconvenience is required, and did the court of appeals properly review the district court’s balancing of the
interests in this litigation? Id. at 247–261.
Rule and Holding
A less favorable substantive law ordinarily receives neither conclusive nor substantial weight in the forum non
conveniens inquiry. The court must first identify an available, adequate alternative forum. A remedy so clearly
inadequate that it amounts to no remedy may defeat that prerequisite or weigh heavily against dismissal; a
lower potential recovery or absence of strict liability does not alone establish inadequacy. Scotland supplied an
adequate alternative here. Id. at 247, 254–255 & n.22.
The plaintiff’s forum choice normally receives a strong presumption, but the inference of convenience is
weaker when the plaintiff or real parties in interest are foreign. A district court’s reasonable balancing of the
relevant private and public interests is reviewed for clear abuse of discretion. The district court did not abuse
that discretion in dismissing this action in favor of Scotland. Id. at 255–261.
Judgment
The Supreme Court reversed the Third Circuit’s judgment. It upheld the district court’s forum non conveniens
dismissal rather than directing a trial in Pennsylvania. This disposition did not transfer the American case into a
Scottish court or adjudicate whether a defect or negligence caused the accident. It left the claimants to pursue
the available foreign forum on the defendants’ stated undertakings. Id. at 242, 261.
Reasoning
The Court treated convenience as the central purpose of forum non conveniens. If a plaintiff could defeat
dismissal whenever the foreign forum offered less favorable law, the doctrine would provide little relief in the
very cases where a plaintiff selected an inconvenient forum for a legal advantage. A single factor would
displace the flexible assessment of the particular dispute. The Court’s earlier Canada Malting decision had
allowed dismissal despite a comparable claimed disadvantage. Id. at 247–251.
The proposed rule would also make a preliminary forum decision require extensive choice-of-law and
comparative-law litigation. A judge would have to determine the law applicable in each forum and compare
rights, procedures, and remedies before deciding where the action should proceed. That requirement would
undermine one purpose of the doctrine: avoiding unnecessary burdens associated with adjudicating disputes
centered elsewhere. These were reasons the Court gave for rejecting a categorical rule, rather than findings that
foreign claimants generally lacked valid claims. Id. at 251–252.
Section 1404(a) did not supply the contrary rule. Van Dusen’s preservation of applicable law concerned
movement within a unified federal system under a statute designed to facilitate transfer. A common-law
dismissal in favor of a foreign forum involves a different procedural mechanism. The statute expanded federal
transfer discretion and did not simply codify all aspects of forum non conveniens. The consequences for

The Court preserved an important limit: practical inconvenience cannot justify sending a plaintiff to a forum
offering no meaningful remedy. Scottish negligence remedies, however, allowed litigation of the accident even
without strict liability or the same damages measure. The undertakings to submit to jurisdiction and waive
limitations defenses supported availability. The comparison concerned an adequate opportunity to litigate, not
equivalence to every advantage of American law. Id. at 242, 254–255 & n.22.
Reduced deference followed from an inference about convenience. A person suing at home ordinarily has an
evident convenience reason for doing so; that inference is weaker for foreign beneficiaries represented by a
locally appointed administratrix. It did not follow that a foreign plaintiff’s choice receives no weight, or that an
American plaintiff’s home forum can never be disturbed. In either situation the court still balances the
case-specific burdens. Id. at 255–256 & nn.23–24.
On private interests, the Court acknowledged evidence pointing both ways. The manufacturers’ American
design and testing records favored access here. British maintenance, pilot-training, accident-investigation, and
damages evidence favored Scotland. The district court could reasonably conclude that a Scottish trial would
present fewer proof problems overall. The defendants needed enough information for a meaningful
comparison, but they did not have to identify every unavailable witness and precisely forecast all testimony
before obtaining relief. Id. at 257–259.
The inability to implead the owner, operator, and pilot’s estate particularly mattered. The manufacturers’
defense attributed the crash to others’ conduct, and a single proceeding could resolve those competing
accounts. Separate contribution or indemnity suits after an American trial would be more burdensome and
could create inconsistent factual outcomes. The appellate court’s distinction between burden and unfairness set
the bar too high: serious practical burden is relevant to this doctrine even without independently wrongful
treatment of a party. Id. at 259.
Public interests also supported the district court. Scotland had a strong local interest in an accident in its
airspace involving its residents and local operators. Jury burdens, judicial resources, and possible application of
foreign law were legitimate considerations. Even assuming the Third Circuit was right that American law
would govern, the remaining factors still supported Scotland. The Supreme Court therefore did not have to
settle the lower courts’ competing choice-of-law analyses to sustain the dismissal. Id. at 259–261.
Finally, appellate review required deference to a reasonable district-court balance. The Court of Appeals had
substituted its own assessment of individual factors after nominally acknowledging the abuse-of-discretion
standard. The Supreme Court accepted the district court’s conclusion without holding that every factor favored
Scotland or that every international product-liability claim must be dismissed. Id. at 257–261.
Separate Opinions
Justice White agreed with Parts I and II, including rejection of the rule that less favorable foreign law
automatically prevents dismissal. He would not have proceeded to decide the remaining balancing issues.
Justice Stevens, joined by Justice Brennan, likewise agreed that the law-disadvantage question should be
answered against Reyno, but would have remanded to the Third Circuit for reconsideration of whether
Pennsylvania was inconvenient for claims against a Pennsylvania manufacturer concerning manufacture and
design there. Their disagreement concerned the Court’s further disposition after answering the question on
which review had been granted. Id. at 261–262.

Notes
Keep availability, adequacy, deference, interest balancing, and appellate review analytically separate. A
defendant’s submission to a foreign court can establish practical availability; it does not establish that the
foreign remedy is adequate or that the balance favors dismissal. Likewise, fewer favorable causes of action do
not automatically establish the absence of a remedy. Id. at 242, 254–259.
The Court expressly left unresolved whether federal or state forum non conveniens law governs in a diversity
case because the relevant state and federal standards produced the same result here. It also did not conclusively
determine which substantive law would govern in Pennsylvania. Those reservations limit what Piper
establishes about Erie and choice of law. Id. at 248 n.13, 260. [Full-opinion qualification.]
