# Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc. v. Environmental Protection Agency
655 F.2d 318 (D.C. Cir. 1981) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Court, stage, and disposition

The D.C. Circuit reviewed consolidated petitions challenging EPA diesel-emissions regulations: particulate limits for passenger vehicles and light-duty trucks, temporary nitrogen-oxide waivers, and hydrocarbon testing procedures. Judge Mikva wrote the court’s opinion, joined by Judge Ginsburg. Judge Robb concurred in the nitrogen-oxide waiver holding but dissented from sustaining the 1985 particulate standards. The court affirmed all regulations reviewed in this proceeding. A separate challenge concerning the interaction of California nitrogen-oxide requirements with the 1982 particulate standard had been held in abeyance; the judgment should not be expanded to decide that reserved matter. 655 F.2d at 319–324 & n.7, 344–346.

## Regulatory facts and chronology

Diesel engines offered fuel-economy advantages but emitted substantially more particulate matter than gasoline engines with modern emission controls. EPA had authority under Clean Air Act §202(a)(1) to regulate vehicle pollutants reasonably anticipated to endanger public health or welfare. Section 202(a)(2) required sufficient time for development and application of necessary control technology. The 1977 amendments also supplied a particulate provision directing standards reflecting the greatest achievable emission reduction while considering cost, noise, energy, and safety, and authorized temporary waivers of the statutory nitrogen-oxide limit. These provisions presented different questions of authority, technological prediction, and temporary accommodation. Id. at 319–324, 325–327, 340–341.

EPA’s February 1979 proposal contemplated a particulate limit of 0.60 grams per mile for 1981 and 0.20 grams per mile for 1983. Its final rule retained 0.60 but postponed its effective year to 1982 because 1981 certification testing had become impracticable. The more demanding passenger-vehicle limit moved to 1985. Although EPA thought development by 1984 likely, it allowed an additional year for uncertainty. Light-duty trucks received a 1985 limit of 0.26 grams per mile, reflecting approximately 30 percent greater untreated particulate emissions. The regulatory category “light-duty trucks” included some vehicles classified as heavy-duty under the statute; it was not interchangeable with the passenger-vehicle category. Id. at 320–324 & nn.3, 7–8, 338–340.

The projected advance was a trap-oxidizer: exhaust would pass through material collecting particles, which would periodically be burned away to regenerate the trap. Engine modifications alone promised only part of the needed reduction. The central uncertainties concerned filtering material that survived repeated incineration, adequate collection efficiency, and reliable initiation and control of regeneration. No completed trap had demonstrated the desired lifetime performance. A promising GM Opel experiment had reached only 12,800 miles under unusually mild driving conditions, with approximately 55 percent collection efficiency. EPA nevertheless predicted that focused engineering could solve the remaining problems before manufacturers had to commit to mass production. Id. at 321–323, 332–336, 345–346.

Nitrogen-oxide control interacted with particulate control. Exhaust-gas recirculation reduced nitrogen oxides but increased particulates, so relaxing the former temporarily could facilitate particulate compliance and diesel development. EPA granted manufacturers waivers for 1981–1982, generally allowing 1.5 grams per mile and differing Volkswagen limits within the 1.3–1.5 range. The statutory ordinary limit was 1.0. The manufacturers had requested longer relief, and some initial applications were denied for inadequate information before additional submissions supported grants. Id. at 322–324, 340–344.

## Parties’ positions and questions

GM, Mercedes-Benz, and Volkswagen challenged the 1985 particulate limits as unsupported predictions about unavailable technology. GM also disputed EPA’s statutory basis, the truck adjustment, and testing that counted hydrocarbons associated with particles. NRDC attacked the standards from the opposite direction: EPA had allegedly delayed unnecessarily, insufficiently accounted for carcinogenic risks, and protected the highest-emitting diesel models instead of imposing more demanding differentiated limits. NRDC also disputed the health and air-quality justification for nitrogen-oxide waivers. The resulting questions were whether EPA had lawful authority, a reasoned basis for projecting future technology, and adequate explanations for these related regulatory choices. Id. at 323–324, 336–344.

## Statutory authority

EPA was wrong to read §202(a)(3)(A)(iii) as a mandatory particulate-standard provision covering all light-duty vehicles. Its isolated reference to classes or categories of vehicles favored EPA, but the court read the surrounding provisions and legislative history together. The Senate provision concerned heavy-duty vehicles and motorcycles; its redistribution at conference, the enacted section’s title, and adjacent heavy-duty limitations showed that omission of an express heavy-duty qualifier did not establish the broad mandate EPA claimed. The court treated §202(a)(3)(A)(iii) as a heavy-duty provision. Id. at 325–327 & nn.10–16.

That interpretive error did not invalidate the rules. Section 202(a)(1) independently authorized particulate regulation of light-duty vehicles, and EPA had made the necessary health findings and exercised the relevant discretion. The record supported action under that provision even though EPA had also cited an inapplicable mandatory provision. The court therefore declined to require a remand merely to correct the statutory citation. This was a reasoned assessment of the particular administrative record, not permission to invent a new agency rationale whenever the stated legal basis fails. Id. at 327–328.

## Review of technological predictions

The arbitrary-or-capricious standard required searching review of EPA’s explanation without substituting judicial technical preferences. Congress expected technology forcing: standards could promote advances rather than await an already available control device. Requiring the certainty appropriate to existing technology would defeat that design. Yet a prediction needed a defensible methodology and an explanation rooted in the record; agency confidence alone was insufficient. Id. at 328–331.

Lead time mattered in three ways. It created uncertainty, provided an opportunity to turn theoretical solutions into working equipment, and left room to adjust the standard before manufacturers irrevocably committed to production. The court distinguished earlier litigation in which production deadlines were close and EPA had inadequately justified adjustments to existing data. Here, development time remained before a separate period for production preparation. The governing inquiry was whether EPA answered theoretical objections, identified the major refinement steps, and offered plausible reasons that each could be completed within the available time. The opinion’s discussion of other statutes did not convert their distinct economic-achievability language into a universal Clean Air Act test. Id. at 329–332 & nn.19–22.

## Application to particulate standards

The majority found enough supporting evidence despite the incomplete prototype. Trap collection worked in principle. Materials research had advanced, alternative materials and combinations remained available, and the problem had only recently become the subject of concentrated particulate-control development. The court accepted EPA’s distinction between established theoretical obstacles and engineering refinements reasonably expected to yield to further work. GM’s own statements that compliance might be possible reinforced the prediction, although they did not estop GM from challenging it. Id. at 331–334 & n.22.

Durability, efficiency, and regeneration were related. The court treated adequate efficiency as dependent on durability and regeneration, and relied on materials already demonstrating sufficient initial efficiency; EPA could consider a modest adjustment if durable traps ultimately fell slightly short for the highest-emitting models. Regeneration could employ throttling or independent heat, with catalysts, insulation, or chamber arrangements addressing particular difficulties. Existing control technologies supported EPA’s confidence that these approaches could be coordinated. An unsuccessful test did not establish an insuperable obstacle. Nor did speculative safety concerns require EPA to abandon the approach when it would reconsider demonstrated serious risks. The rule prescribed emissions performance rather than compulsory use of a particular trap design. Id. at 333–336 & nn.25–31.

NRDC’s demand for a 1984 deadline did not eliminate EPA’s authority to allow a reasonable uncertainty margin. EPA had considered potentially carcinogenic constituents rather than ignored them. A uniform limit also remained reasonable: the record did not establish a consistent engine size or vehicle weight relationship supporting the proposed graduated standards, and differentiation could disadvantage cleaner models. The statute permitted EPA’s approach to accommodating the highest-emitting models; the court did not hold that every future standard must protect every existing model. EPA could consider diesel development and energy advantages within the statutory scheme. Id. at 336–338 & nn.32–33.

The truck adjustment rested on observed industry data and relevant differences in inertia, aerodynamic drag, road load, downsizing prospects, and nitrogen-oxide control. GM proposed a different account of exhaust-flow effects but supplied no data showing that its factor would change the result. It likewise identified no specific technical barrier to adapting passenger-vehicle particulate controls to trucks. EPA was not required to conclusively disprove every conceivable future complication. The discussion of nonconformance penalties concerned trucks within the statutory heavy-duty category, rather than a general exception for all regulated vehicles. Id. at 338–340 & nn.34–35.

## Nitrogen-oxide waivers and hydrocarbon testing

The waivers were temporary accommodations, not permanent findings that diesel emissions were harmless. EPA considered their incremental health effects during the waiver period, fuel-economy benefits, and the prospect of compliance after expiration. Nitrogen-oxide increases had to be assessed alongside the particulate increase that strict nitrogen-oxide control could cause. NRDC’s prediction that lower prices would expand diesel sales enough to offset particulate benefits lacked a factual basis, and its comparison incorrectly treated particulate limits as independent of the nitrogen-oxide tradeoff. Id. at 340–342 & nn.36–40.

EPA reasonably interpreted the long-term air-quality requirement to ask whether there was a substantial likelihood that engines would meet applicable standards after the waiver. The court supported that reading with the statutory setting and legislative explanation, while recognizing it as an interpretation rather than the only imaginable meaning. Applicable standards included agency particulate limits as well as statutory nitrogen-oxide limits. The opinion’s interpretive deference belongs to its 1981 doctrinal setting and should not be presented as a certification of current administrative-law rules. Id. at 342–344 & nn.40–41.

GM’s hydrocarbon challenge also failed. The Act regulated total hydrocarbons, not only constituents proven reactive by GM’s proposed measure. EPA reasonably found GM’s atmospheric simulations unpersuasive and left open whether particle-bound hydrocarbons contributed to both pollution problems. Although testing counted these constituents in both measurements, the technology-based particulate baseline already incorporated that measurement effect. GM therefore had not demonstrated an unfair additional burden. Id. at 344.

## Robb’s separate opinion and significance

Robb agreed that EPA could make technological predictions and accepted the majority’s stated test. He disputed its application to trap durability. GM had researched the problem since 1974, tested materials from at least sixteen manufacturers, and evaluated twenty-two leading materials, yet its best filter survived far less than the statutory five-year/50,000-mile useful life or EPA’s contemplated 100,000-mile practical durability. The limited Opel trial offered weak grounds for expecting ordinary driving and repeated regeneration to be solved within the development window. In Robb’s view, an assertion that determined engineering would optimize a design was hope rather than a plausible evidentiary explanation. He therefore dissented from Part II’s approval of the 1985 particulate standards while expressly concurring in Part III’s waivers. Id. at 345–346 & Robb nn.1–2.

The assigned excerpt selects the majority’s explanation of technology forcing, lead time, and the distinction between theoretical obstacles and engineering uncertainty. The complete decision additionally resolves statutory scope, NRDC’s demands for tighter regulation, truck adjustments, temporary waivers, and testing. Its historical lesson is that uncertainty can coexist with reasoned regulation when the agency explains the remaining work and its realistic timetable; the dissent identifies the evidentiary limit of that principle.
