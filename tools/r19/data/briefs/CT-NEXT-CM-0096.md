# Keith v. Buchanan
173 Cal. App. 3d 13, 220 Cal. Rptr. 392 (1985) | Contracts

## Court and procedural posture

California’s Second District Court of Appeal, Division Six, decided the case on October 9, 1985. Justice Ochoa wrote; Presiding Justice Stone and Justice Gilbert concurred without separate writings. Keith appealed a judgment for the sellers entered under Code of Civil Procedure §631.8 at the close of his case in the trial proceedings. The appellate court reversed the determination that no Commercial Code express warranty existed, affirmed the remaining determinations, and remanded because the trial court had not decided whether the warranty was breached. This was neither a damages award to Keith nor an appellate factual finding that the boat was unseaworthy. 173 Cal. App. 3d at 18–19, 26.

## The transaction and disputed representations

In October 1978, Keith visited a Long Beach boat show, examined several vessels, spoke with sales representatives, and collected advertising brochures. He had attended sailing school, belonged to a yacht club and the Coast Guard Auxiliary, and sailed on many yachts to determine his preferences, although he had never owned a yacht. The sellers’ Island Trader 41 brochures described a seaworthy live-aboard vessel. Keith also discussed his desire for long-distance ocean cruising with a sales representative and testified that he relied on the brochure representations. He asked his friend Buddy Ebsen, who was involved in boatbuilding, and another experienced person to observe the boat; they advised that it would suit his needs. He paid a deposit, contracted, ordered accessories, and bought the vessel in November for $75,610. After delivery, its seaworthiness became disputed. Id. at 18–19.

Keith sued for express and implied warranty breaches. The trial court reasoned that no defendant had made a written undertaking to preserve performance or compensate for performance failure, classified the brochures as opinions or commendations, and found no reliance on the sellers’ selection judgment because Keith used his own experts. Those conclusions involved different warranty doctrines. The appeal required the court to keep the statutory consumer warranty definition, Commercial Code express warranty, and implied warranty of fitness separate. Id. at 19–20.

## The statutory distinction the trial court missed

The Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act defined an express warranty in terms of a written undertaking to maintain a consumer good’s utility or performance or compensate for a failure. The trial court correctly found that kind of written undertaking absent. But the Act did not eliminate the buyer’s independent Commercial Code rights. Keith’s counsel had argued both statutory grounds, and the trial court had not properly analyzed Commercial Code §2313, California’s enactment of UCC §2-313. The appellate court therefore affirmed the Song-Beverly determination while undertaking the separate Commercial Code inquiry. Id. at 19–20.

Section 2313 requires three inquiries: whether the seller made an affirmation of fact, promise, or description rather than mere opinion or commendation; whether that representation became part of the basis of the bargain; and whether the goods breached the resulting warranty. Formal warranty language and a specific intention to warrant are unnecessary. The complete statute reproduced in footnote 1 also addresses samples and models, but this case concerned brochure descriptions, not a disputed sample. Id. at 20–21 & n.1.

## Why the seaworthiness language was factual

The characterization of sales language depends on its context. The opinion recognized a tendency to construe affirmations of quality in consumers’ favor, but did not equate every favorable adjective with a warranty. Lack of specificity, equivocation, or disclosure that goods are experimental can point toward opinion. Here the advertisements made definite, unequivocal representations of seaworthiness; the representative knew that Keith wanted a boat for long-distance ocean travel, and the literature described production following careful testing. Nothing suggested an experimental vessel. In that setting, the descriptions addressed the boat’s actual quality and condition. The use of promotional language around that representation did not make its seaworthiness assertion mere puffery. Id. at 21–22. Footnote 2 supplies comparative decisions on the fact–opinion distinction rather than an additional holding.

## Basis of the bargain and the limited inspection

Former law demanded proof of reliance. Section 2313’s “basis of the bargain” language and Official Comment 3 changed both the degree of reliance needed and its proof. The buyer need not establish that the statement was the dominant reason for buying or that he would have refused the transaction without it. A seller’s warranty representation is presumptively a factor inducing the bargain; the seller bears the burden of showing that the bargain did not rest on it at all. The court acknowledged earlier disagreement about whether the Code abolished reliance or shifted its burden, then applied this presumption and affirmative-proof approach. It did not hold that representations entirely outside the bargain necessarily bind a seller. Id. at 22–24.

Actual knowledge of the goods’ true condition before contracting could demonstrate that a representation did not induce the purchase. Inspection can matter for the same reason, but observing the goods does not automatically erase an express warranty when the supposed defect was not discovered and waived. Ebsen and his associate observed the boat without testing it in the water. Their limited inspection could not establish whether it would be reasonably fit in materials, construction, and equipment once put to sea. Keith knew the brochures and discussed his cruising plans before contracting; the sellers did not rebut the presumption that seaworthiness formed part of the bargain. Id. at 23–24.

Footnote 3 adds an important timing distinction. Other evidence concerned examination after the sales contract but before delivery and acceptance. Such an inspection was irrelevant to formation of this express warranty. Drawing an instructive distinction from the Code comment governing implied warranty exclusions, the court explained that a precontract examination can bear on responsibility assumed at contracting, whereas a later acceptance inspection is a different event. The assigned casebook retains this note but renumbers it as note 1. Id. at 24 & n.3; casebook printed page 635.

## Why the implied fitness claim nevertheless failed

An implied warranty of fitness for a particular purpose under §2315 depends on the buyer’s actual reliance on the seller’s skill or judgment to select suitable goods and the seller’s reason to know that reliance, as well as the particular purpose and knowledge of it. Those reliance requirements differ from the express warranty’s basis-of-the-bargain presumption. The trial court found that Keith relied on his own experience and advisers to select a vessel. On appeal that factual determination received substantial-evidence review: the court viewed the evidence favorably to the respondents and accepted reasonable supporting inferences, rather than deciding reliance anew. Id. at 24–26.

Keith’s sailing experience, precise specifications, comparisons among boats, attention to literature, and use of friends’ expertise adequately supported that finding. The same friends’ limited observation could therefore support rejection of reliance on the sellers’ selection judgment without proving discovery and waiver of the express seaworthiness warranty. The difference is what the buyer sought from the seller: a warranted factual quality of the selected boat, versus reliance on the seller to choose a suitable boat for him. The implied fitness judgment was affirmed. Footnote 4 expressly states that Keith presented no implied merchantability claim; the court did not reject such a claim on its merits. Id. at 25–26 & n.4.

## Exact result and significance

Only the ruling that no express warranty existed was reversed. The trial court heard considerable conflicting evidence about breach of the seaworthiness warranty but made no finding on that question, so further proceedings remained necessary. The other aspects of the judgment were affirmed, and each party bore its own appellate costs. Id. at 26. The decision illustrates why warranty existence, a buyer’s reliance in selecting goods, discovery of defects during inspection, and proof of breach must be analyzed separately; success on warranty formation does not itself establish breach or damages.
