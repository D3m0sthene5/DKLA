# Mosley v. General Motors Corp.

**Civil Procedure · CP-081**  
497 F.2d 1330 (8th Cir. 1974) · May 31, 1974 · Opinion by Judge Ross

## Facts and proceedings

Nathaniel Mosley and nine other employees brought individual claims and proposed class claims against General Motors and Local 25 of the United Auto Workers. They alleged discrimination under Title VII and 42 U.S.C. § 1981. Each had filed an EEOC charge, and the agency had found reasonable cause to believe that GM's Chevrolet and Fisher Body divisions and the union engaged in unlawful employment practices. The employees then received notices authorizing suit. The agency finding and the complaint's allegations were the procedural background, rather than a judicial determination that discrimination had occurred. 497 F.2d at 1331.

The first eight individual counts concerned the Chevrolet division; the remaining two concerned Fisher Body. The alleged practices included discriminatory promotion and hiring, race-based discharge, retaliation for protests, and unequal relief time. The union allegedly discriminated in relief time and failed to pursue discrimination grievances, described in the assigned text as “6a” grievances. The employees sought injunctions, back pay, fees, and costs. Two additional counts proposed class relief against the respective GM divisions. These varied employment events were connected, according to the plaintiffs, by an overarching discriminatory policy.

GM moved to strike or clarify allegations and raised questions about class treatment. The district court severed the ten individual counts into ten separate actions, requiring separate filings. It left the proposed class action open for further allegations by the plaintiffs individually or collectively. The court viewed the individual disputes as largely unrelated, sharing little beyond a defendant, and considered the combined pleading unmanageable. It certified its order for interlocutory review under 28 U.S.C. § 1292(b), and the Eighth Circuit permitted that appeal. 497 F.2d at 1331–32.

## Issue, rule, and review standard

The central question was whether the district court abused its discretion by requiring separate actions despite allegations that a common company and union policy caused the employees' injuries. Rule 20(a) allowed joinder when the asserted rights to relief arose from the same transaction, occurrence, or series of transactions or occurrences **and** a common question of law or fact would arise. Both requirements mattered. Rules 20(b) and 42(b) supplied tools for separate trials or other protective orders, so allowing joinder did not require trying every issue together. Appellate review was for abuse of discretion. 497 F.2d at 1332–33.

The transaction requirement received a practical, case-specific construction. Events could constitute a series because they were logically related; they did not need to be identical or immediately connected. The common-question requirement likewise did not require every factual or legal question to be shared. The rules aimed to reduce unnecessary suits, expense, and delay while preserving fairness.

## Application and reasoning

The employees did more than identify a common employer. Each alleged injury from the same general discriminatory policy of GM and the union. That alleged policy linked otherwise different personnel actions into a series of transactions or occurrences. The court analogized to *United States v. Mississippi*, where separate voting registrars' actions could be joined because they allegedly implemented a statewide system of racial discrimination. A companywide employment policy could supply the same logical connection. 497 F.2d at 1333–34.

The existence and discriminatory character of the asserted policies also supplied a common factual question. Each employee's recovery depended on showing that the defendants' discriminatory conduct wronged that employee. Differences in the effects of the policy, including differences in damages, did not eliminate the shared question whether the policy was discriminatory. The court used decisions construing Rule 23's commonality requirement as an interpretive aid; it did not hold that satisfying Rule 20 automatically established all requirements for a certified class.

The district court's manageability concern did not justify forcing ten new lawsuits at this stage. If individualized damages or another issue later warranted separate trials, the court could separate those issues after deciding common questions. This preserved useful common litigation without requiring that every employee's claim receive precisely the same trial treatment. 497 F.2d at 1334.

## Exact disposition and limits

The Eighth Circuit **reversed and remanded the ruling disallowing joinder of the individual claims, with directions to permit the plaintiffs to proceed jointly**. It **affirmed the portion withholding a determination of the proposed class's propriety until further discovery**. It denied appellate attorney fees as premature, without prejudice to renewal at final disposition. The latter two outcomes appear in the complete opinion and are omitted from the assigned excerpt's abbreviated conclusion. 497 F.2d at 1334.

Ross wrote for the panel consisting of Ross, Stephenson, and Senior District Judge Van Pelt, sitting by designation; no separate writing is reported. The only report footnote identifies Van Pelt's designation. This joinder ruling established neither ultimate discrimination liability nor class certification.

## Assigned emphasis and sources

The case teaches how an alleged common policy can connect different events under Rule 20, while separate-trial authority addresses later individualized issues. The policy allegation must explain the connection: the decision does not announce that sharing an employer alone permits every employment dispute to be joined.
