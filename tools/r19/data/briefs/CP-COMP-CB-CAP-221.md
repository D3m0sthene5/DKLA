# Flintkote Company v. Aviva PLC

**177 F. Supp. 3d 1165 (N.D. Cal. 2016), April 4, 2016.** District Judge Susan Illston entered this order concerning insurance payments to an asbestos bankruptcy trust, a statutory bond, and sealing. The assigned account is CivPro physical page 278, printed page 519, Note 5. Its caption says “ACIA PLC”; the actual judicial report identifies **Aviva PLC**. This is a discrepancy in the original assigned page, not merely its OCR.

Flintkote manufactured building materials containing asbestos and faced substantial bodily-injury claims. It entered Chapter 11 in Delaware in 2004. Its confirmed § 524(g) reorganization plan became effective September 30, 2015, channeling asbestos claims to the Flintkote Trust. Flintkote Company and the Trust sought payment under policies issued by a predecessor insurer; the defendants included Aviva PLC, Aviva International, and Ocean Marine, collectively called Aviva in the opinion. Disagreement over the correct successor insurer remained relevant to the bond and was not definitively resolved here. (1166–1167 & nn.1–6; 1181 n.15.)

The Trust evaluated claims under distribution procedures assigning liquidated values by disease or individual review. Those values did not equal the claimant's enforceable payment from the Trust. The applicable payment percentage was 8%: a mesothelioma claim scheduled at $184,000 yielded $14,720. The percentage sought to preserve equitable treatment of current and future claimants against finite assets. Trustees could reconsider it with the consent of the Trust Advisory Committee and Future Claimants Representative. Flintkote argued that insurers should pay the full liquidated value, adding assets that could improve recoveries. Aviva argued that its indemnity obligation covered only what the Trust actually paid. (1167–1169.)

The insurance relationships also involved the 1985 Wellington Agreement, which Flintkote had signed but these insurers had not, and a separate 1989 agreement incorporating many Wellington provisions. Unlike the Wellington arrangement, the parties' agreement provided for litigation rather than arbitration. Other insurers nevertheless participated in parallel Wellington arbitration presenting substantially the same payment issue. The disputed choice-of-law language required application of the applicable common law of United States states; it did not identify a particular state. (1169–1171.)

Procedural maneuvering made that omission significant. After mediation failed, Aviva sought relief from the bankruptcy stay to sue in California. Flintkote filed an anticipatory Delaware action before the scheduled hearing. A Delaware order compelling arbitration was reversed, and the litigation ultimately was transferred to California. The Delaware court found evidence that Flintkote had selected that forum to avoid California's unfavorable *Fuller-Austin* decision. The Delaware court did not itself decide the ultimate insurance choice of law. In this order, Flintkote sought a declaration requiring full liquidated-value payments; Aviva sought partial summary judgment limiting its obligation. Their material disagreement concerned legal interpretation rather than disputed facts. (1170–1173; 1171 n.8.)

The first issue was which state's law supplied that interpretation. Flintkote proposed generally applicable insurance-contract principles and maintained that the relevant decisions agreed on full-value coverage. Illston rejected a free-standing national or federal general common law under *Erie*. The decisions Flintkote invoked applied Illinois or Maryland law; their disagreement with California did not establish a uniform rule binding this court. Nor did the Wellington wording instruct the court to select whatever position seemed the majority rule. It required identification of an applicable state's law. (1174–1176.)

Under *Klaxon*, a diversity court ordinarily applies the forum state's conflicts principles. The court rejected Flintkote's argument that Delaware's conflicts rules had followed the transferred case. Footnote 12 treated this anticipatory filing to avoid California law as outside the transfer principle on which Flintkote relied, analogizing *Atlantic Marine*'s rejection of rewarding inappropriate forum selection. That reasoning is an important part of this particular order; it should not be expanded into a claim that every transfer eliminates the transferor's conflicts rules. (1175–1176 & n.12.)

Applying California's governmental-interest approach, the court found that California had the compelling connection to the insurance dispute. Flintkote was headquartered there; California appeared to be the principal location of the insured risk, and many asbestos cases had been brought there. Delaware's incorporation and trust-administration interests did not transform interpretation of much older insurance policies into a question about the Trust's internal operation. The court also cited Flintkote's own earlier arguments for California law and the final parallel arbitration ruling favoring California. Flintkote had relied on preliminary arbitral views without acknowledging that the arbitrator changed those views in the final Phase I ruling. Uniform interpretation of the related arrangements supported the same result. (1175–1178 & n.11.)

California's *Fuller-Austin* decision then controlled the payment question. The policies indemnified sums Flintkote was obligated to pay by law. The Trust was obligated to pay the payment percentage, not the hypothetical full liquidated value. Public policy favoring asbestos compensation could not enlarge the contractual coverage. Flintkote's effort to distinguish the trusts failed: both permitted adjustment of percentages and supplemental payments; neither made additional insurance recoveries automatically increase the percentage. The waiver of exclusionary defenses likewise did not enlarge the initial scope of the insuring agreement. The dispute concerned the coverage promised, rather than a condition or exclusion defeating otherwise promised coverage. (1178–1181.)

Flintkote also obtained a bond ruling. California Insurance Code § 1616 required an unauthorized foreign or alien insurer to obtain authority or post security before pleading. Aviva had not produced the requisite certificate. Its § 1620(a) exception argument failed because it had not supplied affirmative evidence of compliance with the asserted foreign jurisdictions' laws, and California law governed interpretation here. The alternative discretionary provision did not displace the mandatory requirement applicable to this dispute. The court did **not** fix the bond at either side's proposed amount: Flintkote sought $15 million and Aviva proposed $2 million. It required consultation and short submissions by April 22, 2016, so the amount could reflect the payment ruling. Defendants remained jointly responsible unless a proper motion resolved individual liability. (1181–1183 & n.15.)

The exact disposition was to **deny Flintkote's trust-payment declaration, grant Aviva partial summary judgment, and grant Flintkote's request for a bond**, with the amount left for further proceedings. The court also granted the listed sealing motions at that time, reserving possible further briefing. Payment timing, whether these were “pay first” policies, defense-cost effects on policy limits, and coverage of certain Trust expenses remained unresolved. This was a single district-judge order, with no separate writing or numerical appellate vote. (1166–1167 n.4; 1172; 1183 & n.16.)

The assignment isolates *Erie* and *Klaxon*. The complete opinion shows why those methodological choices affected actual trust recoveries and reveals the special transfer rationale omitted from the account. This brief describes the 2016 order without certifying its treatment in later decisions. Independent-review status appears in the accompanying register.
