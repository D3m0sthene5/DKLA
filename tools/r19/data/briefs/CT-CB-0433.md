# Monarco v. Lo Greco
35 Cal. 2d 621, 220 P.2d 737 (Cal. 1950) | Contracts | Reliance, unjust enrichment, and estoppel to assert the statute of frauds

## A lifetime of work for an eventual inheritance

Natale and Carmela Castiglia married in Colorado in 1919. Carmela had three children from an earlier marriage, including Christie Lo Greco and Rosie Norcia. Natale's grandson, Carmen Monarco, was the child of his deceased daughter from an earlier marriage. When the family moved to California, Natale and Carmela invested approximately $4,000 in a half interest in agricultural property. Rosie and her husband Nick acquired the other half. Christie moved with the family; Monarco remained in Colorado. These relationships explain why the later dispute involved both ownership of the family venture and conflicting expectations about inheritance. (35 Cal. 2d at 622–623.)

In 1926, eighteen-year-old Christie planned to leave home and establish an independent livelihood. His mother and stepfather offered a different arrangement: if he stayed and worked in the family venture, they would keep their property in joint tenancy. The survivor would then leave it to Christie by will, apart from small devises to John and Rosie. Christie accepted through performance. For approximately twenty years he worked diligently, receiving room, board, and spending money while foregoing further education and the opportunity to accumulate property of his own. When he married and sought a present interest to support his wife, Natale reassured him that his wife could move into the household and that he would ultimately receive the property. (Id. at 622–624.)

Natale and Carmela placed their property in joint tenancy and in 1941 executed wills consistent with the arrangement. Those wills left the property to Christie, subject to small devises to Rosie and John and $500 to Monarco. Although the wills did not refer to the agreement, the three participants agreed on their terms. By Natale's death, the parents' interest in the successful venture was worth approximately $100,000. That figure concerns their interest, rather than the original purchase price or a judicial damages award. (Id. at 622–623.)

Shortly before his death, Natale secretly changed course. Without informing Carmela or Christie, he arranged conveyances terminating the joint tenancies and made a will leaving his property to Monarco. The probate court distributed that property to Monarco, and its decree became final. Monarco then brought two actions seeking partition and an accounting. Carmela cross-complained to have him declared a constructive trustee of property received through Natale's breach of his promise to maintain joint tenancy. The trial court entered judgments for the defendants and cross-complainant. Monarco appealed. One action concerned property in which the Norcias also owned a half interest; the other concerned property formerly owned only by Natale and Carmela. Carmela died after trial, and her executors were substituted. (Id. at 623 and the opinion's sole explanatory footnote.)

## The writing defense and the equitable problem

The controlling question was whether Monarco could invoke the then-applicable statute of frauds to prevent enforcement of the oral agreement. The court addressed the historical writing requirement for the agreement before it, citing Civil Code section 1624 and Code of Civil Procedure section 1973. The assigned textbook's discussion of later probate legislation is editorial updating, rather than a statute applied by the 1950 court. The issue was not whether the oral agreement automatically satisfied the statutory writing requirement. It was whether these facts estopped the party claiming through Natale from using that requirement as a defense. (Id. at 623; assigned printed p. 434.)

Justice Traynor identified two circumstances supporting such an estoppel. Refusal to enforce can produce unconscionable injury when one party has been induced seriously to change position in reliance on the agreement. It can also unjustly enrich a party who has accepted the other party's performance and then seeks to escape the reciprocal obligation. Either can support the doctrine; many cases, including this one, involve both. The court's references to fraud describe the inequitable consequences of permitting the defense. The opinion does not condition relief on proof that Natale intended deception when the original promise was made. (35 Cal. 2d at 623–624.)

Christie's reliance was much more consequential than ordinary disappointment at an unenforced promise. He devoted his adult working life to the venture and surrendered opportunities for education, independent earnings, and ownership. The comparison with the Norcias made the resulting prejudice concrete. Their small original investment produced an ownership interest worth approximately $100,000 that remained legally protected. Christie contributed labor and refrained from seeking a present interest because he repeatedly received assurances of eventual ownership. Treating money investment as protected while denying comparable effect to twenty years of induced labor would disregard the substance of his contribution. The court drew an analogy to a resulting trust that could have protected an investment of money under a comparable oral understanding; it did not declare that Christie had supplied purchase money or resolve the case by mechanically imposing a purchase-money resulting trust. (Id. at 624–625.)

Natale had also received the benefit of that sustained work. Allowing him or those taking through him to retain its fruits while repudiating the promised disposition would unjustly enrich them. The statute therefore threatened both a serious uncompensated change of position and retention of benefits acquired through the same bargain. Those linked facts supported estoppel against Monarco despite his status as devisee rather than the original promisor. (Id.)

## No separate assurance about a writing was necessary

Monarco argued for a narrower rule: estoppel should require a representation that a writing was unnecessary, would be executed, or that the statute would not be asserted. Such assurances appeared in the leading Seymour decision and were repeated in later formulations. Traynor explained that those formulations could not be detached from the circumstances of the cases. Decisions refusing estoppel had found either no unconscionable injury or an adequate remedy in quantum meruit. Other decisions had applied estoppel to prevent unconscionable injury or unjust enrichment without a representation about the writing requirement itself. (Id. at 625–626.)

The court also compared partly performed oral land contracts, where possession or improvements can support specific enforcement without an assurance about statutory formalities. The operative reliance is ordinarily on the promise of performance. A person reorganizes a life or improves property because the other party promises the bargain will be carried out. Likewise, unjust enrichment follows from accepting performance while avoiding the promised exchange, whether or not the promisor discussed a writing. The court therefore rejected a categorical additional requirement of misrepresentation about the statute. This did not dispense with the need for substantial reliance or inequitable retention of benefits on the facts presented. (Id. at 625–626.)

A money remedy would not adequately substitute here. The court regarded ordinary damages and compensation for the market value of services as inadequate for a promise to leave property by will in return for services of a peculiar nature involving continuation of a close family relationship. Christie's contribution was bound up with a lifetime in the family undertaking, rather than a series of readily priced commercial tasks. That inadequacy supported equitable enforcement of the promised property arrangement through the constructive-trust judgments. (Id. at 626.)

## Why Carmela could invoke Christie's reliance

The full opinion addresses an important argument omitted from the assigned excerpt. Monarco maintained that Christie was not a party to these actions and that his reliance could not support Carmela's claim. He also argued that defendants had pleaded an agreement protecting Carmela as surviving joint tenant, rather than an agreement for Christie's benefit. The court answered that Carmela was the person presently entitled to the property under the arrangement when the actions began. It was therefore appropriate for her to seek enforcement of the promise to maintain joint tenancy. (Id. at 626–627.)

To the extent Christie's services supplied consideration for Natale's promise to keep the property in joint tenancy with Carmela, she was a third-party beneficiary of the agreement between Christie and Natale. She could rely on the estoppel arising from Christie's change of position and Natale's acceptance of its benefits. The court did not require a beneficiary independently to repeat the reliance already undertaken by the contracting promisee. Its comparison to Notten, involving heirs claiming through a reciprocal testamentary arrangement, showed why the pertinent reliance could belong to someone other than the claimant seeking the constructive trust. Christie's twenty years of service and Natale's receipt of those services supplied the necessary basis here. (Id. at 627.)

## Judgment and limits

The California Supreme Court affirmed both judgments. The constructive-trust relief enforced the property arrangement against Monarco; the decision did not award Christie a stated sum for twenty years of wages or simply reverse for another trial. Traynor wrote for a unanimous seven-member court: Chief Justice Gibson and Justices Shenk, Edmonds, Carter, Schauer, and Spence concurred, with no separate opinion. (Id. at 627.)
