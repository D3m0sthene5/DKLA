# Lucia v. Securities and Exchange Commission
585 U.S. 237 (2018), No. 17–130 (June 21, 2018) | Legislation and the Regulatory State | SEC adjudicators as constitutional officers

## A securities proceeding conducted by a staff-appointed judge

Raymond Lucia and his investment company marketed a retirement strategy called “Buckets of Money.” The SEC accused them of using misleading slideshow presentations and instituted an administrative enforcement proceeding under the Investment Advisers Act. The Commission assigned the matter to administrative law judge Cameron Elliot, who had been selected by agency staff rather than the Commissioners. At the time, all five SEC ALJs had been selected by staff. Court slip 1–3.

After nine days of testimony and argument, Elliot found violations and imposed sanctions including $300,000 in civil penalties and a lifetime investment-industry bar. His initial decision made findings on only one of four alleged ways the presentation deceived investors. The Commission remanded for findings on the others, explaining that the judge's experience with the witnesses put him in the best position to resolve evidentiary conflicts. Elliot issued additional findings and a revised initial decision carrying the same sanctions. These were administrative findings; the Supreme Court's appointment decision did not determine whether Lucia's presentations actually violated the securities laws. Court slip 2–3.

An SEC ALJ could supervise discovery, issue or modify subpoenas, decide motions, rule on evidence, administer oaths, examine witnesses, regulate the proceeding, and sanction misconduct. At the hearing's conclusion, the ALJ issued an initial decision containing findings, legal conclusions, and an appropriate remedy. The Commission could review that decision on petition or its own initiative. If it declined review, it issued a finality order; the initial decision then became final and was deemed the Commission's action. That combination of adjudicative responsibility and agency supervision drove the disagreement over officer status. Court slip 2, 8–11.

## The appointment objection and the divided court below

Lucia raised the appointment objection before the Commission. He argued that ALJs were officers of the United States, who had to be appointed through the Appointments Clause rather than ordinary staff hiring. The Commission itself qualified as a department head capable of appointing inferior officers, but had not made Elliot's appointment. The SEC rejected the objection, characterizing ALJs as employees who exercised no significant authority independent of Commission supervision. Court slip 3–4, 5 & n.3.

A D.C. Circuit panel agreed. On rehearing, ten judges divided evenly, leaving a per curiam judgment rejecting Lucia's claim, 868 F.3d 1021 (2017). That conflicted with the Tenth Circuit's decision in Bandimere. The federal government changed its position at the certiorari stage and supported officer status. The Supreme Court therefore appointed an amicus to defend the judgment below. The government also requested a ruling on statutory removal protections; the Court declined that request both when granting review and on the merits. No lower court had addressed the removal question. Court slip 4 & nn.1–2.

## Holding, judgment, and participation

The Court held that SEC ALJs were officers subject to the Appointments Clause. Elliot had not been properly appointed when he heard and decided Lucia's case, and Lucia had timely challenged that defect. The Court reversed the D.C. Circuit and remanded for further proceedings consistent with its opinion. Lucia was entitled to a new hearing before a properly appointed different ALJ or the Commission itself. The Court did not erase the enforcement charges or decide their merits. Court slip 12–13.

Justice Kagan wrote for six Justices: Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Kennedy, Thomas, Alito, and Gorsuch joined. Thomas also wrote a concurrence joined by Gorsuch. Breyer concurred in the judgment in part and dissented in part, favoring statutory grounds and disagreeing with the required change of adjudicator. Ginsburg and Sotomayor joined only Part III of Breyer's writing, on the remedy. Sotomayor separately dissented, joined by Ginsburg, from the constitutional officer holding. Those joins distinguish agreement that the challenged appointment was unlawful from agreement with the Court's constitutional rationale and complete remedy.

## Freytag supplied the controlling comparison

Germaine required a continuing position established by law; Buckley required significant authority exercised pursuant to federal law. Everyone agreed that SEC ALJs occupied continuing statutory offices: they received career appointments, and statutes supplied their duties, salary, and appointment framework. Rather than formulate an exhaustive definition of significant authority, the majority applied Freytag, which had treated Tax Court special trial judges as officers. Court slip 5–8.

The comparison was functional. Both groups took testimony, conducted trials, decided evidentiary questions, and enforced discovery compliance, exercising substantial discretion in shaping an adversarial proceeding. Both also prepared findings, legal conclusions, and proposed remedies. An STJ's proposed decision in a major Tax Court case always required a regular judge's adoption. An SEC initial decision could itself become final when the Commission declined review and entered the requisite order. The majority treated the SEC arrangement as providing at least as much independent effect as the arrangement upheld in Freytag. Court slip 8–10.

The defending amicus argued that STJs had stronger contempt powers and that their findings enjoyed a regulatory presumption of correctness absent from SEC rules. The Court found neither difference decisive. Excluding a participant or suspending counsel provided substantial enforcement power even without imprisonment or fines. Freytag had not made a particular degree of review deference part of its officer analysis; in practice the SEC also respected ALJ credibility findings, ordinarily rejecting them only upon overwhelming contrary evidence. The majority did not decide that every one of Freytag's listed powers was individually necessary. Court slip 10–11.

Nor did it accept final decisionmaking authority as an indispensable prerequisite. Freytag's primary analysis rested on significant adjudicative duties and discretion even where the STJ could not issue the final decision. Its alternative observation that STJs could decide some smaller matters reinforced, rather than displaced, that analysis. Court slip 7–8 & n.4. The distinction between principal and inferior officers was not disputed here: the litigants treated the ALJs as inferior officers whom the Commission could constitutionally appoint. Court slip 5 n.3.

## A new hearing before another decisionmaker

Under Ryder, a timely challenger to an adjudicating officer's appointment was entitled to relief through a hearing before a properly appointed official. Lucia had preserved his challenge before the agency and throughout judicial review. The Court additionally required another decisionmaker because Elliot had already heard the evidence and decided the merits. It did not expect him to approach the controversy as if that earlier adjudication had never occurred. The remedy also encouraged litigants to raise appointment objections by providing a meaningful new hearing. Court slip 12–13 & n.5.

This was a qualified requirement. The Court did not require a replacement officer for every conceivable appointment violation. Other ALJs or the Commission were available here; if the defect instead concerned the Commission itself and no substitute existed, the rule of necessity would presumably permit it to rehear the case. The Court also declined to decide whether the SEC's November 2017 ratification of earlier ALJ appointments was valid. The Commission might hear the case itself or use an ALJ independently appointed through a constitutional procedure. Court slip 12–13 nn.5–6.

## The separate writings proposed competing boundaries

Thomas and Gorsuch agreed that Freytag controlled, but advocated a broader original meaning for future cases: federal civil officials continuously responsible for a statutory duty were officers regardless of the duty's importance. Thomas pointed to early ministerial positions, including clerks and customs personnel, as evidence that significant discretion was not historically essential. He limited his discussion to officers versus employees, expressing no view on the principal/inferior distinction or other constitutional uses of the word officer. Thomas concurrence slip 1–4 & nn.1–2.

Breyer would decide the case under the APA's direction that each agency appoint its ALJs. For the SEC, the relevant agency was the Commission itself. No published order delegated appointment authority to staff, and constitutional avoidance further favored reading its general delegation power to exclude such appointments. Other agencies' statutes and ALJ functions could differ. Breyer slip 1–3.

Breyer also considered appointment and removal interdependent. The APA protected ALJs through good cause determined by the Merit Systems Protection Board, whose members themselves enjoyed removal protections. Treating ALJs as officers might, if Free Enterprise Fund applied, undermine the adjudicative independence Congress had deliberately created. He did not hold that those protections were unconstitutional; he emphasized possible distinctions involving adjudicative functions and the strength of the protections. His constitutional approach would give substantial weight to Congress's statutory design in classifying positions. Breyer slip 3–13.

In Part III, joined by Ginsburg and Sotomayor, Breyer disputed excluding Elliot from the rehearing. Ordinary reversals frequently return cases to the same judge, and the appointment error implied no misconduct or incapacity. He would at least leave that question for consideration on remand rather than decide an inadequately argued remedial issue immediately. Breyer slip 13–14.

Sotomayor and Ginsburg would require authority to make final binding decisions as a component of significant authority. They viewed SEC ALJs as recommenders subject to plenary, de novo agency review; even an unreviewed initial decision required the Commission's finality order and became its action. They reconciled Freytag through STJs' ability to decide some cases finally, rejecting its broader discussion as unnecessary to the result. The majority expressly disagreed with that reading. Sotomayor dissent slip 1–5 & final starred note; Court slip 8 n.4.

## Assigned material and limits

The assigned material is an editorial discussion in note 2 on printed 153–154, physical PDF 83–84 of the September LRS casebook. It places Lucia beside Freytag and the broader Thomas–Mascott understanding of officer status. Its description of the majority declining that broader suggestion means the Court did not adopt it; the actual opinion principally left a more elaborate general test for another day. The remaining discussion of departments, historical customs officials, and new duties belongs to surrounding instructional material.

The complete official 39-page slip opinion was read, including the Court's six numbered notes, Thomas's two notes, Breyer's entire fourteen-page writing, and Sotomayor's five-page dissent and starred note. The syllabus was distinguished from judicial reasoning. The brief describes the 2018 holding and its stated reservations; it does not certify later developments in removal law or current administrative appointment practices.
