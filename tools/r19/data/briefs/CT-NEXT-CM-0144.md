# Vitex Manufacturing Corp. v. Caribtex Corp.
377 F.2d 795 (3d Cir. 1967) | Contracts | Fixed overhead in lost-profit damages

## A reopened plant received no cloth

Vitex chemically shower-proofed imported wool in the Virgin Islands. The regulatory setting created a commercial opportunity: foreign goods sufficiently increased in value through island processing could enter the continental United States without otherwise substantial tariffs. Local quotas limited each processor's output. Caribtex imported cloth, arranged for processing, and exported the finished goods. In autumn 1963, Vitex had unused quota but no customers and closed its plant. Caribtex then acquired Italian wool and negotiated with Vitex in New York. Despite conflicting accounts of the negotiations, the district court found an agreement to process 125,000 yards. 377 F.2d at 796–797.

Vitex reopened its plant, recalled workers, ordered chemicals, and prepared to perform. Caribtex supplied no cloth despite repeated demands, apparently because it doubted whether customs officials would accord the processed wool duty-free treatment. Vitex sued for its lost profits, and the district court, sitting without a jury, awarded $21,114 plus interest. Chief Judge Staley's appellate opinion sustained the damages findings and addressed whether fixed overhead had to be deducted. Id. at 796–798.

The reported numbers contain an unresolved inconsistency. The opinion states a price of twenty-six cents per yard, yet uses $31,250 as the gross amount from which $10,136 in costs was deducted to produce the $21,114 award. Multiplying 125,000 by twenty-six cents gives $32,500; $31,250 instead corresponds to twenty-five cents. The assigned scan itself prints “26 [25?]” cents. This brief retains the court's actual award and calculation rather than silently replacing either figure or claiming that the source establishes a corrected contract price. Id. at 797; assigned printed959.

## Proof of losses caused by repudiation

Caribtex attacked several components of the estimated costs. The appellate court found substantial record support for the trial judge's computation and emphasized that exact costs were difficult to establish because Caribtex repudiated before processing occurred. The breaching party could not benefit from uncertainty it had itself caused. That proposition did not dispense with proof: the court expressly found a sufficient evidentiary basis for substantial damages. Id. at 797–798.

The first judicial footnote resolves a specific apparent arithmetic problem. The original transcript seemed to say the plant required 37,000 gallons of fuel oil per week, although the witness's dollar estimate used 7,000 gallons. The official reporter checked his notes and certified that the witness had actually said 7,000. The supposed $4,500 understatement over three weeks consequently disappeared. This was a correction to the preserved testimony, not a judicial decision that a genuine cost discrepancy could be ignored. Id. at 797 & n.1.

## The difference between allocation and avoided expense

Caribtex first raised its overhead objection at appellate oral argument. Overhead included continuing business expenses such as executive and clerical salaries, property taxes, and administration. Caribtex's theory was that a pro rata share of annual overhead should count as a cost of this transaction, reducing recoverable profit. Vitex had not separately demanded overhead reimbursement, but the court recognized that omitting such a deduction effectively preserved the transaction's contribution toward those expenses. Id. at 798.

The court adopted the better view, while acknowledging contrary authority, that fixed overhead normally need not be deducted when calculating profits lost on a particular contract. The governing compensatory principle allowed losses incurred and gains prevented, less savings made possible by the breach. Because nonperformance did not save these overhead expenses, subtracting them would undercompensate Vitex. Id.

The plant's closure made the distinction concrete. Without the Caribtex transaction, Vitex would still have borne its existing overhead. Reopening and processing the wool would add labor, chemicals, fuel, and other direct expenses. Those additional costs were properly deducted. Indeed, the second footnote clarifies that the trial court charged all expenses directly associated with reactivation and actual processing; “overhead” here did not conceal omitted labor or reopening costs. The unchanged fixed expenses were neither attributable to nor increased by this particular contract. Id. at 798 & n.2.

## Why business accounting did not change the remedy

Staley anticipated the response that a business must price its contracts to cover overhead as well as direct costs and net earnings. The court agreed that allocating overhead to transactions can be useful for pricing and planning. It denied that this analytical allocation necessarily identified a cost saved by breach. Expenses paid from overall business receipts do not acquire a direct causal relationship to every individual transaction merely because an accountant spreads them across the year's output. Id. at 798–799.

The court also supplied an alternative compensatory explanation. Even if overhead allocation were accepted, the contract's lost contribution would leave the remaining business to bear a larger share of fixed expense. Its hypothetical business had $10,000 overhead and five similar transactions, each contributing $2,000. Losing one transaction would spread the same expense across four, raising their allocated burden to $2,500 apiece. Those were illustrative numbers, not Vitex's established overhead. Commercial parties could foresee the loss of the promised contribution alongside the loss of clear profit, making that contribution compensable as a loss incurred. Id. at 799.

The UCC reinforced the conclusion as persuasive authority. Its seller's lost-profit provision expressly included reasonable overhead when ordinary market damages would not put the seller in the performance position. This processing agreement was not controlled by the Code, however. The opinion used the Code to support its reasoning rather than treating §2-708(2) as the directly governing statute. Id.

## The unconscionability defense and judgment

Caribtex separately argued that the agreement was unconscionable because Vitex stood to earn a large profit while Caribtex bore the customs risk. The court rejected the argument on the bargaining record. Negotiations were extensive, the parties apparently had equal strength, Vitex was not the only available island processor, and Caribtex had obtained successive substantial price reductions. The agreement was therefore not an adhesion contract merely because the allocation of risk proved unfavorable. Id. at 799–800.

The Third Circuit affirmed the $21,114 judgment plus interest. Staley wrote for a panel also including Judges Maris and Coffin, the latter sitting by designation; no separate writing was reported. The decision's treatment of overhead rests on the finding that these expenses remained fixed and unsaved by breach. It does not establish that every expense labeled overhead must always be recoverable without a factual inquiry into whether performance would have incurred it or breach avoided it.
