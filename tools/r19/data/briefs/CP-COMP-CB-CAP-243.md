# Gibbons v. Pepper

**1 Ld. Raym. 38 (K.B. 1695); assigned parallel citation 91 Eng. Rep. 922**  
**Court of King's Bench | Easter Term, 1695**  
**Civil Procedure - confession and avoidance, the general issue, and involuntary injury**  
**Assigned:** CivPro PDF 297, printed 558, own-caption account within the discussion of pleading “color.”

## Facts as pleaded and the procedural dispute

Gibbons sued Pepper in trespass for assault and battery after Pepper's horse ran over him. Pepper pleaded that he was riding along the King's highway when his frightened horse ran away, beyond his ability to stop it. Several people, including Gibbons, were standing in the way. Pepper said that he warned them to take care, but Gibbons remained where he was, and the horse ran over him against Pepper's will. This account was Pepper's pleading; the reported judgment does not rest on a jury's finding that his description was true. (Ames reproduction, 17.)

Pepper framed those circumstances as a justification of the alleged trespass. Gibbons demurred, asking the court to decide the legal sufficiency of that response. The issue was therefore not simply whether involuntary contact might defeat liability. It was whether Pepper could present facts denying his responsibility for a battery as a plea that purported to admit and justify the battery.

Serjeant Darnall, for Pepper, argued that a justification demonstrating inevitable accident, not caused by the defendant's negligence, should entitle him to judgment. He cited Weaver v. Ward. Northey, for Gibbons, distinguished a proper justification, which first admits a battery and then explains why it is justified, from Pepper's account, which denied that a battery legally attributable to Pepper had occurred at all. (17.)

## Holding and the court's explanation

The whole court agreed with Gibbons's objection. On Pepper's proposed circumstances, there was no battery by Pepper to confess and avoid. The facts instead could be presented in evidence under a plea of the **general issue**, disputing the plaintiff's case. Pepper's potentially useful defense did not become a valid special justification merely because it was spelled out in detail. (17–18.)

The court used two illustrations to distinguish the person responsible for an impact from a person whose body or movements were merely used as its instrument. If a third person whipped a rider's horse, causing it to run away and strike someone, that third person, rather than the involuntary rider, committed the battery. If the rider caused the event by spurring the horse, however, the rider would be responsible. Likewise, when A takes B's hand and uses it to strike C, A is the trespasser, rather than B. These are the court's explanatory hypotheticals, not findings that someone actually whipped Pepper's horse or seized his hand. (17–18.)

The examples explain both halves of the ruling. Pepper's facts, if established in the appropriate manner, could negate a battery attributable to him. Precisely because they negated it, they did not supply new matter excusing a prima facie battery he otherwise admitted. The substantive distinction between acting and being an involuntary instrument produced a procedural distinction between denying the claim and admitting but avoiding it.

## Judgment and limits

The court gave **judgment for Gibbons, the plaintiff**, on the defective plea. The per curiam passage expressly says Pepper could have offered the same circumstances in evidence under the general issue. Gibbons's victory consequently should not be described as a factual determination that Pepper negligently controlled the horse, intentionally struck Gibbons, or failed to give the alleged warning. Nor did Pepper win merely because his pleaded account described an involuntary accident. He lost on the manner in which he presented that account. (18.)

The reproduction states that the whole court agreed, but does not identify a numerical vote, an individual opinion author, a dissent, or a separate concurrence. It gives no judicial footnotes for Gibbons. The numbered editorial note visible at the bottom of the preceding page belongs to **Cole v. Turner**, whose separate report appears above Gibbons; it is not a Gibbons note.

## Connection to the assignment

The textbook explains that a plea in confession and avoidance had to give “color”: it had to recognize an apparent right in the opposing party before offering matter that defeated it. Its Gibbons account illustrates the risk of using that form to plead facts that instead denied the opposing party's claim. A traverse or general-issue denial preserved a different procedural route. This is the historical lesson of the case; modern pleading permissions should not be projected into its 1695 judgment.
