# Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer

343 U.S. 579 (1952) | Legislation and the Regulatory State | Supreme Court of the United States | June 2, 1952

## Events, parties and the controversy

The steel companies and the United Steelworkers were negotiating new collective bargaining agreements during the Korean conflict. Their existing agreements expired December 31, 1951. The union announced a strike, federal mediation failed, and President Truman referred the dispute to the Wage Stabilization Board on December 22. The Board recommended a settlement, but the parties did not settle. The union then announced an industry-wide strike beginning at 12:01 a.m. April 9, 1952. Truman regarded uninterrupted steel production as indispensable to weapons, other defense materials and national security. Shortly before the strike was to begin, he issued Executive Order 10340 on April 8, directing Secretary of Commerce Charles Sawyer to take possession of most steel mills and operate them. Sawyer immediately ordered the companies' presidents to act as operating managers for the United States. They complied under protest. 582–584.

The order was more than an instruction to buy steel for the military. It authorized government possession and operation of private businesses, determination of employment terms, regulations and delegation of authority. Existing management, business payments and obligations continued unless the Secretary directed otherwise; the Secretary could return a plant when further possession was unnecessary and effective future operation assured. Its asserted foundation was the President's constitutional and statutory powers in the aggregate, rather than a particular seizure statute whose conditions had been satisfied. The complete order appears at 589–592.

Truman reported the seizure to Congress April 9 and again April 21. Congress had not acted on those reports when the Court decided the case. That immediate inaction was not the only relevant congressional evidence: existing statutes prescribed ways to address defense procurement and labor emergencies, and Congress had rejected a general plant-seizure proposal when adopting Taft-Hartley. The central disagreement among the opinions was what those enactments meant for the President's constitutional authority. 583, 585–589, 598–610, 639–640, 701–708.

## Litigation, arguments and exact disposition

The companies sued Sawyer for declarations that the orders were invalid and for preliminary and permanent injunctions. They characterized seizure as lawmaking entrusted to Congress. The Government asserted inherent presidential authority, historical practice and the combined executive, Take Care and Commander in Chief powers to avert a national catastrophe. It separately argued that a preliminary injunction was improper because the companies could obtain compensation and had not shown irreparable injury. The District Court rejected those arguments and issued a preliminary injunction April 30; the D.C. Circuit stayed it the same day. The Supreme Court granted certiorari May 3 and heard argument May 12–13. 582–585.

The Court held the constitutional issue ripe at this preliminary stage and held the seizure unauthorized. Its formal judgment was **affirmance of the District Court's judgment**, sustaining the preliminary injunction, rather than affirmance of the Court of Appeals' stay. The reported lower decisions were 103 F. Supp. 569 and 197 F.2d 582. The two docketed matters were the companies' petition, No. 744, and Sawyer's cross-petition, No. 745. 579–581, 584–585, 589.

The result was **6–3**. Black wrote the Court's opinion. Frankfurter, Douglas, Jackson and Burton joined it while writing separately; Clark concurred in the judgment alone. Vinson dissented, joined by Reed and Minton. There are five separate concurrences and one three-Justice dissent. Jackson's familiar categories belong to his concurrence; Black's opinion supplies the Court's stated reasoning. 589, 593, 629–630, 634, 655, 660, 667.

## The Court's reasoning

The Government's equitable objection did not require postponing the constitutional question. Prior cases cast doubt on whether the Court of Claims could compensate an unlawful taking by officials. More fundamentally, seizure and operation of ongoing businesses would cause present and future injuries difficult or impossible to measure. The District Court could decide the legality of the orders on this record without forcing the owners to wait for uncertain damages proceedings. 584–585.

For Black, presidential authority had to come from Congress or the Constitution. No statute expressly or fairly impliedly authorized this seizure. The Selective Service Act and Defense Production Act permitted property acquisition under conditions the Government admitted were unmet. Describing the latter process as cumbersome did not supply missing authority. Congress had also rejected emergency plant-seizure authority during consideration of Taft-Hartley, choosing mediation, inquiry, reports and temporary strike injunctions while ultimately preserving the right to strike. 585–586 & nn.2–5.

The Commander in Chief power did not allow domestic seizure of private property to stop production losses from a labor dispute. Authorities concerning commanders in a theater of military operations did not decide this situation. The Take Care duty presupposed laws to execute; it did not confer legislative power. Truman's order prescribed his own policy and authorized rules to carry it out, rather than executing a congressional policy through congressionally prescribed means. Congress could authorize takings, regulate labor relations and prescribe wage or dispute-settlement rules, but those legislative powers were not subject to presidential control. Assertions that earlier Presidents had seized enterprises did not transfer Congress's constitutional lawmaking authority to the Executive. The seizure therefore could not stand. 587–589.

## Frankfurter: congressional policy and historical gloss

Frankfurter joined Black's application while emphasizing a more flexible separation-of-powers analysis. He first addressed equitable avoidance: compensation could not translate all seizure consequences into money, and permitting a claimed public emergency automatically to foreclose review would effectively insulate the very assertions of power most needing examination. He nevertheless insisted on deciding only the concrete dispute, reserving situations involving no relevant legislation or a genuinely limited seizure automatically expiring without congressional approval. 593–597.

His detailed statutory inquiry found an affirmative congressional choice against general seizure authority. Repeated seizure enactments contained limits concerning duration, emergencies, refusal to fulfill government orders, agency responsibility and compensation. Taft-Hartley's legislative history reserved new seizure authorization for Congress in a particular emergency. Defense Production Act mediation and the subsequent Wage Stabilization Board arrangements did not reverse that choice: voluntary settlement machinery was not a hidden authorization for coercive government possession. A court balancing equities also could not discard Congress's allocation of competing public interests. 598–610 & nn.1–18.

Frankfurter allowed sustained historical practice to inform the meaning of executive power. A systematic, unbroken and previously unquestioned practice known to Congress could supply constitutional gloss. But he distinguished the long-established public-land withdrawals in Midwest Oil from the asserted seizure practice here. Wilson's seizures were made or purportedly made under statutes; many Roosevelt seizures were statutory or occurred after a declared war. The three potentially comparable prewar seizures in 1941 were too isolated to establish the requisite construction or congressional acquiescence. He did not decide their validity. Distributed power entails delay and friction, but that cost protects against arbitrary government. 610–614 & nn.19–20.

His appendices support this historical argument rather than creating additional holdings. Appendix I, 615–619, compares eighteen listed enactments or amendments by duration, authority, conditions, employment terms and compensation, including its railroad-termination star note. Appendix II, 620–628, traces Civil War and World War seizures and subsequent labor-related seizures, separately listing nonlabor seizures and preserving unknown information. Its notes1–11 explain sources, statutory references, contested legality and qualifications. All table rows and notes are part of the full source, although omitted from the assigned account.

## Douglas: emergency, taking and appropriations

Douglas agreed that the crisis was serious but distinguished an occasion for exercising power from a source of new power. Deciding which branch could act fastest would evade the constitutional allocation. Seizing an industry to settle a labor controversy was a taking requiring just compensation, even if temporary. Congress controlled revenue and payment; Douglas therefore reasoned that only Congress could authorize condemnation or subsequently ratify what the President had done. His notes distinguish ratification of extraordinary action and takings under existing authority from this unauthorized seizure. 629–632 & nn.1–2.

Presidential leadership, recommendations and persuasion remained substantial, but military command did not create general authority over civilian affairs. Political stalemate was a risk of the chosen structure. Allowing this President to use seizure for a wage increase would also authorize a later President to use it against labor. Changing that allocation required constitutional change rather than a judicial accommodation to immediate convenience. 632–634.

## Jackson: fluctuating authority and application of the three categories

Jackson joined both opinion and judgment. He warned against judging permanent executive power by the attractiveness of the current policy or occupant. Constitutional provisions operate as an integrated arrangement of separated yet interdependent institutions. Presidential authority consequently varies with its relationship to Congress, which he organized into three practical situations. 634–635.

1. With express or implied congressional authorization, the President combines his independent powers with all authority Congress can delegate. A seizure in that posture receives the strongest presumption and interpretive latitude; an invalid act usually indicates that the Federal Government as a whole lacks the power. 635–637 & nn.1–2.
2. Without congressional grant or denial, the President relies on independent powers in a zone of possible concurrent authority or uncertain allocation. Inertia or acquiescence may practically enable action, but this is not a rule that silence always authorizes it. The test depends on events and circumstances. 637 & n.3.
3. Against Congress's expressed or implied will, the President's authority is at its lowest. Sustaining exclusive executive control requires finding that Congress cannot constitutionally regulate the subject; the executive claim must be scrutinized carefully. 637–638 & n.4.

This seizure fit the third situation. The Government conceded no statutory authorization, eliminating the first. Congress had supplied three inconsistent policies—government procurement orders, condemnation and labor-emergency procedures—so the field was not open for the second. Their prerequisites were not invoked. Sustaining the order would therefore require recognizing seizure of strike-bound industries as an exclusive presidential domain beyond Congress's control, which Jackson rejected. His note2 distinguishes Curtiss-Wright's congressional delegation from unilateral action; notes5–8 qualify historical analogies and identify the controlling statutes. 638–640.

Neither a bulk reading of executive power nor the military title supplied that exclusive authority. Sending forces abroad could not itself enlarge the President's mastery of domestic affairs. Jackson expressly declined to decide the Korean enterprise's legal status. Congress controlled raising, supporting and supplying forces and limited domestic military use. He favored wide latitude for lawful command directed outward, but not for intervention in a lawful internal labor disagreement. The Take Care duty reached only as far as law allowed. 640–646 & nn.9–15.

Jackson also rejected undefined emergency authority based on partisan executive assertions. His own prior advocacy was not binding precedent: note17 distinguishes North American Aviation's government contracts, property, consent and insurrection-like circumstances. Comparative emergency regimes showed why independent control over extraordinary authority mattered. Congress already could activate substantial emergency powers by statute. Presidential political influence, communications and party leadership also supplied practical power beyond the constitutional text; adding unilateral legislative authority was unnecessary. Congress must exercise its own responsibilities timely to preserve them. 646–655 & nn.16–27.

## Burton and Clark: statutory procedures control this emergency

Burton joined the opinion and judgment. Congress had anticipated this kind of emergency and prescribed mechanisms, reserving further seizure authorization to itself. The President's mediation alternative did not become seizure authority when unsuccessful, even if comparable to the Taft-Hartley delay. Neither condemnation procedures nor compulsory defense orders had been used. Burton reserved imminent invasion, threatened attack and total-war military-command situations: his conclusion concerned this emergency and its governing statutes. 655–660 & nn.1–7.

Clark joined the judgment, expressly accepting extensive independent presidential authority in grave emergencies when Congress had not supplied controlling procedures. Little v. Barreme illustrated the decisive qualification: a statutory specification of enforcement displaced a different presidential route. Here the Defense Production Act allowed mediation and ordinary condemnation; Taft-Hartley provided an eighty-day injunction procedure and report to Congress; the Selective Service Act permitted seizure after a producer refused or failed to fill properly placed defense orders. 660–665 & nn.1–13.

Mediation had postponed the strike ninety-nine days, but functional similarity to Taft-Hartley's delay did not make either statute authorize this seizure. The Government had not explained why it could not promptly use the actual compulsory-order statute. Clark's note14 gives historical examples of rapid seizures following compulsory orders and explains that an order need not occupy a plant's entire capacity. Presidential emergency discretion did not permit disregarding Congress's available methods. 665–667 & nn.14–15.

## Vinson, Reed and Minton: preserving Congress's programs

The dissent would reverse the District Court's injunction. It emphasized uncontroverted government affidavits concerning weapons, ammunition, defense facilities, scarce inventories and the consequences of an industry-wide shutdown. Neither an ordinary small-business seizure nor a harmless short stoppage described this record. The dissent assumed equitable review and, without deciding the question, assumed courts could examine the President's emergency finding; it regarded the actual evidence as supporting that finding. 667–679 & nn.1–25.

Vinson characterized the action as execution of Congress's defense procurement and anti-inflation programs rather than unilateral creation of new legislative goals. Steel shutdown would defeat procurement; granting the industry's demanded price increase would defeat stabilization. The President took temporary possession, immediately reported it and offered to abide by Congress's response. Just compensation would accompany a lawful taking. A Constitution intended to survive crises did not require presidential paralysis until Congress supplied a particular method. 679–683, 701–704.

The dissent's extensive history treated neutrality, treaty execution, Lincoln's actions, Neagle, Debs, Midwest Oil and industrial seizures as evidence of initiative in preserving legal programs pending legislative action. It disputed the majority's reading of earlier seizure statutes and congressional responses. It also regarded Taft-Hartley as a discretionary peacetime route alongside the later defense-dispute machinery, not an exclusive path or an implied prohibition. Congress had retained the Board's disputes functions after debate, and mediation already delayed the strike longer than the eighty-day injunction period. 683–701 & nn.26–82; 704–708 & nn.87–94.

This was a contested construction of congressional policy, not an assertion that the President could ignore an express prohibition or act indefinitely against Congress. The dissent regarded temporary preservation of the legislative programs as constitutionally permissible and the majority's decision as an unwarranted restriction on necessary executive initiative. Its concluding note95 cites Sterling concerning executive emergency findings; the proposed reversal appears at710. 701–710 & nn.83–95.

## Assigned account and limits

The assigned passage, physical13–16/printed257–260, is an editorial account under the Youngstown heading, not an excerpt reproducing all judicial writings. It accurately emphasizes the competing statutory readings, Jackson's framework and Frankfurter's gloss. It groups Burton and Clark as receptive to independent emergency authority; the full opinions require distinguishing Clark's affirmative position from Burton's reservation of catastrophic situations. Likewise, the account's general discussion of Commander in Chief authority should not erase the dissent's broader reliance on the combined constitutional role and execution of Congress's programs. The actual opinion does not decide the validity of committing forces to Korea or every future emergency seizure.

The decision establishes the invalidity of this seizure and the appropriateness of its preliminary restraint. It does not make every instance of congressional silence an invitation, turn every rejected proposal into an exclusive constitutional limit, or treat each separate Justice's reasoning as a holding of the Court.
