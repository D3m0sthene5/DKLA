# Gottlieb v. Tropicana Hotel and Casino
109 F. Supp. 2d 324 (E.D. Pa. 2000) | Contracts

## Court and posture
District Judge Bartle decided Tropicana's summary-judgment motion on July 5, 2000. This was a federal diversity action in the Eastern District of Pennsylvania, rather than an appeal. Rena and Sheldon Gottlieb claimed that Tropicana refused to pay a $1 million promotional prize. Tropicana denied that they won and had asserted third-party claims against its insurer and two insurance brokers under a prize-indemnity policy. The ruling addressed Tropicana's motion against the plaintiffs, without resolving the third-party coverage dispute. (109 F. Supp. 2d at 326–327.)

The motion was granted against Sheldon on all counts and against Rena on Count IV, the Pennsylvania consumer-protection claim. It was denied against Rena on breach of contract, misrepresentation, and fraud and deceit. Those denials left factual disputes for a jury; they did not award the $1 million or establish that the wheel reached the jackpot. The opinion and its accompanying order contain five notes and no separate writing. (Id. at 327 n.2, 331–332.)

## Facts and disputed events
The uncontested facts placed the Pennsylvania-resident Gottliebs in Atlantic City during summer 1999. After dinner with friends on July 24, they visited Tropicana. The casino's free Diamond Club required an application and identification, collected contact information, and issued each member a uniquely numbered card. Swiping the card during play supplied information about gambling habits, which the casino's marketing department used to tailor promotions. Rena had already been a member for several years. She went to the Fun House Million Dollar Wheel and waited approximately five minutes. Members received one free spin per day, with a possible $1 million prize. Although the casino advertised in both states, there was no evidence that the Gottliebs saw the advertisements. (Id. at 327.)

The parties agreed that Rena presented her card, an attendant swiped it, and she pressed a button that started the wheel. They sharply disputed what followed. Rena claimed it landed on the million-dollar prize and that the attendant immediately swiped another card and restarted the wheel, producing two show tickets instead. Tropicana claimed the wheel simply landed on the lesser prize and denied both a jackpot result and intervention. At summary judgment, reasonable inferences had to be drawn for the nonmoving party. Neither the casino's account nor the plaintiffs' account became an adjudicated finding merely because the opinion described it. (Id. at 326–327, 330–331.)

## Consideration and choice of law
Tropicana argued that New Jersey law precluded consideration for free promotional participation. Rena urged Pennsylvania law, under which she argued the participation was sufficient. The court applied Pennsylvania's conflicts rules as the diversity forum's rules, but first examined whether the substantive laws actually conflicted. Both states required a bargained exchange that could consist of a benefit to the promisor or a detriment to the promisee. (Id. at 327–328.)

Tropicana's New Jersey authority was an unpublished 1985 trial-court ruling concerning whether promotional games constituted illegal gambling. That ruling applied the statutory requirement that participants transfer something of value. It did not decide that participation could never furnish the minimum consideration supporting a contract. The court instead relied on New Jersey's Lucky Calendar decision, which recognized that completing and delivering an entry coupon could supply requested inconvenience and benefit the business through publicity and patronage. Pennsylvania's Cobaugh decision likewise recognized promotional publicity and an act the participant had no prior duty to perform as consideration. The two states therefore agreed on the pertinent minimum-contract-consideration principle, eliminating any need to choose between conflicting rules. The court's fourth note cautioned that the older Lottery Act at issue in Lucky Calendar had since been repealed. (Id. at 328–329 & nn.3–4.)

Rena's acts were requested as part of the opportunity to win: going to the casino, waiting, presenting her card and permitting collection of gambling information, and participating in the casino's entertainment. The promotion sought commercial patronage and excitement. Those requested detriments and benefits supported an exchange even though no admission charge or price was paid for the spin. The distinction from a merely conditional gift rests on what the casino sought to induce, rather than the monetary value of opening an account or waiting in line viewed in isolation. (Id. at 329–330.)

## Illegality and the unresolved jackpot dispute
Tropicana alternatively argued that the very consideration supporting the contract made it an unlawful lottery. The court rejected the equation between ordinary contract consideration and the more restrictive statutory phrase something of value. It examined the New Jersey Criminal Code's lottery definition and credited a 1983 New Jersey Attorney General opinion explaining that the legislature intended to exclude the sort of personal inconvenience sufficient for a contract. The Attorney General's role in casino administration and gambling enforcement gave that interpretation substantial weight. Because Rena paid no statutory something of value for the chance, the contract was not unenforceable as an illegal lottery. (Id. at 330.)

That conclusion established enforceability, not that Rena had performed the winning condition. Tropicana relied on computer records to deny a win, while Rena relied in part on her testimony and her husband's eyewitness testimony. Resolving that conflict was a jury task, so summary judgment on her contract count was denied. (Id. at 330–331.)

## Remaining claims and exact order
The misrepresentation and fraud counts alleged a representation that patrons who spun the wheel and landed on the million-dollar symbol would receive the prize. Tropicana disputed reliance and falsity. Rena's trip, wait, card presentation, and spin sufficiently supported reliance at this stage. Her evidence also created a genuine factual dispute about the claimed winning result, defeating summary judgment on falsity. Counts II and III accordingly survived without an ultimate finding of fraud. (Id. at 331.)

Count IV failed for a different reason. Pennsylvania's Unfair Trade Practices and Consumer Protection Law provided a private claim to a person purchasing or leasing goods or services for personal, family, or household purposes and suffering the requisite loss. Rena had neither purchased nor leased anything in the ordinary statutory sense: the spin cost no money, and the consideration she provided was not its monetary equivalent. The contract-consideration holding thus did not make her a statutory purchaser. Her authority concerned an Attorney General enforcement action, which did not establish eligibility for a private action. The court did not reach whether the Pennsylvania statute applied to conduct occurring outside Pennsylvania. (Id. at 331–332 & n.5.)

Sheldon's claims failed because he had not participated in the game, and Tropicana's motion against him was uncontested. The New Jersey consumer-fraud claim in Count V had been voluntarily dismissed. The accompanying order entered judgment for Tropicana against Sheldon on all counts and against Rena on Count IV, denying the remainder of the motion against her. (Id. at 327 n.2, 332.)

## Assigned account and limits
The complete assigned Note (3), “The Million-Dollar Swipe,” occupies C&M physical PDF 129, printed 83. Its consideration quotation accurately reflects pages 329–330, but its account describes recent club enrollment rather than Rena's established membership. It also calls the remaining proceedings a remand and describes a marker falling off the jackpot position. The actual district-court ruling contains no appellate remand; it denies part of a summary-judgment motion and describes the competing accounts as an allegedly restarted wheel versus an initial lesser-prize result. This brief covers that July 2000 ruling and does not certify an eventual jury outcome or present law. Separate source review is tracked in the accompanying register.
