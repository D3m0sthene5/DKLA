# Southworth v. Oliver
284 Or. 361, 587 P.2d 994 (Or. 1978) | Contracts

## Decision and procedural posture

The Oregon Supreme Court affirmed a decree requiring Joseph and Arlene Oliver to sell Southworth approximately 2,933 acres of ranch land. Although their letter described enclosed material as information and went to four neighbors, the prior negotiations and definite sale terms made it an offer to Southworth, which he accepted. The missing security provisions were subordinate details that equity could supply. The sellers' statute-of-frauds objection failed because they raised it for the first time in the Supreme Court, rather than because the court found the writing sufficient under the statute. Tongue wrote for the five-member participating court; the report contains no separate opinion. (284 Or. at 361–363, 372–384.)

Southworth originally sought a declaration that the Olivers were obligated to sell the land. After a decree of specific performance, they appealed on formation, acceptance, and definiteness grounds and also filed a general demurrer in the Supreme Court asserting a statute-of-frauds defect. This was a review of a completed trial and decree, not a decision merely finding a sufficiently pleaded contract. (Id. at 363, 380–383.)

## Facts and negotiations

The Olivers owned ranches in Oregon's Bear Valley and John Day valley. In 1976, Joseph, Arlene, and their son jointly decided to reduce their operation by selling some Bear Valley land and Forest Service grazing permits. After consulting his accountant and attorney, Joseph determined that the land sale had to provide installment payments for income-tax reasons. Southworth, another cattle rancher, owned adjoining land and had long wanted the property. (Id. at 363.)

On May 20, Joseph approached Southworth about selling the ranch land. According to testimony the Supreme Court credited, he suggested that Southworth would be interested in the land while another neighbor, Clyde Holliday, would be interested in the permits. The two identified the proposed land on a map but did not yet fix price or payment terms. Their understanding was that Joseph would determine the property's value and price while Southworth investigated financing. Joseph's account emphasized providing information and giving neighbors a first chance, but he acknowledged that the mapped land was the property eventually offered to the four neighbors. There was no discussion that buying the land required buying the permits. Southworth later spoke to Holliday, who expressed interest in permits and a possible land exchange rather than purchasing the offered tract. (Id. at 364–365.)

On June 13, Southworth telephoned to confirm that the sale plans continued. Joseph said they did, explaining that he was waiting for the assessor's information. Southworth told him that financing was ready. A June 17 letter then enclosed information concerning the ranch sales previously discussed, explained that the prices reflected the assessor's market valuations, and invited questions. One enclosure identified the approximately 2,933 acres near Seneca, listed $306,409 for land and $18,010 for improvements, and stated the $324,419 total. The terms were 29 percent down, the balance over five years at eight percent interest, and a sale date to be negotiated for December 1, 1976, or January 1, 1977. Availability followed the hay harvest and arrangements to remove hay, equipment, and supplies. The enclosure separately listed two grazing permits; another enclosure concerned approximately 6,365 acres near John Day. (Id. at 365–366.)

Arlene drafted the letter and enclosures after discussions with Joseph; he read and signed the letter. He sent the material to Southworth, Holliday, and two other neighbors. Joseph testified that he intended further negotiations and a combined transfer of property and permits. That testimony described his asserted intent, which the court distinguished from the objective meaning of his conduct. On June 21, Southworth sent a letter accepting the offer for the Bear Valley land. After Holliday reported difficulties arranging an exchange with Southworth, Joseph said he did not want to arbitrate disagreements among neighbors and might withdraw the property. The Olivers' June 24 letter denied making a firm offer, asserted that the property and permits were not offered separately, challenged the legal description, and invited further negotiation. (Id. at 366–368, 383.)

## Whether the information letter was an offer

The sellers argued that their subjective intention, the informational language, the absence of the word “offer,” the inclusion of permits, and distribution to several people showed preliminary negotiations. The court instead asked what a reasonable person in Southworth's position would understand from the words and conduct in their full setting. A quotation standing alone ordinarily is not an offer, but surrounding circumstances can turn it into a commitment that the recipient's assent concludes. Distribution to several recipients is a relevant consideration, not a categorical bar. The court adopted interpretive guides concerning the language of commitment, the identified or indefinite recipients, and the proposal's definiteness, while emphasizing that they operate within the objective test. (Id. at 368–372 & nn.1–5.)

Here the letter did not arrive unexpectedly or merely answer an unsolicited request for prices. Joseph had sought out Southworth, identified the tract, promised to determine its price, and received assurances that Southworth would arrange and then had arranged financing. The enclosure supplied that price and definite installment terms for the already mapped property. Its recipients were four specific neighbors rather than an indefinite public. Taken together, those facts reasonably communicated an offer. The labels “information” and “selling,” and the omitted word “offer,” did not outweigh the prior dealings. Nor could the sellers' undisclosed intention control the manifestation reasonably conveyed to the buyer. (Id. at 372–375 & n.6.)

## Acceptance and the grazing permits

The Olivers contended that Southworth's land-only acceptance varied from a package offer. They stressed that Forest Service permits needed commensurate land or cattle to support a transfer, and that they did not wish to sell cattle. The trial court found both that the negotiations allowed a reasonable understanding of a separate land sale and that the permit terms were insufficiently specific to constitute a firm offer for permits. The Supreme Court identified those as independent grounds; it did not need to approve every aspect of the second ground to affirm. (Id. at 374–377 & n.7.)

The decisive point was the prior discussion that Holliday might acquire the permits while Southworth acquired land. The trial court explained that a neighbor could arrange commensurate land, or the sellers might retain permits supported by remaining land or cattle. Thus Southworth could reasonably understand the offered land as separately available. Any inconsistency in finding the land terms definite but the permit terms indefinite did not defeat that conclusion. Once the separate land offer was established, the land-only response accepted it rather than counteroffering for selected components of an inseparable package. Ambiguity remained relevant to the objective interpretation, but it did not independently require rejection of that interpretation. (Id. at 375–377.)

## Definiteness and specific performance

The sellers argued that omission of an agreed security device made the installment sale unenforceable. The court distinguished an earlier case in which the preliminary agreement neither settled the manner of conveyance nor entitled the buyer to force the seller to accept installments; that buyer could pay cash instead. Southworth's bargain expressly required a down payment and five years of installments. He had no contractual right to substitute full cash payment, and the sellers had agreed to receive deferred payments. (Id. at 377–379.)

Equity may make subordinate details certain within reasonable limits when the parties have sufficiently manifested an intent to contract. The court treated the omitted security provisions as such a gap, observing that the sellers' attempt to withdraw did not arise from concern about those provisions. The decree called for a standard printed installment land-sale contract, with customary security terms. This was not the note and mortgage used in the analogous precedent. Footnote 8 describes repair and insurance, taxes and liens, default remedies, time-of-the-essence, and nonwaiver provisions. The court sustained the supplied terms; it did not announce that courts may create missing essential bargains whenever a party requests specific performance. (Id. at 379–380 & n.8.)

## Statute of frauds, disposition, and limits

The appellate demurrer asserted that Joseph had not subscribed the purported agreement, Arlene had not signed it, and the complaint did not allege written authority for Joseph to act for her. The sellers invoked a precedent stating that failure to plead a cause of action could be raised at any time. But that precedent had involved a demurrer actually filed and overruled below, unlike this case. Moreover, the statute-of-frauds defense could be waived, and an objection below could allow a plaintiff to plead and prove facts overcoming it. The court held that where no trial-court demurrer or other contention invoked the statute, the sellers could not raise it for the first time on appeal through a demurrer or otherwise. (Id. at 380–383 & nn.9–10.)

The court noted that Arlene drafted the June 17 letter, Joseph signed it as Joe, and both signed the later June 24 letter without denying joint authorization of the earlier communication. It also mentioned the buyer's expense in bringing suit in reliance on the offer. Those circumstances might or might not establish ratification, waiver, or estoppel. The court expressly declined to decide their sufficiency because the failure to raise the defense below resolved the issue. Thus the holding supplies neither a ruling that a spouse's unsigned agreement necessarily satisfies the statute nor a finding of equitable estoppel on these facts. The specific-performance decree was affirmed. (Id. at 383–384.)
