# Hadley v. Baxendale
9 Ex. 341, 156 Eng. Rep. 145 (Court of Exchequer 1854) | Contracts

## Material facts and chronology

Hadley and another proprietor operated the City Steam-Mills at Gloucester. Their steam engine’s crankshaft broke, stopping the mill on May 11. The fracture was discovered on May 12. Joyce & Co., the engineers at Greenwich who had manufactured the engine, needed the old shaft as a pattern to make a replacement that would fit the surviving machinery. The replacement’s arrival therefore depended on getting the broken shaft to Greenwich. This was a dependency peculiar to the plaintiffs’ operation, rather than an inevitable consequence whenever a carrier transported a damaged machine part. 9 Ex. at 341–344, 355–356.

On May 13 the millers sent a servant to the office of Pickford & Co., the carriers operated by the defendants. The report’s trial narrative says the servant told the clerk that the mill had stopped and that the shaft must be sent immediately. The clerk answered that a shaft received before noon would reach Greenwich the following day. The plaintiffs delivered it before noon the next day and paid £2 4s for carriage. They also asked that any necessary special entry be made to hasten delivery. Neglect delayed delivery; the new shaft consequently reached the mill several days later than it otherwise would have, prolonging the shutdown and costing the millers profits. Id. at 344.

The declaration alleged that the replacement and reopening were delayed five additional days, that the plaintiffs lost sales and profits, bought flour to serve some customers, and paid employees whom they could not put to work. It claimed £300. Those pleaded categories should not be mistaken for separate amounts independently awarded or examined by the court. Id. at 342–343. The original scan reads £300; the volume’s machine transcription misreads the initial digit as 9.

## Claims, procedure, and opposing arguments

The declaration contained two counts. The first alleged a promise to deliver on a specified second day; the plaintiffs discontinued that count before trial. The surviving count alleged a failure to use due care and diligence to deliver within a reasonable time. The defendants paid £25 into court in satisfaction of that claim. The plaintiffs replied that the deposit was insufficient. At the Gloucester Assizes, Crompton, J., left the case generally to the jury despite the carriers’ objection that lost profits were too remote. The jury awarded another £25 beyond the deposited amount. The carriers obtained a rule nisi for a new trial on the ground of misdirection. Id. at 341–345.

The millers argued that the shutdown profits were the natural and necessary result of the delay and the only loss they actually sustained. They emphasized the notice to the clerk, compared cases involving delayed mill construction and detained workmen’s tools, and maintained that the jury should assess the resulting loss. The reported argument includes exchanges with Parke, Alderson, and Martin, BB., about foreseeability, disproportionate consequences, and the difference between making machinery operational and carrying an individual part. Id. at 345–349.

The carriers answered that compensation did not encompass every loss causally traceable to breach. They argued that mere notice to a receiving clerk did not enlarge their ordinary carriage duty into a special undertaking to bear all consequences of nondelivery. Their examples included a merchant’s failure to supply coal without notice that it was needed immediately to keep a business operating. They asked the court to treat the profits as legally remote and, absent other proven loss, to limit recovery to nominal damages. That requested nominal-damages result was counsel’s position; the court’s actual order was a new trial. Id. at 349–353, 356.

## Issue, rule, and holding

The question was what damages instruction should govern a jury assessing breach of this carriage contract, and whether the reported facts permitted it to include the mill’s lost profits. Alderson, B., delivered the judgment of the court. The report contains no separately written concurrence or dissent and does not provide a numerical vote that should be inferred from the preceding case’s panel footnote. Id. at 353–356.

Recoverable damages must fairly and reasonably arise naturally in the usual course of things from the breach, or have been reasonably contemplated by both parties when contracting as its probable result. If communicated special circumstances make a particular loss an ordinary consequence under those circumstances, damages may reflect that knowledge. If the circumstances were unknown, the breaching party can ordinarily be taken to contemplate only the loss arising in the multitude of ordinary transactions. Knowledge matters because it gives the parties an opportunity to arrange special terms addressing the increased risk. Id. at 354–355.

The court held that the plaintiffs’ mill profits fell outside both routes on the circumstances it treated as communicated. The jury should have been instructed to exclude them. A new trial was required. Id. at 355–356.

## Reasoning and the notice problem

A jury’s general power to assess compensation needed a definite legal boundary. Without an instruction limiting the compensable consequences, similarly situated defendants could face radically different awards depending on circumstances they had no reason to anticipate. The court therefore formulated a rule governing both ordinary consequences and special risks known at formation. It treated conventional rules concerning nonpayment of money or failure to convey good title as consistent with that principle: parties may be presumed to know an established damages rule and contemplate its operation. Id. at 353–355.

Applying the rule, the court characterized the communicated circumstances as the shipment of a broken mill shaft by the millers who owned it. That information did not necessarily reveal that delay would prevent the entire mill from earning profits. A mill might have a spare shaft already installed or being installed; alternatively, other defective machinery might keep it idle regardless of when this shaft returned. These were hypothetical explanations of why shutdown profits were not the ordinary result of delayed carriage, rather than findings that these plaintiffs actually possessed a spare shaft or had other defective machinery. Id. at 355–356.

The court expressly accepted the actual causal chain: the old shaft served as the replacement’s model, the absence of the replacement was the sole cause of stoppage, and the carrier’s delay caused lost profits. Actual causation did not establish compensable remoteness. The decisive limitation was that the court treated those special dependencies as neither communicated nor known when the parties contracted. Id. at 356.

There is a material tension between that application and the trial narrative’s statement that the clerk had been told the mill had stopped and delivery was urgent. The brief preserves both passages rather than silently replacing one with the other. The assigned editorial notes quote *Victoria Laundry* as interpreting Alderson’s judgment to reject the relevant notice evidence. That is a later interpretation reproduced by the editors, not an express explanation in Hadley’s own judgment of how the factual tension was resolved. C&M assigned printed 1019; compare 9 Ex. at 344, 355–356. The narrow, secure holding rests on the communicated circumstances as the court characterized them, not a categorical rule that telling a carrier of an urgent shutdown can never establish knowledge of special loss.

## Exact disposition and source scope

The court made the rule absolute and ordered a new trial with an instruction excluding lost profits on the facts before it. It did not enter a final zero-damages judgment, adopt the carriers’ nominal-only request as the complete decree, or calculate a replacement award. 9 Ex. at 356.
