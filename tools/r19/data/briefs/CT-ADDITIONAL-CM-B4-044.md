# Smith v. Zimbalist
2 Cal. App. 2d 324, 38 P.2d 170 (1934) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and disposition
California’s District Court of Appeal, Second Appellate District, Division One, decided the appeal on November 20, 1934. Justice Houser wrote; Presiding Justice Conrey and Justice York concurred. The court affirmed judgment for the buyer in the seller’s action to collect the unpaid balance for two violins. The appellate caption identifies George H. Smith as administrator, while the opinion’s account describes the elderly collector who made the sale. The report later notes denial of a hearing in the California Supreme Court on January 17, 1935. That notation is subsequent procedural history, not a separate reasoned opinion reproduced here. (2 Cal. App. 2d at 324–327, 333.)

## The parties and their written exchange
The trial findings described an eighty-six-year-old amateur collector of rare violins, rather than a dealer, and Efrem Zimbalist, an internationally prominent violinist who also owned and collected old instruments. Zimbalist visited to see the collection at another person’s suggestion; the collector had not invited him to buy. Zimbalist picked up a violin, called it a Stradivarius, and asked its price. The collector offered $5,000, saying he would charge less than a dealer because of his age. Zimbalist then selected another violin, called it a Guarnerius, and was offered the pair for $8,000. He accepted, requesting $2,000 down and $1,000 monthly installments for the balance. (Id. at 325–326.)

Both sides signed documents identifying one Joseph Guarnerius violin and one Stradivarius dated 1717. Zimbalist’s receipt acknowledged the instruments and payment terms. The seller’s bill of sale repeated their makers, price, and installments and gave Zimbalist a right to exchange them for other instruments in the collection. The opinion reports that exchange provision but does not separately resolve the case by construing or enforcing it. (Id. at 326.)

The findings stated that both parties fully believed the instruments were made by those masters. Before closing, the seller had made no representations or warranties about the makers and had acted without fraud; the findings also stated that a preponderance of the evidence showed the instruments were imitations, worth no more than $300. That absence of prior fraudulent representation was distinct from the appellate court’s legal effect for the signed descriptions. The opinion was reviewing findings from trial, not simply accepting a pleaded assertion that the violins were inauthentic. (Id.)

## Claim, trial theory, and seller’s appeal
The seller sued for the unpaid purchase-price balance. The trial court concluded that mutual mistake defeated recovery and entered judgment for Zimbalist. On appeal the seller urged caveat emptor: the buyer had examined the violins and should bear the risk of his own attribution judgment. The actual opinion describes this collection action and defense; it does not report an affirmative judgment returning the $2,000 down payment or adjudicate a buyer’s counterclaim for that sum. (Id. at 327.)

## Older caveat-emptor authorities and the court’s response
The opinion traced authorities treating an artist’s name in a catalogue as opinion rather than warranty and older decisions denying relief for incorrect descriptions without an express warranty or fraud. Such examples included an attributed painting, a supposed bezoar stone, incorrectly identified wood, and materials sold under the wrong trade description. They supported the seller’s argument that mistaken judgments about objects open to inspection could leave a sale binding. (Id. at 327–329.)

The court then examined criticism and qualifications of those decisions. Written descriptions in sale papers could themselves warrant kind and quality; a buyer’s opportunity to examine did not necessarily waive that protection. It discussed later cases treating an attribution or bill-of-parcels description as a contractual undertaking, including Power v. Barham, where the surrounding evidence properly permitted a jury to find a warranty of the named painter. It also described Sherwood as supporting avoidance for a shared error about the substance of the bargain while acknowledging Wood’s contrary treatment of a discovered diamond’s value. These are the California court’s accounts of its authorities, rather than independent complete readings of every cited judgment. (Id. at 329–331.)

Henshaw was especially useful to the court because inspection need not defeat the buyer when an imitation would deceive a skilled dealer without additional analysis. The California precedent Flint similarly treated a flour brand in a sale note as a warranty: a buyer who bought that brand could not be compelled to accept a different one merely because it was equally good. The issue was conformity with the promised article, not simply whether the purchaser had obtained something of respectable quality. (Id. at 331–332.)

## Mutual mistake and alternative warranty reasoning
The court rejected strict caveat emptor on these findings. Its stated exception concerned parties honestly mistaken, in the essence of their bargain, about the identity of its subject matter. Both believed they were contracting for authentic instruments made by the named masters; under the court’s formulation, no enforceable sale had occurred. The finding of good-faith belief therefore did not establish the seller’s right to the balance. (Id. at 332–333.)

The court supplied an alternative route if the transaction were treated as a sale subject to a voidable condition. Despite the trial finding that the seller had made no representations or warranties, the documents and conduct established a warranty in law. The seller acquiesced in the buyer’s attribution and signed a bill of sale certifying the identified makers. Its description committed him to delivering instruments corresponding to that description. Later authorities and Civil Code section 1734 supported that legal consequence without requiring a separately spoken warranty or fraudulent intent. The court therefore affirmed the judgment against collection of the balance under either analysis. (Id. at 333.)

## Assigned problem and actual-result limits
The Sham Stradivarius problem in C&M Textbook 1051–1313, physical PDF page 89, printed page 1097, deliberately changes the actual transaction. It presents one violin, a $9,000 price, $3,000 down, a value of at most $500, and opposing claims including the buyer’s demand for repayment after tendering return. The reported case involves two violins, an $8,000 total price, $2,000 down, a combined value of no more than $300, and affirmance against the seller’s collection claim. The additional hypothetical repayment claim must not be presented as an award actually entered in Smith. The report’s warranty reasoning also supplies more than a bare mutual-mistake label for analyzing the adapted problem.
