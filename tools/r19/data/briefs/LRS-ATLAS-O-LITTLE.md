# Little v. Barreme
6 U.S. (2 Cranch) 170 (1804) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Decision and governing distinction

On February 27, 1804, the Supreme Court affirmed with costs the Circuit Court’s damages decree against Captain George Little for capturing the neutral brigantine Flying Fish. The statute authorized high-seas seizure of covered vessels sailing to a French port, while the executive instructions extended to vessels sailing to or from such ports. The Flying Fish was sailing from Jérémie to St. Thomas. The broader instructions could not legalize a capture outside Congress’s prescribed authority or protect Little against the resulting damages. Chief Justice Marshall delivered the Court’s opinion and expressly abandoned his initial inclination to excuse a military officer obeying executive orders. No separate Supreme Court writing is reported. (176–179.)

The assigned account describes a unanimous decision limiting presidential seizure authority. The actual report contains no numerical tally; Marshall’s acquiescence in his brethren’s position supports the account’s characterization without establishing an attendance count. The textbook’s description of a vessel sailing from France to the United States is inaccurate: the actual voyage was between Caribbean ports, from the French dependency of Jérémie to neutral Danish St. Thomas, followed by an involuntary trip to Boston after capture. The constitutional and statutory issue turns on the direction relative to a French port, not a planned voyage to the United States. (170–173, 175–178; assigned PDF 20–21/printed 264–265.)

## Capture and the reasons for suspicion

On December 2, 1799, the frigates Boston and General Greene captured the Flying Fish near Hispaniola on suspicion that it violated the February 9, 1799 nonintercourse legislation. The Supreme Court’s narrative identifies the Boston, commanded by Little, as the capturing vessel and says the vessel was taken to Boston and libeled as American property violating the law. The more extensive record identifies both frigates and provides the ownership and voyage details. (170, 172–173, 176.)

The vessel and cargo belonged to Samuel Goodman, a Prussian-born inhabitant of St. Thomas. Its master also came from that island, but had worked on American vessels, spoke English with an American accent, and appeared American. The mate was an American citizen. The vessel carried provisions and dry goods from St. Thomas to Jérémie and was returning with coffee when captured. During the chase the master threw overboard the logbook and other papers. A protest asserted that the ship had been bound to Port-au-Prince and forced into Jérémie by Rigaud’s vessels; the District Court found that story false. After capture the master asked the crew whether they would support it. The record also described a practice at St. Thomas of disguising American property to evade the restrictions. These facts explained the suspicions, but did not establish that the vessel was American: the courts treated the actual property as Danish and neutral. (172–175.)

## Statute, instructions, and proceedings

Section 1 restricted vessels owned, hired, or employed wholly or partly by persons resident in the United States from the specified commerce with French territory or persons under French authority, and supplied forfeiture and judicial condemnation provisions. Section 5 authorized presidential instructions to public armed vessels to stop and examine suspected American vessels on the high seas. If examination showed a vessel bound or sailing to a French port in violation of the act, the commander was to seize it and send it to an American port for adjudication. The statute’s high-seas seizure provision specified the direction to a French port. (170–171, 177.)

The March 12, 1799 instructions transmitted through the Navy Department told commanders to treat section 5 as part of their instructions. They also demanded vigilance against apparently foreign vessels or cargoes that were really American and bound to or from French ports. Captains were to send supporting evidence with a detained vessel and avoid injuring neutral trade and lawful American commerce. The instructions therefore pursued the statute’s enforcement objective through a direction broader than the statutory high-seas seizure authorization. (171–172, 178.)

The Massachusetts District Court restored the ship and cargo because the libellants had not proved grounds for forfeiture, but refused damages or costs. Judge Lowell’s reproduced opinion considered whether the maritime consequences of suspicious neutral conduct applied during the limited hostilities with France. He reasoned that established belligerent and neutral practices required honest papers and permitted examination; false papers and destruction of documents could excuse detention even where the property was eventually restored. He acknowledged difficulty in applying those principles to this imperfect war. That is the District Court’s reasoning, not an alternative opinion by a Supreme Court Justice. (172–175.)

The claimants appealed. The Circuit Court accepted the stated facts but found that Little knew the brig was sailing from Jérémie to St. Thomas, a neutral port. Although suspicion could justify stopping and examining it, seizure and removal to an American port lacked statutory authority. The court reversed the refusal of damages and costs, and assessors fixed damages at $8,504. The captors then appealed that final decree to the Supreme Court. Dexter argued for the captors and Martin and Mason for the claimants at the December 1801 term. The report names counsel but does not reproduce a separate full transcript of those arguments. (175–176.)

## Statutory authority controlled the capture

Marshall distinguished section 1’s general forfeiture language from section 5’s specific high-seas seizure provision. The former contemplated seizure within the United States; the latter supplied the special authority for seizure at sea and restricted it to covered vessels bound to a French port. Congress had prescribed a method of executing the law that excluded the seizure at issue. Suspicion that this neutral ship was secretly American could not cure that defect, because even a genuinely American ship on this outbound-from-French-port voyage would not have been subject to this high-seas seizure. (177–178.)

The Court did not deny that broader instructions might improve enforcement. Marshall recognized that restricting seizure to inbound-to-French-port vessels made evasion easier and that the executive’s broader construction better advanced the law’s practical objective. Nevertheless, that assessment of usefulness could not substitute for the authority Congress enacted. He also left open whether the President’s faithful-execution and commander-in-chief responsibilities, without the particular statutory restriction, might have supported seizure of American vessels engaged in the illicit commerce under the circumstances then existing. The decision rests on the enacted restrictions; it does not announce that the President always lacks independent authority over maritime enforcement. (177–178.)

## Instructions, liability, and exact disposition

Marshall initially thought the distinction between civilian and military officials, and between domestic proceedings and action at sea, might justify excusing an officer who acted in good faith under lawful superiors’ orders. Military obedience was indispensable, and he considered whether redress should instead run against the government through negotiation. He expressly rejected that initial position and adopted the Court’s conclusion that instructions cannot transform the transaction or legalize conduct that otherwise constitutes trespass. Little remained answerable to the neutral owner for damages. This is reasoning within the opinion for the Court, not a recorded dissent or concurrence. (178–179.)

Because the seizure would have been unlawful even if the suspicion about American ownership had been correct, the Court found it unnecessary to decide the broader question whether probable cause could excuse damages for mistakenly seizing a neutral vessel in a case otherwise within the statutory authority. It also refused an objection to the damages account: nothing objectionable appeared on its face, and counsel had not excepted to it before the proper tribunal. Finding no error, the Court affirmed the Circuit Court’s judgment with costs. The result preserved the $8,504 award; it did not reinstate the District Court’s denial or newly condemn the vessel. (179.)
