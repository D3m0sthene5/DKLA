# Delchi Carrier SpA v. Rotorex Corp.
71 F.3d 1024 (2d Cir. 1995) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
Judge Winter wrote for Judges Jacobs and Leval in this appeal and cross-appeal under the United Nations Convention on Contracts for the International Sale of Goods (CISG). The Second Circuit upheld summary judgment establishing Rotorex's liability and the existing damages award following a bench trial. It reversed part of the denial of Delchi's additional incidental and consequential damages and remanded, including for findings about whether shutdown wages were fixed or variable costs. The opinion reports $1,785,772.44 as the judgment under appeal, and $1,248,331.87 as the damages assessed at trial, with prejudgment interest separately awarded under Article 78. Those figures should not be treated as competing descriptions of principal damages. There is no separate writing. 71 F.3d at 1026–1027, 1031.

## Facts and procedural history
In January 1988, New York seller Rotorex agreed to provide 10,800 compressors to Italian air-conditioner manufacturer Delchi for its Ariele portable units, which were to be sold during spring and summer 1988. Rotorex had supplied a sample and specifications for cooling capacity and power consumption. Three shipments were due before May 15. The first shipment left by sea March 26 and arrived April 20; the second left around May 9. Delchi paid for both through letters of credit. While the second was in transit, Delchi discovered that the first shipment failed to match the agreed performance. On May 13 it reported that quality control rejected 93 percent of the compressors. They cooled less effectively and consumed more electricity than the sample and specifications promised. Efforts to cure failed. Rotorex refused to supply conforming replacements, asserting that it had inadvertently communicated the specifications. Id. at 1026–1027.

Delchi cancelled on May 23. It expedited compressors already ordered from Sanyo, but could not obtain sufficient timely substitutes elsewhere. The shortage interrupted manufacturing and reduced seasonal sales. Judge Cholakis granted partial summary judgment on liability in January 1991. After discovery, Judge Munson held a bench trial on damages. He allowed lost profits, attempted cure expenses, expedited Sanyo shipping, and handling and storage of rejected compressors. He rejected several other expenditures as duplicating lost profits, denied electrical-panel modification expenses for inadequate proof of causation, and rejected a claimed 4,000 additional Italian sales as insufficiently certain. Both sides appealed. Id. at 1027.

## Issues and governing law
The parties agreed that the CISG governed. The court approached the Convention through its text, general principles, international character, and the need for uniform application under Article 7. Analogous UCC decisions could inform interpretation where the language corresponded, but did not automatically govern. Footnote 1 explained that parties may exclude or vary the CISG under Article 6; here their agreement did not select another regime and both parties were located in signatory nations. Id. at 1027–1028 & n.1.

Articles 35 and 36 require contractually conforming goods, including the qualities of a seller's sample or model. A fundamental breach under Article 25 substantially deprives the other party of its contractual expectation, subject to that article's foreseeability qualification. Articles 46 and 49 authorize substitute performance or avoidance in the appropriate circumstances. Article 74 compensates the loss, including lost profit, attributable to breach, subject to what the breaching party foresaw or should have foreseen at formation as a possible consequence. The court reviewed summary judgment and the legal damages measure independently, while reviewing trial findings for clear error. Id. at 1028–1029.

## Arguments and reasoning
Rotorex disputed breach, but its own engineering representative, president, answer, and deposition testimony admitted departures from the specifications or sample. No genuine factual dispute remained about nonconformity. Cooling power and energy use were central determinants of compressor value, so the deprivation was substantial and the fundamental-breach determination was proper. Id. at 1028–1029.

Rotorex next argued that Delchi's total available inventory over the selling season exceeded all possible lost orders. The argument ignored timing. An air conditioner available in late July could not fill a spring order. The May shutdown and production delay could therefore cause lost sales even if aggregate inventory through August was adequate. The trial judge's findings were supportable and conservative. Orders through European affiliates and Italian sales agents were also foreseeable: a manufacturer could predict that Delchi would accept orders based on the compressors it had contracted to receive. The trial judge could evaluate the documents and testimony to decide which orders were sufficiently proven. Id. at 1029.

For lost profits, the trial court subtracted unit variable manufacturing cost of 478,783 lire from average sales revenue of 654,644 lire. Rotorex wanted fixed costs and depreciation deducted too. Those costs would have been incurred in Delchi's continuing business regardless of this breach. Their subtraction would undercompensate the lost contribution of the foregone sales. Although the CISG did not prescribe this precise calculation, the court approved deducting variable rather than unaffected fixed costs. Id. at 1029–1030.

Delchi's cross-appeal exposed a different accounting error. The lost-profit calculation involved hypothetical sales revenue less hypothetical variable costs never incurred for those sales. It did not reimburse actual expenditures that produced no saleable goods. Shipping and customs for the returned compressors, storage of the second shipment at Genoa, and unreimbursed tooling, insulation, and tubing expenses therefore did not duplicate the profits award. They were foreseeable incidental or consequential losses. Footnote 2 reproduced the relevant UCC categories as an analogy, including commercially reasonable expenses associated with rejected goods and losses from requirements the seller had reason to know. Id. at 1030 & n.2.

Shutdown labor presented a narrower factual issue. Those wages were foreseeable consequences of receiving unusable compressors, but the trial court's label of fixed costs did not establish whether Delchi would have paid them regardless of output. If truly unaffected fixed expenses, their treatment differed from variable costs incurred fruitlessly because of breach. The appellate court lacked findings to classify them and remanded. It did not itself award the wages. Id. at 1030–1031.

The court upheld the denial of electrical-panel modification costs. Delchi had not persuaded the trial judge that they were extra costs caused by Rotorex's breach rather than ordinary Sanyo production expenses; that credibility-based determination was not clearly erroneous. It also upheld rejection of the additional 4,000 Italian sales. Testimony that agents would have ordered more did not require the trial court to accept those speculative orders. Id. at 1031.

## Holding and significance
The existing award stood; specified additional expenditures were recoverable without duplicating lost profits, and the shutdown-wage classification required further proceedings. This decision distinguishes the legal damages measure from factual proof of particular losses, and hypothetical avoided costs from actual wasted expenditures. Its statutory language is Article 74's possible-consequence formulation. The opinion also uses probable-consequence terminology in discussing additional expenses; the casebook's question about that terminology is editorial analysis, rather than a separate holding changing the Convention's text.
