# Columbia Nitrogen Corp. v. Royster Co.
451 F.2d 3 (4th Cir. 1971) | Contracts | Commercial context, merger language, and a partial retrial

## A phosphate supply contract during a falling market

Royster made and sold mixed fertilizer, whose principal components were nitrogen, phosphate, and potash. Columbia primarily produced nitrogen and also made some mixed fertilizer. For years Royster bought substantial quantities from Columbia, while Columbia bought little from Royster. In fall 1966 Royster built a phosphate-production facility with capacity exceeding its own needs. After extensive negotiations, the companies executed a May 8, 1967 contract for Columbia to purchase at least 31,000 tons annually during an initial term running July 1, 1967 through June 30, 1970, with specified extension and renewal provisions. The minimum comprised 15,000 tons each of diammonium phosphate and granular triple superphosphate and 1,000 tons of run-of-pile triple superphosphate. Prices were specified, with an escalation mechanism tied to raw-material and labor costs. (451 F.2d at 6–7, 12; n.2.)

Phosphate market prices then fell sharply. At Columbia's request, Royster reduced its diammonium-phosphate price for three months in 1967 but reserved the original price thereafter. Even the reduced price remained materially above market. Columbia ordered less than one-tenth of the first year's scheduled amount and offered to take the remainder at market and resell without a brokerage fee. Royster insisted on the contract price; after Columbia refused delivery, Royster resold the rejected goods for Columbia's account below that price. (Id. at 7.)

Royster sued for breach, invoking diversity jurisdiction. Columbia defended that the writing, construed through industry usage and their previous dealings, did not impose an inflexible obligation to take the minimum amounts at the listed prices. It also alleged that Royster's reciprocal purchasing practices violated federal antitrust law, both as a defense and as a treble-damages counterclaim. The district court excluded Columbia's commercial-context evidence, submitted only the coercive-reciprocity antitrust theory, and declined the alternative noncoercive theory. A jury found for Royster on the contract and against Columbia on antitrust, producing a $750,000 contract judgment. Columbia appealed. (Id. at 6; n.1.)

## The excluded offers concerned industry practice and six years of dealings

Experienced trade witnesses would have testified that fertilizer-material price and quantity provisions were projections commonly adjusted for weather, crop conditions, farming practices, government programs, and market conditions. The complete third footnote reproduces descriptions of customary adjustments, including releasing buyers when a seller would not meet competitive prices. Some statements characterized written quantities as estimates rather than inflexible commitments. Those were offers of proof whose weight remained for retrial, not findings by the appellate court that the entire industry treated contracts as legally worthless. (Id. at 7–8; n.3.)

Columbia also offered six years of dealings, predominantly nitrogen sales to Royster and inventory exchanges. It claimed repeated substantial deviations from specified price or quantity, four transactions in which Royster took none of the contracted goods, and over $500,000 in reduced sales. An officer would explain that this pattern informed Columbia's negotiating understanding. The district court excluded everything because detailed, facially clear terms could not be contradicted by usage or dealing. The appellate question was admissibility, not whether Columbia had already proved the asserted practice. (Id. at 8.)

## The UCC did not require facial ambiguity

Judge Butzner held that Virginia's adoption of the UCC displaced the older prerequisite of finding ambiguity before considering this kind of evidence. Sections 8.1-102 and 8.2-202 promoted commercial practices and permitted usage and dealing to explain or supplement a final written expression. The official comments expressly rejected ambiguity as a condition precedent. The court accordingly did not need to declare the written quantities facially uncertain. (Id. at 8–9; nn.4–5.)

The consistency limit nevertheless remained. Usage and dealing could not be admitted when no reasonable construction reconciled them with express terms. Section 8.1-205(4), reproduced in the seventh footnote, gave express terms priority when reconciliation was unreasonable and dealing priority over usage. The correct test therefore was reasonable consistency, not whether the agreement appeared complete and detailed. The court did not authorize commercial custom to defeat every specifically negotiated term. (Id. at 9; nn.6–7.)

Here, several features supported reconciliation. The writing did not expressly exclude commercial-context interpretation; it neither permitted nor prohibited adjustment to a declining market. Its quantity heading described products supplied under contract, rather than simply products purchased. The express default clause addressed unpaid delivered goods. Columbia rejected a proposed $10-per-ton liquidated charge for quantities it declined, while Royster rejected a clause expressly requiring conformity to competitors' prices. Neither rejected proposal became an agreed term. The final document left the consequences of refusal to accept unresolved, supporting inquiry into commercially understood meaning before deciding whether a breach occurred. (Id. at 9–10; nn.2, 8.)

Royster also characterized Columbia's argument as unilateral modification. The court answered that the offer sought to prove a practice of mutual adjustments sufficiently prevalent to form part of this agreement, rather than a discretionary power for Columbia to change any term. That distinction preserved the factual and legal limits of its holding. (Id. at 10.)

## The merger clause did not specifically negate trade context

The contract rejected verbal understandings, represented itself as expressing all terms and conditions, and required duplicate signatures and written seller approval. But section 8.2-202 distinguished consistent additional terms from evidence of usage and dealing. Complete integration could exclude additional terms without automatically excluding the commercial context informing the language. The official comment treated established usage and prior dealings as part of the writing's assumed background unless carefully negated. Stereotyped merger language referring to verbal understandings did not specifically negate those separate categories. The offered evidence should have been admitted, requiring vacatur and retrial of the contract judgment. (Id. at 10–11; nn.9–11.)

This ruling does not establish that every merger clause fails or that courts may disregard an express exclusion of usage. It explains why this clause and these offered practices did not justify blanket exclusion before determining the factual bargain. The appellate court did not enter judgment that Columbia had no purchase obligation. (Id.)

## A later purchase order did not independently change the bargain

The complete opinion separately rejected Columbia's demand for judgment based on a purchase order issued ten days after execution and acknowledged by Royster. Its reverse-side form reserved a right to change the order and claimed priority over conflicting seller terms. Section 8.2-207 concerned formation; the bargain was already effective before this order expressly referring to the existing May 8 contract. The order therefore did not qualify as the acceptance or confirmation relied upon in Columbia's cited formation cases. (Id. at 11–12; n.12.)

Nor did section 8.2-209's elimination of a consideration requirement prove a modification. The reserved power concerned the order's shipping and invoicing instructions, with an equitable adjustment for resulting seller costs, rather than alteration of the main contract. Those matters were not in dispute. The order did not corroborate the asserted industry practice because neither the usage nor dealing theory sanctioned unilateral changes regardless of market circumstances. Its exclusion was not an abuse of discretion, although it could have been admitted with instructions explaining its limited role. (Id. at 12.)

## Damages instructions and antitrust had different outcomes

Royster's resale to Mobil had to occur in good faith and commercially reasonably to support recovery of the contract-resale difference under section 8.2-706. The damages instruction was not reversible error, but retrial required amplification: the jury should be told of the duty to realize as high a price as possible under the circumstances and dispose of the goods to best advantage. Conflicting evidence about market price, storage, and disposition of a large quantity made that guidance significant. The court did not decide that the Mobil sale was commercially unreasonable. (Id. at 12–13.)

All antitrust rulings were affirmed. On coercive reciprocity, the jury rejected Columbia's claim that Royster used nitrogen-buying leverage to force the phosphate agreement. Columbia had not distinctly preserved its objection to the interstate-commerce instruction and instead sought, and eventually received, a favorable supplemental instruction within the same per se framework. It could not shift theories on appeal, and the verdict revealed no prejudice from the challenged threshold. The complete fifteenth footnote further notes that the propriety of initially submitting coercive reciprocity was not directly at issue because Royster prevailed. (Id. at 13–14; nn.13–17.)

For noncoercive reciprocity, the panel assumed without deciding that the alleged voluntary arrangement violated Sherman Act section 1. It nevertheless refused that defense to this contract action under *Kelly v. Kosuga*: the phosphate sale was an intelligible transaction for fair consideration, voluntarily accepted, and the reciprocal arrangement had ended before suit. A contract-damages judgment would not itself enforce the precise forbidden practice or exclude competitors. The court did not decide the different treatment of a coercive arrangement. (Id. at 14–15; nn.18–20.)

The counterclaim also failed under the panel's reading of *Perma Life*. That decision involved unequal parties whose meaningful participation was not voluntary and left complete involvement unresolved. Here, after the jury rejected coercion, the available theory involved economically comparable participants voluntarily and equally responsible for the arrangement. Such a participant could not claim treble damages from its partner when falling prices made the bargain unprofitable. The panel reserved whether government or outsiders could challenge the arrangement and did not finally adjudicate its underlying legality. These historical antitrust rulings should not be mistaken for a holding validating all reciprocal purchasing. (Id. at 14–16; nn.18–21.)

## Exact disposition and source scope

On October 26, 1971, the Fourth Circuit affirmed all antitrust issues, vacated Royster's contract judgment, and remanded for further proceedings consistent with the opinion, including a new contract trial. Each side bore its own costs. Butzner wrote for the panel with Chief Judge Haynsworth and Judge Winter; there was no separate writing or recorded dissent. (Id. at 6, 16.)
