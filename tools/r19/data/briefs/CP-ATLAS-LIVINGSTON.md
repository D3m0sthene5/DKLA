# Livingston v. Jefferson

**Civil Procedure — 15 F. Cas. 660, No. 8,411 (C.C.D. Va. 1811), also reported at 1 Brock. 203. United States Circuit Court for the District of Virginia, December 5, 1811. District Judge John Tyler and Circuit Justice John Marshall each gave an explanation supporting the defendant’s jurisdictional objection.**

## The land dispute and the jurisdictional pleading

Edward Livingston, a New York citizen, sued Thomas Jefferson, a Virginia citizen, for alleged trespasses involving the Batture of the Suburb St. Mary in New Orleans, then in the Territory of Orleans. The suit began in 1810, after Jefferson’s presidency ended. Livingston attributed the challenged acts to Jefferson while he was President. The reported pleadings alleged entry onto land claimed by Livingston, destruction of tools and materials, removal of soil, expulsion of Livingston, interruption of canal and embankment construction, and resulting damage. These were allegations in the declaration, not findings that Jefferson had committed unlawful acts or that Livingston owned the disputed property. 15 F. Cas. at 660–661.

The eight counts used the traditional pleading device of identifying New Orleans as the location of the events and then adding a fictional Virginia venue: Richmond, in Henrico County. Jefferson demurred to several counts and pleaded both the general issue and justifications, including that he acted under congressional authority as President and without malice. The court did not decide those merits defenses. The immediate question arose from a separate plea denying the Virginia court’s jurisdiction because all the land was in the Territory of Orleans, where a court competent to hear trespass actions existed. Id. at 661.

Livingston replied that Jefferson had remained outside the Orleans courts’ jurisdiction since the cause of action arose and was instead resident within the Virginia court’s reach. Thus, Livingston argued, locating jurisdiction solely where the land lay would leave him unable to sue this defendant there. Jefferson demurred to that reply, and Livingston joined in the demurrer. The legal sufficiency of the attempted answer to the jurisdictional plea was therefore the immediate posture. Id.

## Issue and result

Could the federal circuit court in Virginia hear a claim for trespass to land situated outside its district when the defendant lived, and could be found, in Virginia? Both judges answered **no**. Trespass to land was classified as a local action, and the defendant’s presence did not convert it into a transitory action. Tyler said the cause must leave the court; Marshall concluded that the law on the demurrer favored Jefferson. This was a jurisdictional defeat for Livingston, rather than an adjudication that Jefferson’s conduct was lawful or that Livingston lacked a substantive property right. Id. at 661–665.

## Tyler: settled locality and limits on effective judicial action

Tyler emphasized the longstanding common-law distinction. Although many formerly local actions had become transitory, trespass to land—traditionally described as *quare clausum fregit*, or breaking the plaintiff’s close—remained local. The location was not a freely interchangeable allegation. A fiction useful in a transitory contract action could not move a New Orleans parcel into Richmond. Id. at 661–663.

He supplied practical reasons for respecting the rule. A land-trespass case could require examination of title and boundaries; neighbors would know the relevant lines and local circumstances. A defendant could rely on a better title, making a survey necessary. Judicial process might need to reach a surveyor, sheriff, or jury at the place in question, and a court without authority over those persons could not necessarily complete what it began. Tyler used a Virginia waste case from the wrong judicial district to illustrate that a local action could fail for want of jurisdiction even after a verdict; the parties’ pleadings could not supply the missing authority. Id. at 662.

Livingston’s appeal to general or universal law did not persuade him. Tyler rejected the suggestion that broad natural-law language specified the appropriate court or displaced established procedure. He also rejected changing the rule solely to prevent a failure of justice. A competent court existed where the alleged trespass occurred; a defendant might nevertheless escape its reach, but occasional gaps did not authorize this court to enact a new jurisdictional rule. Tyler acknowledged the possibility of such an evasion as an exception to the general arrangement. His explanation supported the jurisdictional plea without resolving title, liability, or Jefferson’s asserted statutory justification. Id. at 662–663.

## Marshall: a binding technical rule despite its doubtful logic

Marshall concurred in the result but approached the rule more skeptically. Originally, common-law actions depended on trial by a jury of the vicinity, which made judicial authority local. English superior courts could direct juries throughout the realm, and judicially created venue fictions eventually allowed personal tort and contract claims arising elsewhere to be heard against defendants within reach. The fiction had limits: it could not be contradicted merely to defeat a transitory action it was designed to sustain, but the allegation of place could be contested when necessary to the merits or when jurisdiction lay outside the fiction’s intended protection. Id. at 663–664.

The difficult comparison was a contract involving land. Such a contract action could be maintained where the defendant was found even though it might require the same inquiry into title, boundaries, or surveys as a land-trespass action. For an action seeking damages against a person whom the court could reach, Marshall saw no satisfactory nontechnical reason why those practical difficulties should be decisive in one category and tolerable in the other. The defendant’s presence created the prospect of an enforceable personal remedy. A proceeding directed at the property itself presented a different problem because relief could require control over the thing. Id. at 664.

Nevertheless, the settled line drawn by the authorities treated transactions that might occur anywhere as transitory and causes necessarily tied to a location as local. Marshall regarded that limit as too firmly established to disregard. He examined Lord Mansfield’s attempts to distinguish proceedings against property from claims for personal damages, but explained why they did not displace the rule: the broader remarks in *Mostyn v. Fabrigas* came in a personal-injury action; Mansfield’s land-trespass decisions had occurred at nisi prius without the sanction of a reviewed court decision; and *Doulson v. Matthews* later rejected that attempted expansion. Id. at 664–665.

Marshall did not treat post-Revolution English cases as independently binding American authority. He treated them as evidence of the ancient rule when a later attempt to alter that rule had itself failed. Virginia had adopted English common law, but his final jurisdictional analysis also stressed that federal courts’ jurisdiction depended on the Constitution and federal statutes. The argument that a Virginia statute permitting juries from bystanders changed the state-court rule did not determine this federal court’s authority. Id. at 665.

He recognized that the federal arrangement intensified the practical problem: jurisdiction depended on the character of the parties, yet the defendant had to reside or be found in the appropriate district. Requiring the land’s district could leave a plaintiff with a substantive right but no effective remedy against a defendant who never appeared there. Marshall considered that argument weighty, but concluded that precedent still required judgment for Jefferson on the demurrer. His dissatisfaction was not a dissent from the disposition. Id.

## Assigned treatment and limits of the decision

The assigned *Reasor-Hill* passage describes Livingston as the leading American land-trespass precedent, accurately identifies both judges’ agreement, and highlights Marshall’s dissatisfaction. Its own account appears at CivPro printed 402, PDF page 222. A second assigned account, Note 4 at printed 409, PDF page 226, quotes Marshall’s local/transitory distinction from report page 664 and asks how later venue legislation affects it. Those later statutory questions are course discussion; this 1811 judgment did not construe modern 28 U.S.C. § 1391.

The reporter’s later note 5 likewise describes an 1819 Virginia enactment permitting specified land-related actions where the defendant resided or could be found. That is subsequent editorial information, not a statute the judges applied in 1811. The decision’s immediate lesson is that obtaining authority over a defendant did not then necessarily give a federal court authority over a local land-trespass action. It does not establish presidential immunity, determine the ownership of the batture, or decide whether every claim connected to land is local: Marshall expressly distinguished transitory contract actions involving land.
