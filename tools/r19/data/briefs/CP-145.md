# Holmberg v. State, Division of Risk Management

**Civil Procedure · CP-145 · 796 P.2d 823 (Alaska 1990) · August 3, 1990**

Holmberg accepts that judicially adequate administrative proceedings can produce issue preclusion, including across different agencies. It nevertheless denies preclusion in this case for two independently sufficient reasons: the state as employer was not in privity with the retirement system represented in the other proceeding, and the workers’ compensation decision was the earlier final judgment despite its pending appeal. The court also found substantial evidence supporting that earlier decision and affirmed.

Karen Holmberg began working in the state’s Division of Risk Management in 1979. She had a history of back injuries and surgeries, but reported a long period without back pain before her condition worsened during this employment. Her work involved prolonged sitting, and she experienced additional falls and work-related knee injuries. The employer supplied a standing desk, reclining chair, and secretarial help with bending and lifting, and allowed considerable control over work arrangements. She resigned because of back pain on June 30, 1987. (796 P.2d at 824.)

The Alaska Workers’ Compensation Board awarded temporary total disability benefits on February 18, 1988 but denied permanent total disability. Holmberg appealed in March. Meanwhile, the Public Employees Retirement System’s administrators awarded nonoccupational disability benefits, but initially denied occupational benefits. On her appeal, the Public Employees Retirement Board ruled on April 20 that she was permanently and totally disabled because of work accidents and awarded occupational disability benefits. Holmberg added that later decision to the record of her earlier workers’ compensation appeal and argued that it conclusively established her inability to perform her job. The superior court affirmed the workers’ compensation ruling, and she appealed again. (Id.)

Justice Moore distinguished the ultimate statutory disability standards from a shared factual issue. Workers’ compensation examined ability to earn wages in the same or other employment, considering injury, age, education, available work, and related circumstances. PERS focused on the employee’s usual duties or another comparable job offered by the employer. Different benefit entitlements therefore did not automatically establish or defeat one another. As judicial notes 2 and 3 explain, Holmberg’s refined argument concerned the narrower fact of physical ability to work at Risk Management, rather than identical remedies under the two statutes. (Id. at 824–27 & nn.2–3.)

The court rejected a categorical barrier between administrative tribunals. PERB hearings provided evidence, witness testimony, and cross-examination, and the statutory scheme did not signal that those adjudications must lack preclusive force. Differences in agency functions can matter where one lacks authority to represent the other’s interests or legislative policy requires a new determination, but the two boards had no materially different expertise on this narrow physical-capacity question. Nor did statutory deference to the workers’ compensation board’s credibility findings imply permission to relitigate identical facts decided elsewhere. (Id. at 825–27 & n.4.)

Preclusion still required a party or privy against whom the judgment could operate, an identical issue, and a final merits determination. Here, the relevant privity question concerned the parties represented before the boards, not merely the governmental identity of the boards themselves. Retirement and Benefits appeared before PERB for PERS, an independent retirement plan funded by participating employers and employees. It did not represent the state in its separate capacity as employer. The state’s interest as one contributor to the common fund supplied only nominal representation, not adequate assurance of notice, opportunity to be heard, and protection of its particular interests. Shared administration and some financial overlap did not make PERS and the state privies. (Id. at 827–29 & nn.5–6.)

Independently, the workers’ compensation decision came first. A final judgment retains preclusive effect while an appeal is pending. Treating appeal as a suspension of finality would invite duplicate proceedings and permit a later favorable adjudication to overturn an earlier loss by collateral means. PERB’s unappealed decision consequently could not preclude the earlier board’s contrary determination. The court noted that, if the other requirements were satisfied, the earlier ruling could have been invoked in PERB; it did not hold that every requirement for doing so was established. To handle the risk of eventual reversal, the second tribunal can sometimes stay proceedings, and a judgment resting on a subsequently reversed prior judgment can be attacked under Rule 60(b)(5). (Id. at 829–30 & n.7.)

Finally, the court considered Holmberg’s substantial-evidence objection. A vocational counselor’s job analysis and two physicians’ conditional approval of that work supported the board’s finding that she could perform her duties. Her difficulty using one stool was insignificant given the employer’s willingness to modify the station further. The report did not say every task could be performed in every posture; the counselor acknowledged that typing or computer work could not be done in the reclining chair. Holmberg had not shown that the physicians relied on the contrary assumption or that the work description was inaccurate. (Id. at 830.)

The superior-court decision was **affirmed**. Moore wrote for the five-member court—Chief Justice Matthews and Justices Rabinowitz, Burke, Compton, and Moore—with no separate opinion or dissent reported. The complete decision includes seven judicial notes. The assigned principal, printed pages 1318–1323, retains notes 2 and 3 in appended PDF page 696 but omits the final evidentiary discussion. Administrative adjudication’s potential quality, the identity of represented interests, and chronological finality are distinct requirements; passing the first does not establish the others. This brief states the historical decision without an unchecked current-law conclusion.
