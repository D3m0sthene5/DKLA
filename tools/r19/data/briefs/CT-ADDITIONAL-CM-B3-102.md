# Northern Delaware Industrial Development Corp. and Phoenix Steel Corp. v. E. W. Bliss Co.
245 A.2d 431 (Del. Ch. July 9, 1968), reargument denied July 22, 1968 | Contracts

## Court, stages, and posture
Vice Chancellor Marvel of the Delaware Court of Chancery declined specific performance of a proposed construction work schedule and then denied reargument. Both rulings appear in the complete reported decision. They concern whether equity should require a contractor to add a night shift to a large modernization project, rather than a final adjudication of all delay-damages claims or the arbitration question. (431–434.)

## Facts and requested relief
Under a May 26, 1966 contract, E. W. Bliss agreed to provide labor, services, materials, and equipment to modernize and expand Phoenix Steel’s plant in Claymont. Northern Delaware Industrial Development and Phoenix were plaintiffs. The job cost $27.5 million and covered approximately sixty acres. Work had fallen behind the contractual schedule on the sparse record before the court. Plaintiffs sought an order compelling Bliss to requisition 300 additional construction workers for a night shift during the shutdown and renovation of one mill. Around the complaint’s filing, Bliss was running a single shift of between 192 and 337 workers daily. (431–432.)

The claimed obligation came from an incorporated proposal by prime subcontractor Noble J. Dick. Its working-schedule provision quoted the work on a normal forty-hour, five-day basis, except necessary service tie-ins, and included premium time for the mill shutdown when work in two shifts was contemplated. Plaintiffs described the industry phrase as meaning day and night shifts over a seven-day week; Bliss did not deny that meaning. Plaintiffs inferred an entitlement to at least 300 workers on each shift. But note 1 states expressly that the contract did not promise any particular number of workers. That difference between a contemplated work pattern and a promised headcount is important to the court’s refusal. (432 & n. 1.)

## Procedural issue and first ruling
Plaintiffs also sought damages and other relief, whose consideration was deferred. Bliss had moved to dismiss or stay on an asserted contractual arbitration obligation. At argument, the court raised the distinct question whether it should exercise equitable jurisdiction to order specific performance of the claimed staffing provision. The decision resolves that question without deciding the asserted arbitration obligation or awarding delay damages. (432.)

The court held that the proposed order would commit it to supervising a massive, complex, unfinished construction project. The concern was not merely the administrative act of placing an employment requisition. Keeping a prescribed workforce on the site to accelerate the project implicated continuing technical construction management. The Byrne affidavit also suggested that the extra labor might be unavailable and could impede orderly completion even if available. The court expressed those points cautiously on this record; it did not make a general factual finding that increasing labor always delays construction. (432–433.)

## Rule, distinctions, and reasoning
Equity can require completion of a sufficiently defined construction undertaking in a suitable case, especially when tied to a land sale and largely completed. The court thus rejected a universal absence of jurisdiction over construction contracts. It distinguished defined plans permitting effective judicial supervision from this attempt to accelerate general work on the plaintiffs’ property through a staffing clause that merely contemplated a second shift. (433.)

An impractical construction order ordinarily should not issue absent special circumstances or direct involvement of the public interest. Note 2 discusses traditional exceptions involving certainty, construction on the defendant’s land or land acquired from the plaintiff, and part performance conferring benefits in specie. City Stores did not require a different result: that case involved definite shopping-center plans and a promised tenancy whose prospective opportunity could otherwise be lost. The court considered its circumstances different from this indefinite demand to manage steel-mill staffing. (433 & n. 2.)

The combined defects were contractual imprecision and impracticability, potentially impossibility, of enforcing the desired mandatory workforce order. Plaintiffs remained able to seek legal relief for actionable building delays. On notice, they could apply for an order dismissing the action insofar as it sought specific performance of the Dick proposal, or alternatively for removal of that cause to a competent court. The opinion did not dismiss every possible contract claim with prejudice or decide that Bliss could not be liable for damages. (433.)

## Reargument
On reargument, plaintiffs narrowed their characterization: they claimed to seek only a ministerial act of hiring more workers, not judicial supervision of the whole undertaking. They requested an opportunity to show labor availability and that fewer than 300 additional workers might suffice. The court answered that even sustainable new factual contentions would not eliminate its authority to decline this form of equitable relief. (433–434.)

Marvel additionally invoked the principle that a court does not affirmatively and directly enforce personal-services performance, even when unique, because the practical difficulties resemble those of supervising construction. The court denied reargument and permitted presentation of an appropriate order on notice. This reasoning does not establish that every construction contract is categorically unenforceable or that negative restraints on personal services are the same as affirmative compulsion. (434.)

## Assignment, disposition, and source precision
The assignment’s statement that adding 300 workers was required by the contract compresses the plaintiffs’ position. The full opinion expressly says no specific headcount was promised. The assignment correctly includes both the initial refusal and the ministerial-act argument on reargument. Its next arbitration hypothetical is editorial; this case did not decide whether an arbitral award of the same relief must be enforced. (Assigned 946–947; 432 n. 1, 433–434.)

The exact result is refusal of the staffing-specific-performance application, with the stated dismissal-or-removal procedure, followed by denial of reargument. Marvel authored both rulings; no separate writing appears. The original report prints the July 9 merits date and July 22 reargument date. Although the last paragraph calls the denied request the defendant’s motion, the reargument account itself expressly identifies plaintiffs as the movants, consistent with the assignment. The complete original four-page report, all judicial text and both notes, both stages, and both assigned pages were read. Separate review is tracked in the accompanying register.
