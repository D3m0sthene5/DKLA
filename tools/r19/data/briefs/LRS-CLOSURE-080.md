# Arizona State Legislature v. Arizona Independent Redistricting Commission
576 U.S. 787 (2015), 135 S. Ct. 2652 | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Decision, votes, and the dispute
On June 29, 2015, the Supreme Court affirmed dismissal of the Arizona Legislature's challenge to congressional redistricting by an independent commission created through a voter initiative. The Legislature had Article III standing, but lost on the merits: the Elections Clause and 2 U.S.C. § 2a(c) permitted Arizona's arrangement. Ginsburg wrote for Kennedy, Breyer, Sotomayor, and Kagan. Roberts dissented, joined by Scalia, Thomas, and Alito. Scalia and Thomas each also wrote a separate dissent joined by the other, arguing that the Legislature lacked standing and the suit should be dismissed for want of jurisdiction. The merits outcome was 5–4; the jurisdictional objection in those separate dissents must not be conflated with Roberts's merits objection. (790, 793, 799–804, 824, 854–862.)

Arizona's Constitution makes the electorate a coordinate source of legislation through initiative and referendum. Against a history of disputed redistricting, voters adopted Proposition 106 in 2000 to reduce gerrymandering and improve participation. It transferred congressional and state-legislative redistricting to the Arizona Independent Redistricting Commission. Legislative majority and minority leaders select four commissioners from a prescribed list; those four choose a fifth, independent chair. The commission draws and certifies maps after each census. The Legislature may make nonbinding recommendations and must appropriate funds, but does not adopt the binding plan. Restrictions govern commissioners' party composition, officeholding, and removal. (791–798.)

After the commission approved its 2010-census maps on January 17, 2012, the Legislature sued on June 6, seeking a declaration and an injunction against using commission congressional maps after the 2012 election. It conceded that voters could control state-legislative districting, but argued that the federal Elections Clause specifically assigned congressional districting to the elected representative body. The commission argued that the relevant term, “Legislature,” encompassed the state's lawmaking authority as constituted under state law. A three-judge district court unanimously found standing and dismissed the merits claim by a 2–1 vote. The Supreme Court postponed jurisdiction and examined both standing and the constitutional and statutory merits. (792–793, 798–799.)

## Institutional standing without success on the merits
The Court applied injury, traceability, and redressability. The Legislature claimed that it had lost a constitutional prerogative to initiate redistricting, an institutional deprivation that an injunction could remedy. Rejecting that supposed exclusive prerogative on the merits did not erase standing to litigate the claim. The commission urged that some frustrated legislative act was necessary; the United States urged waiting until the secretary of state rejected a competing legislative map. Neither step was required. State constitutional provisions prevented legislative measures undermining an initiative and required implementation of the commission's certified map. Passing a competing map and seeking its implementation would therefore require disregarding those provisions rather than clarifying a speculative injury. (799–801.)

Raines v. Byrd involved individual Congressmembers asserting widely shared institutional injury without authorization from their houses, which opposed their suit. Here the institutional plaintiff sued after authorizing votes in both chambers. Coleman v. Miller supplied the closer analogy of legislative votes completely nullified. Proposition 106 and the restriction on superseding initiatives would nullify any legislative vote purporting to enact a competing congressional map, now or later. The Court expressly reserved whether Congress could sue the President: there is no federal counterpart to Arizona's initiative and such a suit would raise additional separation-of-powers concerns. The standing holding did not depend on a separate exception dispensing with the ordinary framework for state plaintiffs. (801–804 & nn. 10–15.)

## The Elections Clause as a lawmaking assignment
The Court read prior cases as making congressional redistricting a legislative function conducted through the state's prescribed lawmaking process. Hildebrant permitted a popular referendum to reject a legislature's map; Smiley required compliance with the governor's veto. Hawke, by contrast, excluded the referendum from constitutional-amendment ratification, a function assigned by the federal Constitution that was not ordinary legislation. Likewise, pre-Seventeenth Amendment selection of senators was an electoral function. The majority therefore distinguished the identity and authority appropriate to the function at issue instead of giving every constitutional use of “Legislature” an identical institutional scope. Those precedents had not themselves decided an initiative's role, but their lawmaking principle supported Arizona's arrangement. (805–809 & nn. 16–17.)

Founding-era definitions of legislature included the power or authority making laws. In Arizona, that power includes voters adopting initiatives and delegating redistricting to a commission, just as the elected assembly could delegate. The dominant historical purpose of the Elections Clause was to give Congress power to override state election rules, including rules manipulated by politicians, rather than freeze each state's internal lawmaking machinery. Modern initiatives were not known at the founding, but that absence did not imply a ban on later democratic arrangements consistent with popular sovereignty. States ordinarily choose their governmental structures; the Clause did not uniquely prohibit initiatives for federal elections while allowing them for state and local elections. (813–819 & nn. 23–25.)

The majority also disputed reliance on the nineteenth-century contested-election decision Baldwin v. Trowbridge, considering its contrary minority view, tension with an earlier congressional decision, and partisan context. It treated popular sovereignty and the initiative as compatible with the Constitution's conception of political authority. The need to curb self-interested districting reinforced that reading, without establishing that commissions eliminate all political suspicion. Ginsburg and Roberts disputed the characterization of the separate Harris litigation concerning the commission's map: the majority emphasized the findings of good-faith Voting Rights Act compliance, while the dissent stressed evidence of partisan influence. This case did not adjudicate Harris's distinct equal-population issue. (818–822; 848–849.)

## The statutory holding and its limits
Section 2a(c) provides default election procedures until a state is redistricted in the manner provided by its law. Earlier apportionment acts had referred to redistricting by the state legislature. Congress changed that formulation in 1911 while discussing the initiative and referendum and used substantially identical language in 1941. The majority concluded that the provision respected each state's redistricting procedures; Arizona's constitutionally created commission thus produced the presumptively governing map. The practical obsolescence of four default rules under one-person, one-vote precedents did not erase the recognition of state-law redistricting arrangements. (809–813 & nn. 18–22.)

This did not exempt a map from the federal Constitution, the Voting Rights Act, or other federal law: note 20 expressly preserved such challenges. The Court independently held that the Elections Clause permitted the arrangement, avoiding a conflict between the statute and the Clause. Its ruling did not rest solely on Congress's power to enact a particular map itself. The majority also warned that the Legislature's theory could unsettle other initiative-created election rules and election provisions adopted by constitutional conventions without an elected legislature's approval. It affirmed the district court's judgment. (811 n. 20; 812–813, 822–824.)

## Roberts's dissent: the assembly cannot be excluded
Roberts read “Legislature” as the elected representative institution. Dictionaries in context, other constitutional provisions, the Seventeenth Amendment's substitution of popular senatorial election, and the drafting history supported that definition. A body's different functions did not give it different identities. His appendix reproduces the Constitution's seventeen references to state legislatures. State law could allocate lawmaking power generally but could not overcome the particular federal assignment of congressional-election regulation. (824–837; 850–854.)

For Roberts, Hildebrant and Smiley permitted constraints on legislation, not replacement of the legislature. A referendum or governor's veto sent the assembly back to work; Arizona permanently excluded it from the decisive districting role. He distinguished the majority's popular-sovereignty rationale from the specific compromise embodied in constitutional text and emphasized that a useful reform cannot cure a constitutional conflict. Congress's authority and constitutional amendment offered legitimate avenues for reform. Auxiliary commissions in other states would not necessarily be threatened by requiring some legislative role. (837–842, 846–850.)

Roberts also regarded § 2a(c) as an inapplicable default provision that stopped operating after lawful redistricting, not affirmative federal adoption of any state-selected process. Legislative history could not convert it into that approval. The majority's reading raised, in his view, constitutional concerns about Congress eliminating the first part of the Elections Clause, delegating authority away from its constitutional recipient, and treating a preemption power as adoption of state law. He would have rejected the statutory rationale as well. (842–846 & dissent nn. 4–5.)

## Scalia's and Thomas's jurisdictional dissents
Scalia, joined by Thomas, argued that direct interbranch disputes about governmental powers were outside the historical judicial case or controversy. Structural questions could be decided in private lawsuits involving concrete personal harm; that did not authorize a governmental unit to litigate an abstract usurpation of its powers. He relied on Raines and Mellon and questioned Coleman's precedential force, discussing its unusual voting record and political-question ground. He would dismiss for lack of jurisdiction, although he also joined Roberts's merits dissent because he considered the majority's interpretation especially mistaken. The majority answered his Coleman account in note 13; the disagreement is preserved as such, not resolved here by recounting an assumed vote. (803–804 nn. 12–14; 854–859.)

Thomas, joined by Scalia, criticized what he regarded as inconsistent judicial deference to state initiatives across other cases and viewed transferring ongoing authority to an unelected body as reducing future democratic control. He considered the Elections Clause text contrary to the commission, but agreed that Article III barred the Legislature's institutional suit and therefore would dismiss it. Those criticisms are dissenting views, not the Court's holdings about the cited initiatives. (859–862.)
