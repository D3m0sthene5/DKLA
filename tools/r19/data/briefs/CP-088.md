# Smuck v. Hobson

**Civil Procedure · CP-088**  
408 F.2d 175, 132 U.S. App. D.C. 372 (D.C. Cir. 1969) · January 21, 1969 · En banc

## Litigation and would-be appellants

A class action on behalf of Black and poor children challenged the administration of Washington, D.C.'s public schools. The district court found racial and economic discrimination and entered a decree addressing optional attendance zones, faculty integration, transportation from overcrowded schools, pupil-assignment planning, and the existing ability-based “track system.” The appointed school board voted not to appeal and directed Superintendent Carl Hansen not to do so. Hansen resigned, but he and dissenting board member Carl Smuck nevertheless filed appeals. Parents also sought intervention to pursue appellate review. The district court permitted intervention to enable the appellate court to resolve whether it was proper. Meanwhile, Congress established an elected board, which replaced the appointed board. 408 F.2d at 176–78.

The crucial procedural problem was that the principal institutional defendant accepted the decree. The court therefore had to determine who could appeal, what interest intervention would protect, and which portions of the decree those interests permitted the parents to challenge. The complete report additionally decides certain procedural and merits issues absent from the casebook's intervention extract.

## Hansen and Smuck

Chief Judge Bazelon's Part I concluded that Hansen had no continuing official or individual interest. The decree did not bind him personally after leaving office, and he did not claim that reversal would restore him to his position. Injury to his reputation or the suggested effect on his former tenure therefore did not establish an interest that this appeal could protect.

Smuck likewise had no separate appealable interest from his board membership. He had participated in the board's defense and decision not to appeal. The decree operated against the collective board, whose policies were adopted by vote; it supplied no apparent basis on which Smuck individually could violate the decree and face enforcement. Being a named defendant at trial did not by itself establish a distinct interest in overturning the institutional decision. 408 F.2d at 177–78.

## Parents' intervention: the Part I analysis

Rule 24(a)(2), as amended in 1966, required an interest related to the transaction, practical impairment of the ability to protect that interest, and inadequate representation by existing parties. Bazelon's analysis treated the interest requirement as a practical guide to involving concerned people consistently with efficiency and due process. An educational interest could qualify without a direct financial stake. But the requirement remained a prerequisite; it was not erased from the rule. Practical harm and representation supplied more useful limiting inquiries than an abstract search for property rights. 408 F.2d at 178–80.

The parents sought to preserve the board's constitutionally permissible policy discretion. Denying intervention could practically impair that interest. Once the decree became final, they could not necessarily challenge compliant policies in a new suit: a policy might be constitutionally permissible without being constitutionally required, leaving no new constitutional violation on which to sue. An appeal provided a route to argue that the existing restrictions exceeded what the Constitution demanded. 408 F.2d at 180–81.

The board had adequately defended the parents' interests at trial, but its later decision not to appeal presented a different question. The board represented all parents, whereas the applicants could concentrate on their own children's education. Publicity, expense, delay, and institutional convenience could weigh differently for the board and the applicants. No accusation of corruption or bad faith was necessary to recognize these differences. Bazelon found a sufficiently serious possibility of inadequate representation at the appeal stage. Postjudgment timing still required scrutiny of the unusual circumstances; failure to appeal was a relevant factor, not a universal license for dissatisfied citizens to intervene. 408 F.2d at 181–82.

That analysis supported only **limited intervention**. The parents' interest was not vindication of Hansen or the board's reputation; it concerned discretion to set educational policy. They could therefore challenge only decree provisions that actually restrained that discretion. The court did not reopen every underlying finding simply because intervention had been allowed.

## Complete disposition beyond the assigned excerpt

In Parts II and III, four judges rejected the objection that the policy claims required a three-judge district court and upheld the refusal to recuse the trial judge on the belated motion made during trial. They sustained abolition of intentionally segregative optional zones, faculty-integration requirements, and transportation for volunteers from overcrowded schools to schools with spare capacity. They construed faculty integration as allowing alternative methods, rather than an existing categorical command to compel teacher reassignments. 408 F.2d at 182–86.

Part IV treated long-range planning and abolition of the track system as not materially limiting the policy discretion the parents sought to protect. Suggestions about assignment methods were not mandatory choices. Abolition addressed the track system as it then operated, without forbidding ability grouping generally. The court consequently did not resolve the underlying constitutional, factual, and educational objections to those aspects of the decree on their merits. 408 F.2d at 186–90.

The decree, as construed, required no modification to answer challenges the parents had standing to present. The court nevertheless **remanded rather than simply affirming**, to clarify that plans filed by the former board did not foreclose the elected board from developing new programs and proposals. It did not vacate the decree or order a new trial. 408 F.2d at 190.

## Separate opinions and precise vote structure

Bazelon, Leventhal, and Robinson expressly joined Part I. McGowan joined Parts II–IV with those three, supplying four judges for those sections. This makes the intervention rationale in Part I a **three-judge plurality analysis**, rather than an unqualified seven-judge or four-judge majority statement. 408 F.2d at 176.

McGowan would have dismissed all appeals because Congress entrusted the policy choice whether to appeal to the board. Mere disagreement with its good-faith decision did not demonstrate inadequate representation. His footnote rejected using *Flast v. Cohen* as a broad invitation to intervention. Since no colleague fully agreed with dismissal, he joined the procedural and merits dispositions to resolve the litigation and concurred in the result on issues outside the parents' standing. 408 F.2d at 190–91.

Danaher, joined by Burger and Tamm, would have remanded with instructions to vacate the decree, leaving the newly elected board to address the exposed problems through its statutory authority. His dissent invoked judicial restraint and the changed institutional circumstances. Its appendices reproduce a judicial-conference resolution seeking removal of judges' school-board appointment power and a contemporary report about Hobson's plans as a board member. Burger, joined by Tamm, added a separate dissent stressing judicial limits in disputed educational policy and viewing the principal opinion's narrowed construction as essentially advisory. 408 F.2d at 192–97. Those views did not become the court's actual judgment.
