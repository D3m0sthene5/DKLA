# Klar v. H. & M. Parcel Room, Inc.
270 A.D. 538, 61 N.Y.S.2d 285 (N.Y. App. Div., First Department, Apr. 5, 1946), aff'd without opinion, 296 N.Y. 1044, 73 N.E.2d 912 (May 15, 1947) | Contracts | Notice and assent to a liability limitation on a claim check

## The lost package and the disputed $25 ceiling

H. & M. operated a parcel room on the concourse of the Hudson & Manhattan Railroad's 33rd Street station in New York City. Ellis, acting for the plaintiffs, deposited a brown-paper package containing valuable furs and received claim check 34-971. When Sidney R. Klar presented the check two days later, an employee admitted that the parcel had mistakenly been delivered to someone presenting a differently numbered receipt. Ordinary negligence was conceded. The dispute concerned whether the parcel room could reduce its responsibility for the loss to $25. 270 A.D. 538–542.

The cardboard check measured three inches by two and a half inches. Its identifying number appeared prominently in red; the word CONTRACT also appeared in red near the top. Beneath that word, finer print stated that acceptance bound both parties, that checking cost ten cents per twenty-four hours or fraction, and that no claim exceeding $25 could be made for loss or damage to any piece. Ellis testified that he neither read the check nor was asked to read it, and thought it was simply a receipt. No evidence showed a further notice or explanation of the limitation. 270 A.D. 538–541. The assigned photograph, printed page 666, confirms that the limitation was on the face of the check; the case does not concern a clause hidden exclusively on its reverse.

The plaintiffs sued in Municipal Court for $1,000, the alleged value of the package. Following a trial without a jury, they obtained a $939.50 judgment. The Appellate Term reduced recovery to $25 because it regarded the printed limitation as sufficiently communicated to form a contract. The plaintiffs then appealed by permission to the Appellate Division. The $1,000 demand, the $939.50 judgment, and the proposed $25 contractual ceiling are different amounts with different procedural roles. 270 A.D. 541, 544.

## Majority: a receipt did not automatically establish a special contract

Justice Cohn wrote for a three-judge majority, joined by Justices Townley and Glennon. The majority accepted that a bailee for hire owes ordinary care and, absent an effective special agreement, answers for the reasonable value of property lost through negligence. It also accepted that parties may contract to reduce the bailee's common-law obligation, within the limits of law and public policy. The decisive issue was therefore whether this particular limitation had become part of their agreement. 270 A.D. 541–542.

To establish the limitation, the bailee had to show reasonable notice of its terms and the bailor's assent. A ticket issued as a means of identifying property does not necessarily communicate that it also changes substantive rights. The record contained no conspicuous signs, large placards, other notice of special terms, or evidence that the plaintiffs were afforded an opportunity to assent or dissent. Printing words on the face of the ticket, followed by its acceptance, did not compel a finding of agreement as a matter of law. 270 A.D. 542–543.

The majority relied on Madan and Healy to explain why an omission to read a parcel receipt is not invariably negligence or contractual assent. Healy involved fine print on the reverse of a check, whereas Klar's limitation appeared on the face. The majority acknowledged that difference but considered Healy's reasoning applicable: the circumstances and function of the paper could make it reasonable to regard it as identification rather than an agreement limiting recovery. Protection of a bailee's ability to bargain for reduced liability had to coexist with protection of customers against inadequately communicated terms. 270 A.D. 542–543.

The holding was fact-sensitive. Whether acceptance of this check constituted assent to the limitation was a question for the trial judge, and the finding that no such agreement existed had ample evidentiary support. The Appellate Term erred by treating acceptance as establishing the limitation. The majority did not hold that every unsigned ticket is unenforceable, that failure to read always excuses assent, or that every liability limitation is contrary to public policy. 270 A.D. 543–544.

## Dissent: adequate notice and the economics of a ten-cent service

Justice Dore dissented, joined by Presiding Justice Martin. They would have affirmed the Appellate Term's $25 recovery. The dissent viewed the conspicuous word CONTRACT, the brief text printed directly beneath it, the time available to read, and the ten-cent charge as adequate notice of a reasonable limitation. A customer paying so little should reasonably expect some ceiling on liability; unlimited responsibility for undisclosed valuables could expose parcel rooms to disproportionate losses and force prices upward. The dissent treated failure to read this particular notice as inexcusable. 270 A.D. 544–547.

Dore also questioned the credibility of the account of how the valuable furs arrived at the parcel room. The dissent reported testimony that Klar first described a $400–$450 value, whereas trial testimony valued the contents at $735 and the work at $150. It criticized the exclusion of a question about why Ellis had not returned the package directly to Klar's nearby business. Those concerns explain the dissent; they are not majority findings that the package was fabricated or that the trial judgment should be reduced for lack of proof. 270 A.D. 544, 547.

## Judgment and assigned reading

The Appellate Division reversed the Appellate Term's determination and affirmed the Municipal Court's $939.50 judgment, awarding the plaintiffs costs in both appellate courts. The reported vote was three to two. The later Court of Appeals judgment affirmed with costs and no opinion; it did not supply a further written rationale. The casebook cites that memorandum affirmance. 270 A.D. 544, 547; 296 N.Y. 1044.

The assigned material is a casebook account rather than a reproduced opinion: C&M printed 665–667, physical PDF 8–10 of the resident file labelled C&M Textbook 701-1050.pdf. It places Klar within standardized terms, notice, assent, and the comparison with Restatement § 211(3). The Restatement discussion asks how a modern approach might treat the transaction; it is not a rule announced by this 1946 court. The passage describing CONTRACT in conspicuous red lettering comes from Dore's dissent, while the ensuing receipt analysis reflects Cohn's majority, including its quotation of Healy.

The casebook's footnote invokes a 1972 newspaper account of nonunion fur work as a possible explanation for using parcel rooms. That later commentary does not prove that these plaintiffs used a nonunion contractor or violated a labor agreement. The surrounding questions on reasonable expectations and formation are editorial prompts.
