# Feld v. Henry S. Levy & Sons, Inc.
37 N.Y.2d 466, 335 N.E.2d 320 (1975) | Contracts

## Court and result
Judge Cooke wrote the New York Court of Appeals' July 8, 1975 opinion. Chief Judge Breitel and Judges Jasen, Gabrielli, Jones, Wachtler, and Fuchsberg concurred, making the decision unanimous without separate writings. The court affirmed, without costs, the Appellate Division's order sustaining denial of both parties' summary-judgment motions and answered the certified question affirmatively. The seller's good faith in discontinuing production remained a factual issue. The decision neither awarded the buyer damages nor conclusively found a breach. (466, 469, 471–472; the locators below refer to the archived judicial reproduction, independently compared with the original report.)

## The output commitment and discontinued production
Fred Feld operated the Crushed Toast Company, and Levy conducted a wholesale bread-baking business. Their June 19, 1968 written agreement required Levy to sell, and Feld to buy, all bread crumbs produced at Levy's Brooklyn factory from that date through June 18, 1969. It renewed automatically for successive one-year terms. Either party could cancel by at least six months' notice by certified mail. Neither gave cancellation notice. Feld supplied the required performance bond and later a continuation certificate for the term beginning June 19, 1969. (Lines 102–104.)

The crumbs were deliberately manufactured, rather than incidental flakes of bread. Stale or imperfect loaves had their labels removed, passed through two grinders, were toasted in a drum placed in an oven, and were bagged. Levy sold more than 250 tons to Feld before stopping production around May 15, 1969. Its comptroller presented evidence that the oven was too large for the drum and characterized the operation as very uneconomical. Levy did not then try to obtain more economical equipment. The oven was intentionally broken down, partially rebuilt, and completely dismantled during the summer; the space became a computer room. (Lines 105–107.)

Levy indicated that it would resume production if the agreed six-cent price increased to seven cents per pound. After dismantling the machinery it sold the raw materials to animal-food manufacturers. These matters were undisputed, but the record did not establish production costs, crumb profits or losses, or the net or gross return from the animal-food transactions. Those missing facts prevented treating the seller's economic explanation as either established good faith or established bad faith. (Lines 108, 124–127.)

## The competing summary-judgment positions
Feld sought summary judgment on liability. Levy sought dismissal by summary judgment, arguing that it had promised only to sell whatever crumbs it actually produced, not to continue producing them. On that view, dismantling the oven reduced output to zero and eliminated any further delivery obligation. Special Term denied both requests. A divided Appellate Division affirmed, and both parties appealed. Feld's acknowledgment that Levy claimed continued production was economically infeasible did not admit that the claim was true. The Court of Appeals therefore assessed whether the contractual language and governing good-faith duty permitted either party to prevail on the existing record. (Lines 109–110, 124–126.)

## An output contract includes a good-faith constraint
The court rejected the premise that an output contract fails merely because it specifies no fixed quantity or lacks mutuality. UCC § 2-306 measures the seller's obligation by actual output occurring in good faith, subject to its restriction on disproportionate quantities. The official comment links that standard to commercial fair dealing and reasonably foreseeable output. The court also reproduced subsection (2), imposing best efforts to supply and promote goods under an exclusive-dealing agreement unless the parties agree otherwise, and its comment describing reasonable diligence and good faith. These provisions give commercial substance to an output undertaking that otherwise might seem dependent on unrestricted seller choice. (Lines 111–120.)

The court read the agreement against its commercial background and the then-applicable UCC § 1-203 duty of good-faith performance. It acknowledged the general rule that a good-faith cessation of production can end an output seller's further obligations. But Levy had not stopped its principal bread-baking business: it had stopped a single product line. Calling continued production uneconomical or economically infeasible was conclusory, and the latter phrase could describe materially different situations. The seller's motive and financial circumstances required close factual scrutiny. (Lines 121–126.)

## Why the cancellation clause mattered
The six-month cancellation provision supplied a contractual way to end a relationship that was less profitable or advantageous than anticipated, or undesirable for another reason. Notice also gave its recipient time to locate another outlet or supply source. Until such cancellation, Levy had to perform in good faith. The court distinguished a bankruptcy or a genuine threat to the entire business, which would justify stopping the crumb line, from merely earning less profit than expected, which would not. Because this was one facet of the seller's enterprise and the contract expressly provided a cancellation mechanism, good faith could require production even without profit until cancellation. (Lines 128–130.)

The opinion did not impose an absolute duty to operate a ruinous product line. In these circumstances, and without additional justification, the court said losses exceeding the trivial could justify a good-faith cessation before cancellation. Whether that standard was met was a factual question. The proposed price increase, dismantling, and alternative raw-material sales were relevant to the seller's motives, but the absence of financial figures prevented a final determination for either side. Affirmance therefore preserved the dispute for further proceedings rather than resolving the seller's ultimate liability. (Lines 126–132, 136.)
