# Columbia Christian College, Inc. v. Commonwealth Properties, Inc.
286 Or. 321, 594 P.2d 401; rehearing, 286 Or. 669, 596 P.2d 554 (1979) | Contracts

## Court, stages, and precise disposition
Justice Howell wrote the Oregon Supreme Court’s May 8, 1979 opinion and its June 19 rehearing clarification. The original report lists Howell, Lent, and Linde and expressly notes that Presiding Justice Holman did not participate; no separate opinion is reported. The college sued for specific performance of a land sale. The trial court refused specific performance as impracticable but awarded $92,300 equitable compensation for the period during which the land was tied up. Commonwealth appealed the compensation award; the college cross-appealed for more compensation but did not appeal denial of specific performance. Reviewing the equity suit de novo, the supreme court removed the compensation award and awarded Commonwealth return of its $35,000 option payments, while affirming the denial of specific performance. Costs were awarded to neither party. On rehearing it withdrew one explanation but adhered to its factual conclusions and denied the petition. (321–323, 337; 671.)

## Option and extended commitment to purchase
The college owned about 268 acres in east Multnomah County and sought a buyer for residential development. Its 1973 offer provided a 180-day option for $10,000, credited toward the purchase price. Commonwealth would investigate engineering and geological suitability, seek annexation into Gresham, obtain satisfactory zoning, and seek utility permission. An extension provision required Commonwealth to purchase, subject to satisfactory government decisions, if it exercised the extension while applications remained pending. (323–324.)

After initial payments and preliminary work, the parties agreed in February 1974 to a six-month extension for $25,000 instead of the original clause’s ninety days for $10,000. The court rejected Commonwealth’s argument that this difference left it wholly free of the original purchase obligation. The evidence showed the parties retained the remaining terms, including the condition-based purchase commitment. Further extensions followed, including a December 1974 extension supported by an increase in the proposed per-acre purchase price from $3,800 to $4,100. Thus, the court recognized a contractual duty; it did not resolve the case by calling the arrangement only a nonbinding invitation to negotiate. (325–326, 329–330.)

## Unsatisfactory zoning despite conditional approval
Commonwealth first sought approval of a concept plan. The planning commission recommended approval only if concerns raised by staff and the public were solved in a final plan, followed by further hearings. The concerns included drainage and offsite traffic improvements. Commonwealth estimated that research to address them would cost another $100,000 and take six months, with approval still uncertain and offsite expenditures potentially exceeding the parties’ original expectations. It sought the December extension to ask the city council for approval without these conditions. The council instead approved the zoning ordinance in February 1975 conditioned on resolving the concerns in the final plan. (325–327 & n.2.)

Commonwealth subsequently participated in document preparation and requested a title report, while the seller’s broker and Commonwealth’s representatives gave conflicting accounts of conversations about proceeding. Ultimately the college’s attorney received a July letter saying unsatisfactory zoning ended the transaction. The court considered the intervening preparation evidence but concluded that exploring whether to proceed on a transaction of this magnitude did not establish satisfaction with the zoning or preclude reliance on the condition. (327–329, 333.)

## Genuine dissatisfaction can coexist with other motives
The court distinguished satisfaction with the zoning actually obtained from the duty to make reasonable efforts to obtain satisfactory zoning. It agreed that satisfactory zoning had not been obtained: the ordinance left wide discretion over the final plan and suggested substantial offsite demands not contemplated at formation. The seller argued the real motive for withdrawal was a housing-market decline and Commonwealth’s preference for smaller projects. The court required dissatisfaction to concern the condition’s subject matter but did not require it to be the party’s sole dissatisfaction. A genuine contractual ground for refusing performance was not defeated merely because other concerns also made the transaction unattractive. (331–332.)

Here the zoning requirements themselves affected economic feasibility. After reviewing the evidence de novo, the court found Commonwealth’s zoning dissatisfaction in good faith. The relevant conclusion was genuine dissatisfaction with the particular conditioned approval, not a license to manufacture dissatisfaction solely to avoid a bad bargain. The assigned textbook’s example of obtaining cheaper supplies is a hypothetical introduction to the mixed-motive principle; this actual case concerned land-development approval and its burdens. (332–333.)

## Reasonable efforts did not require unlimited expenditure
Commonwealth also had a duty reasonably to pursue satisfactory zoning. Yet it did not have to spend $100,000 and six further months researching solutions for an already marginal project where city comments suggested potentially excessive offsite obligations. Nor was it required to submit a final plan without the research demanded by the ordinance simply to go through the motions. Failure to file that final application was not a breach of the reasonable-efforts obligation on this record. The court also rejected the suggestion that not asking what the ordinance meant was unreasonable; it found the ordinance clear. (334 & n.3.)

The May opinion addressed Commonwealth’s knowledge of the burdens when it sought the December extension, saying this did not relate back to formation. The June opinion expressly withdrew the relate-back explanation as unnecessary. It clarified that later knowledge was not without consequence, but Commonwealth could seek an extension to try for unconditional council approval without committing itself either to abandon immediately or to proceed regardless of the council’s eventual conditions. The final account must preserve that clarification rather than repeat the withdrawn language as the court’s continuing rationale. (335; 671.)

## Compensation denied and deposits returned
Because Commonwealth reasonably pursued the approval and the satisfaction condition failed in good faith, it was excused from purchasing. The college had bargained for the possibility that the property would be off the market without a completed sale, so that occurrence alone did not entitle it to equitable compensation. The court deleted the $92,300 award. (335, 337.)

The court separately granted Commonwealth’s counterclaim for its $35,000 payments. Although it would not ordinarily expect refundable consideration for an option lasting nearly two years, the evidence established this particular refund agreement. The accepted extension letter expressly contemplated return of the money if satisfactory zoning could not be obtained, and the broker’s testimony confirmed that the parties rejected a proposed forfeiture. The court would not relieve the seller from an inequitable bargain knowingly made. The refund therefore rested on proved contract terms, not a rule that all option payments are automatically refundable. (335–337 & n.4.)
