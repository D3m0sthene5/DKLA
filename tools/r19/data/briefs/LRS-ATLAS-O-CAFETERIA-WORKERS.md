# Cafeteria & Restaurant Workers Union, Local 473, AFL-CIO v. McElroy
367 U.S. 886 (1961) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Decision, stage, and dispute

On June 19, 1961, the Supreme Court affirmed the D.C. Circuit’s en banc judgment denying relief to Rachel Brawner and her union. Justice Stewart’s five-Justice opinion held that the commanding officer had explicit authority to exclude her from the Naval Gun Factory and that, in the circumstances shown, withdrawal of her access without specific charges or a hearing did not violate Fifth Amendment due process. Justice Brennan dissented, joined by Chief Justice Warren and Justices Black and Douglas. The four dissenters questioned whether recognition of protection from arbitrary government action could coexist with denial of any means of discovering or contesting its real basis. (886–890, 894–902.)

Brawner was a private concessionaire’s employee, not a federal civil servant dismissed from the federal payroll. The immediate government action withdrew permission to enter one military installation, making her unable to continue working at its cafeteria. The majority did not hold that government employees lack all constitutional protection or that an announced security rationale makes every kind of exclusion lawful. It assumed limits against patently arbitrary or discriminatory grounds but rejected the demanded procedure on this record. (887–888, 894–899.)

## Employment, base access, and badge withdrawal

Brawner had worked for more than six years as a short-order cook for M & M Restaurants, Inc., and her employer considered her performance entirely satisfactory. The cafeteria operated under a concession contract inside the federally owned Gun Factory in Washington. The installation designed, produced, and inspected naval ordnance, including highly classified weapons systems. Guards controlled entry; persons authorized to enter held identification badges issued by the Security Officer under the commanding Superintendent, Rear Admiral Tyree. (887.)

The concession contract prohibited engagement of anyone failing the installation’s security or other regulatory requirements as determined by the Security Officer. On November 15, 1956, Lieutenant Commander Williams required Brawner to return her badge because he determined that she failed the installation’s security requirements. Tyree approved the action and cited the contractual provision. At the union’s request, M & M sought a meeting concerning denial of access, but Tyree said it would serve no useful purpose. Brawner remained excluded. M & M offered her work at another restaurant in suburban Washington; she declined because its location was inconvenient. (888.)

The underlying security concern was not disclosed. The majority expressly stated that nobody had accused Brawner of disloyalty or intentional wrongdoing; the category of security requirements could encompass other matters, such as carelessness with a badge. That was a description of what the record did and did not establish, not a finding that carelessness actually caused her exclusion. Brennan disputed the majority’s optimistic characterization of the stigma, reasoning that the public would understand “security risk” far more ominously. (898–899; 901–902.)

## Suit and authority question

Brawner and the union sued the Secretary of Defense, Superintendent, and Security Officer personally and officially, seeking, among other relief, restoration of the badge and access so that she could resume work. The District Court granted the defendants summary judgment and dismissed the complaint. A divided appellate panel initially reversed, but its opinion was withdrawn on rehearing. The en banc D.C. Circuit affirmed with four dissenting judges. The Supreme Court granted review because of the claimed conflict with Greene v. McElroy. (888–889 & n.2.)

Greene had refused to infer authorization for a procedure revoking an aeronautical engineer’s clearance, seriously impairing pursuit of his profession, without confronting and cross-examining adverse witnesses. Its disposition rested on the absence of explicit congressional or presidential authorization rather than a final constitutional holding. Stewart applied an explicit-authorization requirement here while putting aside the government’s argument for a less demanding delegation standard based on the differing facts. He then asked whether, if authorized, the exclusion violated the Constitution. (889–890.)

## Explicit authorization through Navy regulations

Control of entry onto a military installation fell within Congress’s military and property powers and the President’s commander-in-chief power. Congress charged the Secretary of the Navy with administration and custody of naval property, allowed appropriate departmental regulations, and required presidential approval of Navy Regulations. President Truman had expressly approved the pertinent regulations on August 9, 1948. That approval gave the regulations legal sanction, reinforced by historical statutes and decisions. (890–891 & nn.3–5.)

Article 0701 made the commanding officer’s authority commensurate with his command responsibility, subject to law and the regulations. More specifically, Article 0734 generally excluded tradesmen and their agents except as the commanding officer authorized them for public business, requested private business, or needed services and supplies. The Court regarded Brawner’s concession employment as precisely within that category. It also read the regulation against a long history of commanders controlling private persons’ access to military property. The explicit regulation, rather than the concession contract alone, supplied the authority missing in Greene. (891–894 & nn.6–7.)

The majority’s reference to broad or “absolute” withdrawal power addressed that authorization. It did not terminate the inquiry: the Court separately considered the constitutional limits on exercising the granted power. (894–899.)

## Context-dependent due process

Stewart rejected the easy assertion that because Brawner had no constitutional right to enter the installation she could have no protected interest affected by exclusion. Due process does not invariably require a trial-type hearing, but procedure must be assessed in light of the precise government function and private interest. The assignment’s quotation of that formulation is from report 895; its printed locator to 886 points to the case’s beginning rather than the location of the quoted judicial reasoning. (894–895 & n.8; assigned PDF 42–43.)

The private interest, as the majority understood it, was work at one isolated military installation rather than the ability to practice an entire trade. Brawner could remain a cook for M & M elsewhere or work for another employer. Government acted as military proprietor managing internal affairs rather than as regulator or licensor of an occupation or whole business sector. The Court contrasted professional-qualification cases and analogized to government’s historically broad control over its own employment arrangements. (895–897.)

The majority also acknowledged that government, even in its internal operations, does not enjoy a private employer’s complete freedom. United Public Workers and Wieman recognized constitutional constraints. The Court assumed that exclusion on an openly arbitrary ground, such as being a Democrat or Methodist, would be unconstitutional. It nevertheless found no requirement for specific charges and a hearing where the stated security ground was rational and consistent with the concession agreement. An assumed substantive limitation did not, in its view, entail a hearing right in every exclusion. (897–898.)

Finally, the record did not show a badge of disloyalty or infamy foreclosing other work. Government counsel represented that loss of this access would not legally or factually prevent employment on other federal property. The Court did not adjudicate Brawner’s character; it decided that the limited exclusion, on the facts shown and the traditional military-proprietary authority involved, did not require the procedure demanded. (898–899 & nn.9–10.)

## Brennan’s dissent and the issue of meaningful protection

Brennan expressed serious doubts about statutory or presidential authorization, but accepted the majority’s determination for purposes of addressing the more consequential constitutional issue. He read it as acknowledging a sufficiently definite interest protected against at least some injuries, including openly discriminatory exclusion. The problem was that an unelaborated reference to security requirements could conceal precisely those forbidden grounds. A rule accepting that assertion without inquiry made the supposed substantive protection ineffectual unless an official admitted discrimination. (899–900.)

The dissent did not insist that Brawner must receive a complete trial, confrontation, cross-examination, and proof beyond reasonable doubt. It insisted on some procedures: an explanation of what she was said to have done and a chance to reply. Otherwise, she might be the victim of false allegations or official caprice without knowing it or being able to demonstrate the defect. Brennan viewed a right against arbitrary action with no procedural means of testing arbitrariness as internally contradictory. (900–901.)

He also rejected the view that the designation had no broader reputational consequence. In contemporary public understanding, being treated as a security risk suggested communism or disloyalty rather than harmless garrulousness. Even if the government ultimately had a sound reason for exclusion, it should not affix that potentially infamous designation and destroy a valuable relationship without any statement of charges and opportunity to answer. The dissent disputed adequacy of process; it did not find as fact that Brawner was disloyal or that her exclusion necessarily lacked any just cause. (901–902.)

## Judgment, assigned correction, and scope

The final judgment was “Affirmed,” sustaining denial of the requested relief. The full decision resolved explicit command authority and constitutional procedure separately; it rejected a categorical right-versus-privilege shortcut without requiring a hearing in these circumstances. The assigned account spans October-b PDF 42–43 / printed 471–472, ending before the separate Greene discussion on page 43. Its description of “dismissal on loyalty grounds” must be qualified: the actual majority expressly says that neither disloyalty nor intentional wrongdoing had been charged and that the record showed only failure of this installation’s security requirements. (894–899.)
