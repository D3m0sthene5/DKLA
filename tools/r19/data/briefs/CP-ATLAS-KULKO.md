# Kulko v. Superior Court of California

**436 U.S. 84 (1978) · Supreme Court of the United States · May 15, 1978**

## How the children came to California

Ezra Kulko and Sharon Kulko, later Horn, were New York residents and domiciliaries. They married in California in 1959 during Ezra’s three-day military stopover en route to Korea. Sharon returned to New York, and Ezra returned there after his service and another brief California stopover. Their children, Darwin and Ilsa, were born in New York in 1961 and 1962. The family lived together in New York until the spouses separated in March 1972. The California wedding was thus a brief event in a marriage whose family life was centered in New York. 436 U.S. at 86–87, 93.

Sharon moved to San Francisco. She returned to New York to sign their September 1972 separation agreement, under which the children would live with Ezra during the school year and visit their mother during specified vacations. Sharon waived her own support, while Ezra agreed to pay $3,000 annually for the periods when the children were in her care. Sharon then obtained a Haitian divorce incorporating the agreement and returned to California, where she remarried. The full opinion clarifies that she appeared in Haiti and Ezra supplied a power of attorney; it does not find that both traveled there. Id. at 87 & n.1.

In December 1973, Ilsa told Ezra that she wanted to remain in California after her Christmas visit. Ezra agreed and bought her a one-way ticket. She thereafter spent the school year with her mother and vacations with her father. In January 1976, Darwin separately asked his mother to let him join her. Sharon sent him a ticket without Ezra’s knowledge, and he moved to California. Those different arrangements matter: the California courts based jurisdiction principally on Ezra’s consent to Ilsa’s move, not on a finding that he had arranged Darwin’s. Id. at 87–89.

## A support claim, distinct from custody

Shortly after Darwin arrived, Sharon sued in California to establish the Haitian decree as a California judgment, obtain full custody, and increase Ezra’s child-support obligations. Ezra appeared specially and moved to quash service for lack of personal jurisdiction. He did not contest California’s jurisdiction for the custody determination; his objection on review concerned the personal obligation to pay increased support. The trial court denied his motion, and he sought a writ of mandate. California’s appellate courts sustained jurisdiction. Id. at 88–89 & n.2.

The California Supreme Court, divided 4–2, treated Ezra’s consent to Ilsa’s move as an out-of-state act causing an effect within California. It concluded that he had purposefully obtained the benefits of California’s laws and services through the child’s presence and that asserting jurisdiction was reasonable. It also considered reduced household expenses a benefit. Having found jurisdiction through his acts concerning Ilsa, it sustained jurisdiction for the support of both children. Id. at 89–90, 94–95.

The U.S. Supreme Court dismissed the formal appellate route but treated the papers as a certiorari petition and granted review. Ezra had disputed jurisdiction under the Constitution rather than properly challenging the constitutionality of California’s statute, which itself extended jurisdiction only to constitutional limits. Sharon also argued that his later request to continue proceedings waived the jurisdictional objection. The Court rejected that contention: the cited California authorities did not support it, and the trial court itself still characterized his appearance as special. The issue remained live despite intervening orders concerning arrears and increased support. Id. at 90–91 & nn.4–5.

## Holding: the family arrangement did not supply the necessary contacts

Justice Marshall’s majority held that California’s exercise of personal jurisdiction over Ezra for this support action violated due process. Adequate notice was undisputed; the problem was the quality and nature of his connection with California. Minimum contacts required a fair and reasonable connection attributable to the defendant, assessed in the circumstances rather than by a mechanical rule. Id. at 91–92.

The Court first excluded the brief military stopovers and wedding as a basis for this later, unrelated support action. Nor had California’s highest court relied on the original vacation arrangement. Treating agreement to children’s visits as purposeful forum activity could discourage reasonable visitation arrangements and expose a parent to suit wherever the other parent chose to live. The separation agreement’s provision for mailing payments likewise contemplated whatever address Sharon might later designate, rather than establishing a deliberately California-centered undertaking. Id. at 93–94 & n.6.

The majority then rejected the proposed significance of Ilsa’s move. Accommodating a child’s preference for family harmony was not an effort by Ezra to obtain California’s benefits and protections. Schools, hospitals, and other local services principally benefited the child; they were not benefits the father had purposefully sought for himself. Any reduction in household expense resulted from her absence from his New York home, rather than her presence in California specifically. Whether more support was owed could be litigated in New York. The possibility of a lower current expense therefore did not resolve where jurisdiction existed. Id. at 94–96 & nn.7–10.

## Why the effects theory and California’s interests were insufficient

The California court’s effects approach drew on Restatement (Second) of Conflict of Laws § 37. The Supreme Court explained that the Restatement contemplated wrongful out-of-state conduct producing in-state injury or commercial activity affecting residents, subject to an express reasonableness qualification. Ezra had neither inflicted the kind of physical injury illustrated by that provision nor sought a commercial benefit through California transactions. This dispute arose from a New York separation and a contract negotiated and signed there. Agreeing to a child’s move was not comparable to an insurer’s solicitation of a California policyholder. Id. at 96–97 & n.11.

The Court also considered the practical burden on family relations. Ezra remained in the marital domicile while Sharon moved across the country. His accommodating the child’s preference should not force him to defend a support action roughly 3,000 miles away. A rule turning jurisdiction on whether he bought a ticket or unsuccessfully opposed the departure could penalize cooperative parenting without a sufficient forum connection. The Court expressed this conclusion regardless of the merits of Sharon’s claim for additional support. Id. at 97–98.

California’s interest in its resident children was substantial, but did not itself establish jurisdiction over this defendant. The possibility that California law might appropriately govern some aspect of the dispute was also distinct from California’s adjudicative power. The majority further explained that reciprocal support statutes offered a means of securing support without requiring either parent to travel to the other’s state. A claimant could initiate proceedings in California, transmit them to a responding New York court with jurisdiction over the alleged obligor, and obtain adjudication and collection there. California used the revised 1968 uniform statute; New York used a similar earlier uniform scheme. The Court identified those available mechanisms rather than itself deciding what additional payments Ezra owed. Id. at 98–100 & nn.13–15.

## Dissent and judgment

Justice Brennan dissented, joined by Justices White and Powell. He accepted the same minimum-contacts standard and regarded the question as a close application to these particular facts. His independent weighing agreed with California’s conclusion that Ezra’s connection was sufficient under constitutional reasonableness and fairness. The brief dissent did not propose a categorical rule permitting jurisdiction in every state where a child resides. Id. at 101–102.

Marshall’s opinion had six votes; Brennan’s dissent had three. The Court reversed the California Supreme Court because Ezra’s motion to quash on the support jurisdiction issue had been erroneously denied. It did not eliminate the children’s right to support, set a new support amount, or decide the uncontested custody jurisdiction issue. Id. at 101.
