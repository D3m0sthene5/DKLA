# North Georgia Finishing, Inc. v. Di-Chem, Inc.

**419 U.S. 601 (1975), decided January 22, 1975. Civil Procedure.** Justice White wrote for Douglas, Brennan, Stewart, and Marshall. Stewart also supplied a concurring statement; Powell concurred in the judgment. Blackmun dissented with Rehnquist, and Chief Justice Burger dissented for the reasons in paragraph 5 of Blackmun’s opinion. The judgment was 6–3, with five Justices joining the Court’s reasoning. The assigned accounts appear on CivPro physical page 166, printed 284, following Mitchell in Note 3, and physical page 601, printed 1187–1188, before the Doehr discussion.

## A commercial debt and a frozen account

Di-Chem sued North Georgia Finishing in Georgia’s Whitfield County Superior Court on August 20, 1971, alleging a $51,279.17 debt for goods sold and delivered. At the same time, before the complaint was served, it obtained garnishment of North Georgia’s account at the First National Bank of Dalton. Di-Chem’s president supplied an affidavit stating the alleged debt and his apprehension that some or all would be lost unless garnishment issued. The clerk issued the process and the bank was served that day. 419 U.S. at 603–604.

The statutory scheme did require security: the creditor posted a bond at least double the claimed debt against costs and damages from wrongful garnishment. The debtor could free the property by posting its own bond securing the prospective judgment. North Georgia did so on August 23, and the judge discharged the bank as garnishee. On September 15 it moved to dismiss the writ and discharge its bond, asserting constitutional objections. That motion was denied in November. The case passed through Georgia’s appellate courts, and the Georgia Supreme Court ultimately sustained the statute. Id. at 602–605 & nn.1–3.

The statute allowed the creditor, its agent, or its attorney to make the affidavit. An attorney could swear on knowledge and belief without personal knowledge. The affidavit need state the amount and apprehension of loss, rather than particular facts explaining why collection was endangered. Process could issue through the clerk, without prior judicial assessment or notice to the debtor. The statutory provisions did not assure a prompt factual hearing at which the creditor had to justify continued garnishment. Id. at 602–603 n.1, 607.

## Why temporary commercial deprivation counted

The Georgia Supreme Court had treated Sniadach as a special exception for wage earners to an otherwise permissible garnishment practice. The Court rejected that understanding as inadequate after Fuentes. Temporary deprivation of use and possession is still a deprivation of property, and a bank account is plainly property. The account was placed beyond North Georgia’s use pending litigation unless it supplied a bond. The state therefore had to provide procedures against erroneous seizure even though ownership was not finally transferred. Id. at 605–606.

Di-Chem argued that consumer repossessions and wage garnishment differed from a commercial dispute between corporations with equal bargaining power. It also emphasized its double bond. The Court acknowledged that loss of household necessities might more commonly cause irreparable harm, but freezing a business’s bank account also posed a sufficiently serious risk. The commercial setting did not remove due-process protection. A damages bond was one safeguard, but it did not cure the absence of procedures testing the basis for the initial restraint. Id. at 608.

## The decisive contrast with Mitchell

Mitchell had upheld Louisiana sequestration under a coordinated set of safeguards. The creditor held a vendor’s lien in the goods, supplied factual verification beyond conclusory assertions, obtained a judge’s authorization, and faced an immediate post-seizure hearing at which it had to prove the grounds for the writ. North Georgia’s writ, by contrast, issued on a conclusory affidavit through a clerk without judicial participation. The only dissolution mechanism apparent on the statute’s face was a debtor’s bond. No early hearing required the creditor to establish even probable cause for the restraint. Id. at 606–607.

The holding consequently does not say that every prejudgment garnishment requires a full trial in advance. It holds this Georgia scheme deficient because it lacked the protections that could prevent or promptly correct an erroneous deprivation. The Court observed that the debtor apparently could not challenge the writ without first posting a bond; note 5 preserves the parties’ positions and the respondent’s unsupported assertion that other challenges were available. That discussion should not be converted into a determination of every possible procedural route under Georgia law. Id. at 607 & n.5.

## The separate writings

Stewart’s brief concurrence welcomed the apparent survival of Fuentes after his earlier criticism that Mitchell had effectively overruled it. He joined the Court’s opinion; his statement did not announce a separate test. Id. at 608.

Powell agreed that the Georgia scheme failed but thought the Court relied too broadly on Fuentes and confined Mitchell too narrowly. His preferred accommodation would require adequate creditor security and a factual basis, presented to a neutral officer, for fearing removal or dissipation of assets. A prompt judicial hearing would then place on the creditor the burden of showing probable cause to continue the restraint. Because the attached assets might be unrelated to the underlying debt, the debtor should also be able to substitute adequate security. Id. at 609–612.

Powell expressly disagreed that the issuing official necessarily had to be a judge. A clerk or other neutral court officer could issue the initial writ on a proper affidavit if a prompt hearing before a judge and the creditor’s bond allowed errors to be corrected. The core defect was Georgia’s failure to provide prompt, adequate factual review without conditioning access on the debtor’s bond. His note 5 even suggested that the actual circumstances might justify a brief restraint: other assets appeared encumbered, while bank funds were readily transferable. The problem was that the statutory affidavit did not disclose such a basis. These are his concurring reasons, rather than all elements of the five-Justice opinion. Id. at 611 n.3, 612–614 & nn.5–6.

Blackmun and Rehnquist would have affirmed. Blackmun criticized the unstable relation among Sniadach, Fuentes, and Mitchell and the uncertainty left for other states’ commercial remedies. He treated wage garnishment as distinct and argued that Mitchell had substantially narrowed Fuentes. His discussion of Fuentes’s four-Justice majority and the historical Legal Tender Cases was an argument about constitutional decision-making, not an alternative holding of North Georgia. Id. at 614–619.

In paragraph 5, Blackmun considered the protections adequate for this commercial dispute: the creditor had to institute the underlying suit, supply its affidavit, and post a double bond; the debtor could dissolve the restraint through its own bond. He considered the clerk-versus-judge distinction slight when the official was independent of the creditor. Burger joined those paragraph-5 reasons only, rather than Blackmun’s full criticism of precedent and Court membership. Id. at 619–620.

## Exact result and assigned emphasis

The Court **reversed the Georgia Supreme Court and remanded for further proceedings consistent with its opinion**. It invalidated the challenged garnishment procedure under the Fourteenth Amendment; it did not determine whether Di-Chem ultimately could prove the underlying debt. Id. at 608.

Both assigned accounts emphasize the need for an early hearing or another safeguard against mistaken seizure. The complete report also shows that a creditor’s double bond already existed and nevertheless was insufficient, that the property was a corporation’s account rather than household goods, and that Powell’s proposed rule differed from the Court’s treatment of judicial authorization. Those distinctions keep the case from becoming a generalized rule that any bond validates seizure, or that every temporary restraint automatically requires identical process. This brief describes the 1975 decision and does not certify present Georgia procedures or later doctrinal treatment.
