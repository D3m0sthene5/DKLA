# Swanson v. Citibank, N.A.

**Civil Procedure · CP-071 · 614 F.3d 400 (7th Cir. 2010).** Assigned: CivPro.pdf, PDF pp. 353–356, printed pp. 672–677. Judge Wood wrote the majority opinion, joined by Chief Judge Easterbrook. Judge Posner dissented in part, joining the opinion except for reversal of the housing-discrimination dismissal.

## The loan application and competing explanations

Gloria Swanson alleged that Citibank, appraiser Andre Lanier, and his employer PCI Appraisal Services discriminated against her because she was African-American. The majority accepted the pleaded events as true for purposes of reviewing dismissal, without finding discriminatory conduct as a fact. In February 2009, encouraged by Citibank's announcement of loans funded through the federal Troubled Assets Relief Program, she applied for a home-equity loan. A bank representative, Skertich, said that her husband had to participate because they jointly owned the house. Swanson suspected that this was a discouragement tactic; a manager warned her that Citibank used stricter loan criteria than other banks. She disclosed that Washington Mutual had previously denied her a home-equity loan. (¶¶ 7–8.)

Swanson returned with the application information. Skertich said answering the race question was optional and mentioned that his wife and son were part African-American. Citibank conditionally approved a $50,000 loan, initially using her estimate that the house was worth $270,000. Lanier's appraisal valued it at $170,000, and Citibank denied the loan because the conditionally assumed valuation was not met. Two months later, an appraisal Swanson paid Midwest Valuations to perform valued the house at $240,000. (¶¶ 9–10.)

She alleged that the defendants deliberately depressed the appraisal to avoid lending to an African-American applicant. The dispute's competing explanations were racial manipulation and an ordinary mistaken appraisal or insufficient security. At this stage neither explanation had been established through evidence. (¶¶ 11, 24, 34.)

## Procedure and the scope of the appeal

Swanson's amended complaint invoked the Fair Housing Act and Equal Credit Opportunity Act. The district court dismissed the discrimination claims, relying heavily on Latimore v. Citibank, a decision about the evidence needed to survive summary judgment. It initially allowed a liberally construed fraud theory to proceed, then dismissed that as well because the statements were too indefinite and reliance unreasonable. On appeal Swanson pursued only Fair Housing Act and fraud claims. The Equal Credit Opportunity Act dismissals were therefore outside the appellate challenge. (¶¶ 11, 20, 23.)

Swanson also named her husband Charles Routen as plaintiff and appellant, but she could not represent him while proceeding without counsel. The court dismissed him as a party on appeal and addressed only her claims. This qualification is omitted from the excerpt and matters when stating who obtained relief. (¶ 7.)

## The majority's understanding of plausibility

Wood accepted that Twombly had retired the old idea that any imaginable set of facts consistent with a pleading sufficed. The question was how to reconcile plausibility with Rule 8's continuing requirement of a short and plain statement, Erickson's fair-notice reasoning, and the distinction between Rule 8, Rule 9, and specialized statutory pleading rules. The court refused to treat the Supreme Court's decisions as silently restoring the older code fact-pleading regime. (¶¶ 12–16.)

For the majority, a plaintiff had to supply enough case-specific detail to tell a coherent story and identify the nature and grounds of the claim. Plausibility did not mean determining which account was true, imposing a more-likely-than-not test, or choosing the plaintiff's inference only if it was more compelling than the defendant's. Complex allegations could demand greater explanation of how events connected; abstract recitation of legal elements would not identify a particular dispute. Straightforward discrimination allegations could be sufficient with less detail. Wood drew support from Twombly's favorable treatment of Swierkiewicz, which rejected a special heightened pleading rule for employment discrimination. (¶¶ 17–18.)

The majority recognized that screening claims could reduce expensive, asymmetric discovery. But it also emphasized the limits of interpreting existing rules and the role of formal rulemaking. Those policy concerns did not authorize substituting a different pleading regime for Rule 8. This was the majority's application of existing Supreme Court decisions, not a holding that discrimination cases were categorically exempt from plausibility. (¶ 19.)

## Separate analysis of the defendants and claims

The Fair Housing Act covers racial discrimination in residential real-estate transactions, including secured loans and appraisals. Against Citibank, Swanson identified the asserted discrimination, the actors, and the early-2009 loan transaction. Those specifics sufficiently identified her case. Her additional account of federal funding, the prior loan denial, and Skertich's family did not necessarily plead her out of court. Whether they supported or undermined her proof could be addressed later. (¶¶ 20–21.)

Against Lanier and PCI, she alleged deliberate race-based undervaluation in a specific appraisal transaction, and the appraisers knew her race. The court found those allegations sufficient whether she meant coordinated conduct with Citibank or independent discrimination by the appraisers. But the majority expressly warned that merely showing different appraisal values would not supply enough evidence at summary judgment. Its criticism of the district court's reliance on Latimore therefore concerned procedural stage: an evidentiary requirement was not automatically a pleading requirement. (¶¶ 11, 23–24.)

Fraud had a different result under Rule 9(b) and the applicable substantive damages rules. Swanson alleged that Citibank falsely promised to make government-supported loans while intending to exclude African-American borrowers. Yet she did not identify out-of-pocket loss from applying, such as an application fee or an appraisal charge imposed by Citibank. The court lacked the particular information needed to support actual reliance damages. Her separate later appraisal did not itself allege that she bore the bank's original appraisal cost. The claim against Lanier and PCI likewise failed to allege adequate reliance on their appraisal or resulting out-of-pocket loss. The fraud dismissals were properly sustained against all three. (¶¶ 22, 25.)

## Posner's partial dissent

Posner joined the fraud result but would affirm the housing-discrimination dismissal too. He understood Iqbal to apply generally, including to uncomplicated discrimination cases, and to require attention to obvious innocent explanations rather than merely identifying a possible discriminatory story. Swierkiewicz barred a special judicial amendment of heightened pleading rules; it did not remove discrimination claims from Iqbal's ordinary plausibility inquiry. Erickson, in his view, involved allegations naturally supporting deliberate indifference without the strong alternative explanation present here. (¶¶ 29–33, 37–40.)

The dissent emphasized the loan's financial context. Swanson already owed $146,000 against the house, so adding $50,000 would produce $196,000 total debt. At the appraiser's $170,000 figure the loan would be undersecured. Even accepting that appraisal as mistaken, the pleaded earlier $260,000 valuation and subsequent 16–20 percent decline suggested a value around $208,000–$218,400. Under a hypothetical 20-percent-equity requirement, those numbers would support only about $20,400–$28,720 in additional borrowing. Posner did not establish that Citibank actually applied that precise equity requirement; he used it to explain why rejection could readily be nondiscriminatory. (¶¶ 34–36.)

A previous bank's rejection, tightening credit in the financial crisis, and the commonplace possibility of appraisal error further supported his account. He regarded the assertion of discriminatory motive as speculation insufficient to overcome the obvious alternative. He also stressed that permitting asymmetric corporate discovery could pressure settlement irrespective of merits. The complete dissent adds that limited preliminary discovery might sometimes assist a plaintiff unable to investigate adequately, but nobody proposed that solution here. These were reasons for his proposed dismissal, not findings binding on the majority's revived claims. (¶¶ 35–36, 41–44.)

## Judgment and source scope

**The court reversed dismissal of Swanson's Fair Housing Act claims against all three defendants and affirmed dismissal of her common-law fraud claims against all three. Each side bore its own appellate costs.** The housing claims could proceed, but there was no finding of discrimination or damages award. The panel divided 2–1 on those claims; all three joined the fraud disposition. (¶¶ 26, 29, 45.)
