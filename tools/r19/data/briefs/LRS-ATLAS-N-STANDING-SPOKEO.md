# Spokeo, Inc. v. Robins

**578 U.S. 330 (2016) — Supreme Court of the United States — May 16, 2016.** Justice Alito wrote for six Justices: Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Kennedy, Thomas, Breyer, and Kagan joined. Justice Thomas also concurred separately. Justice Ginsburg dissented, joined by Justice Sotomayor. The assigned account appears in the November continuation at physical page 156, printed page 1240.

The Court vacated a ruling that an inaccurate online personal profile supplied Article III standing to pursue a statutory consumer-reporting claim. It did not decide that the plaintiff lacked standing. Instead, it held that the Ninth Circuit had considered whether the alleged injury was personal to him without adequately considering whether it was concrete. A statutory procedural violation could sometimes supply concrete injury, but violation of a statutory requirement alone did not automatically do so.

## Facts and statutory framework

Spokeo operated a people-search website. A user could enter a name, telephone number, or email address, and Spokeo would search databases and assemble information about the individual. Its services could be used by employers evaluating applicants, as well as people researching romantic partners or seeking other personal information. The complaint did not identify who requested Thomas Robins' profile or explain exactly how he learned about it. **333, 335–336.**

Robins alleged that the profile falsely described him as married with children, in his fifties, employed, affluent, and holding a graduate degree. The dissent further described his allegation that he was unemployed and seeking work, that the report used someone else's photograph, and that its misinformation threatened his employment prospects. His briefing explained how apparently favorable errors could hurt him: a prospective employer might see him as overqualified, expensive to hire, or unlikely to relocate because of family commitments. These were asserted harms, not adjudicated facts. **336, 350.**

The Fair Credit Reporting Act regulated consumer reporting agencies and reports used for purposes including credit, insurance, and employment. It required reasonable procedures to ensure maximum possible accuracy and imposed other notice, employment-reporting, and disclosure duties. For willful noncompliance, it allowed actual damages or statutory damages of $100 to $1,000 per violation, litigation costs and fees, and potentially punitive damages. The Court assumed for this opinion that Spokeo qualified as a consumer reporting agency; it did not decide that classification. **334–335 & nn.1–4.**

## Procedure and issue

Robins filed a class-action complaint in the Central District of California alleging willful FCRA violations. The District Court initially denied a jurisdictional dismissal motion, reconsidered, and dismissed with prejudice because Robins had not adequately pleaded injury in fact. The Ninth Circuit reversed. It relied on circuit precedent that violation of a statutory right was usually enough and emphasized that Spokeo allegedly violated Robins' own rights concerning his individualized information. **336–337 & n.5.**

The Supreme Court reviewed whether that reasoning established Article III standing. A proposed class did not dispense with the named plaintiff's obligation to allege his own injury. The opinion concentrated on injury in fact, while retaining the familiar additional requirements of traceability and likely redressability. At the pleading stage, the plaintiff had to allege facts demonstrating the necessary elements. **337–339 & n.6.**

## Particularization is not concreteness

The Court explained that injury must be both particularized and concrete, as well as actual or imminent. Particularization asks whether the harm affects the plaintiff personally and individually. The Ninth Circuit's observations—that the rights were Robins' and his information was individualized—addressed that question. They did not independently show that an actual harm existed. A harm shared by many people can still be particularized; the Court gave mass-tort victims as an example. **339–340 & n.7.**

Concreteness asks whether the injury is real rather than abstract. It does not demand that every injury be physical, financial, or otherwise tangible. Historically recognized intangible harms can qualify. In evaluating a statutory intangible injury, the Court identified two important guides: a close relationship to harms traditionally supporting lawsuits in English or American courts, and Congress's judgment about which harms warrant protection. Congress could recognize previously inadequate injuries and establish rights aimed at preventing them, but could not eliminate the Constitution's requirement of an actual concrete injury. **340–341.**

That distinction explains why the existence of a statutory cause of action was insufficient by itself. A procedural defect entirely disconnected from concrete harm could not establish standing merely because a statute authorized suit. At the same time, a statutory procedural violation could constitute injury in some circumstances without an additional injury beyond what Congress identified. The Court discussed informational-rights cases and traditional libel and slander rules to illustrate why hard-to-measure or intangible harms were not automatically excluded. Its reasoning therefore did not establish a categorical rule against all procedural or informational injuries. **341–342.**

## Application and limits

Congress had designed FCRA procedures to reduce dissemination of false information. Nevertheless, some violations could cause no harm or material risk of harm. A reporting agency might fail to give a required notice even though its information was completely accurate. An incorrect ZIP code, without more, was another example of an inaccuracy difficult to connect with concrete harm. The Court expressly reserved treatment of other kinds of false information rather than equating all inaccuracies with that example. **342 & n.8.**

The Ninth Circuit needed to ask whether the particular alleged violations entailed a sufficient degree of risk to meet the concreteness requirement. The Court took **no position on whether Robins ultimately had adequately alleged injury in fact**. His actual alleged inaccuracies and their potential employment effects were thus left for further consideration, not rejected as harmless by the Supreme Court. **342–343.**

The exact disposition was **vacatur of the Ninth Circuit's judgment and remand for proceedings consistent with the opinion**. The Supreme Court did not affirm the District Court's dismissal, decide the FCRA merits, certify a class, or award statutory damages. **343.**

## Separate opinions

Justice Thomas joined the Court but explained standing through the distinction between private and public rights. Historically, an invasion of a person's own property, contractual, or other private right could itself support suit without separately proved damages. A private litigant seeking to enforce a public right ordinarily needed special, individualized injury. His starred note preserved the historical qui tam exception. The public-rights limit helped prevent courts and private litigants from taking over political or executive enforcement functions; those concerns had less force in litigation between private parties over their own rights. **343–348 & starred note at 345.**

Applying that framework, Thomas distinguished generalized FCRA duties, such as providing a toll-free disclosure number, from the accuracy provision focused on information about a particular individual. On remand, the circuit could determine whether the latter created a private duty owed to Robins personally or merely authorized consumers generally to police procedures. Thomas did not conclusively decide which characterization controlled this claim. **348–349.**

Justice Ginsburg, joined by Justice Sotomayor, agreed with much of the Court's general discussion but saw no need for another remand. The complaint alleged misinformation about Robins himself and concrete consequences for his search for employment. Those inaccuracies were materially different from a wrong ZIP code. Congress had tied the procedural protections to the substantive goal of protecting consumers against inaccurate reporting. In her view, accepting the pleaded facts made the actual controversy and threatened harm sufficiently clear, so she would **affirm the Ninth Circuit**. Her opinion emphasized the pleading posture and did not find a proven violation or proven lost job. **349–354 & nn.1–3.**

## Assignment and historical significance

The assigned paragraph accurately identifies the importance of concrete injury alongside statutory rights, but its abbreviated description of bare procedural violations needs the Court's qualification: some procedural violations are themselves sufficient when tied to a concrete interest or the harm Congress identified. The source does not support a rule that only tangible harm counts or that every FCRA accuracy violation lacks standing. The complete opinions also expose the disagreement over applying the general principles to Robins' particular allegations.

This brief describes the 2016 judgment and reasoning. The assignment's later reference to *TransUnion* is context, not a claim that the complete later opinion was freshly reviewed for this brief or that Spokeo alone states every current limit on damages standing.
