# Committee on the Judiciary of the U.S. House of Representatives v. McGahn
951 F.3d 510 (D.C. Cir. 2020) (panel); 968 F.3d 755 (D.C. Cir. 2020) (en banc) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Facts, subpoena and district-court judgment

Donald McGahn, former White House Counsel, had witnessed events investigated by Special Counsel Robert Mueller, including President Trump’s efforts concerning Mueller’s investigation. The House Judiciary Committee sought information for investigating possible obstruction, considering impeachment, evaluating remedial legislation and conducting oversight. In March 2019 it requested documents; on April 22 it issued an authorized subpoena requiring document production and McGahn’s appearance for testimony on May 21. On May 20 the White House conveyed the President’s direction that McGahn not appear, relying on an Office of Legal Counsel opinion asserting absolute immunity of close presidential advisers from compelled congressional testimony. McGahn’s private attorney confirmed his refusal while offering to comply with an accommodation between the Committee and White House. The parties ultimately reached agreement on documents but not on his testimony. (February panel opinion 2–4; August en banc opinion 4–6.)

The Committee, authorized by the House to litigate, sued in August 2019 for declaratory and injunctive relief. On cross-motions for summary judgment the district court found Article III standing, statutory subject-matter jurisdiction under § 1331, and an implied constitutional cause of action. It rejected absolute testimonial immunity and directed McGahn to appear, while preserving his ability to invoke applicable privileges in response to particular questions. An order to appear therefore did not itself require disclosure of every requested fact or reject all executive, attorney-client or other privileges. McGahn appealed. The House’s subsequent impeachment vote and the Senate’s acquittal did not end the Committee’s stated legislative, oversight and potential further impeachment interests. (Panel 4–6; en banc 5–7.)

## The February 28, 2020 panel decision

Judge Griffith wrote the panel opinion, joined by Judge Henderson; Judge Rogers dissented. The panel held that the Committee’s direct interbranch information dispute did not present an Article III case or controversy. It **vacated the district court judgment and remanded with instructions to have the complaint dismissed**. It did not decide the merits of absolute testimonial immunity. This is the decision reported at **951 F.3d 510**, not an en banc judgment. (Panel 2, 35–37.)

The panel understood judicially cognizable injury through separation-of-powers principles and historical practice. It reasoned that federal courts traditionally resolve the rights of individuals, including constitutional disputes arising from an injury to an entity outside the federal government, rather than supervise the political branches’ relative powers. Merely describing a disagreement as a legal question did not establish a proper judicial controversy. The direct demand that a presidential adviser answer a congressional subpoena differed from a court’s own criminal subpoena, a contempt prosecution, a habeas application or a private party’s suit concerning congressional process. Those other settings directly implicated personal rights. (Panel 6–12, 20–24.)

Griffith emphasized the historical absence of direct congressional-executive litigation for nearly two centuries and the availability of political tools: accommodation, appropriations, appointments, public pressure, legislative bargaining and impeachment. Judicial resolution could displace negotiations with delay and repeated litigation over privileges, while threatening perceived judicial neutrality. The panel read Raines’s historical and structural analysis more broadly than its specific holding concerning individual legislators. It distinguished recent Circuit precedents involving statutory authorization, intervention or a court-managed accommodation, and treated § 1365’s Senate-only jurisdictional provision and governmental-privilege exception as further evidence against an implied judicial role here. (Panel 12–19, 24–35.)

The panel reserved whether Congress could authorize such suits by statute, whether a congressional chamber could civilly enforce subpoenas against private persons, and the courts’ established authority in criminal cases and cases implicating private rights. Its refusal to hear this suit thus did not decide every conceivable congressional subpoena case. (Panel 35–37.)

## The panel’s separate writings

Henderson expressly joined the holding and its rationale, but resisted McGahn’s broadest assertion that every congressional institutional injury was categorically barred. She recognized that this authorized institutional plaintiff differed from individual members in Raines, while concluding that historical practice and federal separation-of-powers concerns nonetheless required denying standing here. She also separately questioned the legal foundation of absolute testimonial immunity. Specific, qualified privileges appeared more defensible and better suited to accommodation than a categorical refusal to attend. She stressed that no particular privilege had formally been asserted and that the panel was not deciding the merits; her criticism was not a judicial holding compelling testimony. (Henderson concurrence 1–9, 10–20 & nn. 1–12.)

Rogers would affirm the district court. She considered all four Raines circumstances different: the Committee asserted a concrete injury to the House’s own subpoena power, had House authorization, could rely on a history of judicial subpoena enforcement, and lacked an adequate alternative mechanism. OLC itself had treated criminal prosecution or physical detention of presidential advisers as unavailable or impracticable while recognizing civil enforcement as an option. Courts could decide the narrow duty to appear without resolving all subsequent privilege claims. Removing that option, she thought, would encourage executive noncooperation rather than accommodation. She also found a constitutional equitable action, alternatively declaratory relief, and § 1331 jurisdiction; she rejected an inference that the Senate-specific statute implicitly repealed ordinary federal-question jurisdiction over House actions. These were the dissent’s positions, not rulings adopted by the February panel. (Rogers dissent 1–24, 25–31.)

## The August 7, 2020 en banc holding and judgment

The court granted rehearing and vacated the panel opinions. On rehearing, **seven judges held that the Committee had Article III standing**. Rogers wrote for the court; Henderson and Griffith separately dissented. Judges Katsas and Rao did not participate. The en banc court **affirmed the district court judgment in part on standing and remanded to the panel** to address McGahn’s remaining threshold objections and, if reached, the merits. It specifically left subject-matter jurisdiction, the existence of a cause of action and absolute testimonial immunity undecided. Establishing Article III standing did not finally enforce the subpoena or resolve the entire appeal. (En banc 3 & participation asterisk; 7, 37–38.)

The court applied the usual injury, traceability and redressability requirements with the especially rigorous scrutiny appropriate to an interbranch case. In assessing standing, it assumed the Committee’s legal entitlement on the merits rather than deciding every defense. Congress’s investigative power and each chamber’s subpoena power were established constitutional means of obtaining information needed for legislation, oversight and impeachment. A refusal to provide testimony sought through a concededly valid subpoena denied the Committee information it alleged it was legally entitled to receive. That was a real informational injury, supported by analogy to Akins and Public Citizen, rather than abstract dissatisfaction with executive conduct. (En banc 7–15.)

The injury was also particularized. The House had delegated investigative authority to the Committee and authorized this lawsuit. Unlike the individual plaintiffs in Raines or the single chamber seeking to vindicate whole-legislature authority in Bethune-Hill, the litigating body matched the body possessing the relevant power. The Senate did not need to approve the House’s subpoena or join its enforcement action. McGahn’s refusal directly caused the denial, and an order requiring appearance and testimony could remedy it. The House’s sole impeachment power reinforced the specificity of its interest, alongside its legislative and oversight functions. (En banc 15–18.)

## Why structural and historical objections did not defeat en banc standing

The en banc majority rejected an automatic structural bar against interbranch informational suits. Not every separation-of-powers objection concerns the standing doctrine’s function of confining courts to their proper role, and other doctrines, such as political-question limits, remained analytically distinct and outside this rehearing. The court expressed skepticism about treating every structural objection as a standing issue but did not need to settle that broader proposition: none of McGahn’s particular objections persuaded it. (En banc 19–23.)

Judicial enforcement could preserve the established accommodation process rather than replace it. Since the 1970s, negotiated resolutions had occurred against a recognized possibility of litigation. Eliminating that possibility would reduce the Executive’s incentive to negotiate and could turn oversight into voluntary presidential disclosure. Litigation’s slowness meant it was not Congress’s preferred tool; the case involved an impasse after repeated efforts at accommodation. The requested duty to appear was a familiar legal question, and a ruling requiring appearance could preserve valid privilege objections. The judiciary would not simply award Congress every piece of information it demanded. (En banc 23–28.)

The court also rejected the argument that civil enforcement usurped executive prosecution authority. Investigative and informational powers differed from the Executive’s general law-enforcement function, and OLC’s treatment of criminal contempt made the proposed exclusive prosecutorial remedy especially implausible here. Political controversy did not itself authorize judicial avoidance of an otherwise proper case. Raines concerned unauthorized individual legislators; its four considerations did not foreclose this authorized institutional plaintiff’s concrete information injury. Historical practice included both decades of adjudication and a longer tradition of presidential cooperation that often made litigation unnecessary. The majority treated history and structure as part of its analysis, not reasons to dispense with ordinary standing requirements. (En banc 28–38.)

## The en banc dissents

Henderson incorporated her panel concurrence and added that recent Supreme Court precedent, particularly Mazars, required more attention to historical cognizability and the judiciary’s limited role. She did not believe the availability of appropriations, impeachment and other political tools disappeared merely because prosecution or detention was unattractive. Permitting judges to mediate disputes between branches, she warned, would alter a constitutional practice that primarily relied on negotiation. (En banc Henderson dissent 1–6 & nn. 1–2.)

Griffith argued that the majority separated ordinary standing from the very structural concerns that should inform injury in fact. An alleged denial of a legal prerogative was no principled limit: similar reasoning could invite congressional suits over war powers, executive orders and spending, or presidential suits against Congress. He distinguished courts’ own subpoenas and cases affecting private rights from enforcement of an Article I subpoena against an Article II official. He also would decide that the Committee lacked a cause of action: the Senate-specific statutory mechanism, limitations involving governmental privilege, limits on implied constitutional actions and the procedural character of the Declaratory Judgment Act foreclosed the proposed routes. The en banc majority expressly did not decide those cause-of-action arguments. Griffith further predicted that prolonged litigation would impair Congress’s leverage and judicial neutrality without producing timely information. (En banc Griffith dissent 1–16, 16–22.)
