# Voorhees v. Guyan Machinery Co.
191 W. Va. 450, 446 S.E.2d 672 (1994) | Contracts

## Court, chronology, and verdict

The West Virginia Supreme Court of Appeals affirmed a tortious-interference judgment on March 24, 1994. Justice Neely wrote for the court; Justice Workman dissented from the punitive award and the breadth of the mitigation rule. The case concerns a former employer’s interference with a new employment contract, rather than an action claiming that the former employer wrongfully discharged the employee. (191 W. Va. 450, 452–453, 457–458.)

Albert Voorhees joined Guyan Machinery as an outside salesman in July 1981. In December 1985 he was required to sign a covenant prohibiting competition for twenty-four months after departure within a 250-mile radius of West Virginia. He resigned in July 1991 and began work for Polydeck. Guyan’s chairman, Robert Shell, notified Polydeck of the covenant and threatened litigation to enforce it. Polydeck told Voorhees to renegotiate the restriction or lose his job. Negotiations failed, and Polydeck fired him on August 14, 1991. (452 & n. 1.)

Voorhees sued Guyan and Shell in October for intentional interference with the Polydeck employment contract. Later that month, Guyan offered him a sales position beginning November 4. He rejected it as a sham intended to induce abandonment of the lawsuit; that characterization was his position, not an independently established contractual condition in the reproduced offer. The trial court rejected Guyan’s mitigation motion. In July 1992, special jury interrogatories established that the covenant was invalid, Guyan knew or should have known that, and Guyan intentionally interfered. The jury also found that Shell reasonably believed he was protecting a legitimate interest, leading to his dismissal from the suit. The verdict against Guyan awarded $75,000 compensatory damages, $75,000 punitive damages, and $7,065.76 prejudgment interest running from the Polydeck discharge. Guyan challenged liability, damages, and refusal of its reemployment offer as mitigation. (452–453.)

## Interference and the asserted business justification

The court applied Torbett’s four prima facie elements: a contractual or business relationship or expectancy, intentional interference by someone outside it, resulting harm, and damages. Justification or privilege is an affirmative defense; legitimate competition and other proper business interests can support it. Review of the verdict favored reasonable inferences for the prevailing employee, while judgment notwithstanding the verdict required a record permitting only one reasonable outcome without credibility weighing. (453–454.)

Guyan relied on the signed covenant, business competition, and protection of its interests. The evidence defeated those defenses. Polydeck manufactured urethane screens, which represented less than one-half of one percent of Guyan’s total sales. The majority considered that overlap insufficient to justify enforcement against this employee. Restrictive covenants required a protectible interest, such as investment in specialized training or confidential information, together with reasonable necessity and absence of undue hardship. Guyan had not supplied unique training; general sales or managerial abilities were not protectible merely because acquired at work. Its customers’ names were publicly available, and the record did not establish the asserted information as a protected interest. Note 2 distinguishes direct investment in specialized training. The verdict and refusal to set it aside therefore stood. (454–455 & n. 2.)

## Corporate liability, Shell’s dismissal, and punitive damages

Guyan argued that liability and punitive damages were inconsistent with the jury’s acceptance of Shell’s good faith. The majority reconciled the findings by distinguishing the lay chairman’s reasonable belief from the corporation’s responsibility to obtain competent legal advice before attempting enforcement. It inferred that the jury treated entrusting the matter to an uninformed layman as reckless disregard of the employee’s rights. This was the majority’s explanation of the verdict, rather than an additional special interrogatory actually quoted from the jury. The court deferred to the verdict even while acknowledging that it might have reached a different conclusion. (454–455.)

The majority also held that punitive damages could be considered for intentional interference. Under Thacker, malice could be inferred from the wrongful character of the act where the pleaded facts supported that inference. The deliberate threat to litigate an unenforceable covenant actually caused the loss of the new job. Guyan’s later offer did not negate the aggravation of that wrong. The court upheld the punitive award, rather than merely authorizing its reconsideration on remand. (455–456.)

## Mitigation and the rejected return to Guyan

Guyan requested an instruction declaring that refusal of its October offer barred damages after November 1. The majority rejected that automatic cutoff. The wrongful-discharge precedents on which Guyan relied did not establish a duty to accept reemployment from the interfering former employer in this different cause of action. Although the opinion broadly distinguished the wrongful-discharge mitigation principle, its reasoning did not excuse all efforts to seek other employment: note 3 records Voorhees’s contacts with at least twenty-two mining-industry employers, resumes, and $695.40 in search expenses included in damages. Guyan failed to prove inadequate mitigation. (456–457 & n. 3.)

Even assuming reemployment principles applied, an offer could reduce recovery only if it did not prejudice rights under the original contract. A substantially inferior position would not suffice; nor would an offer requiring renewed association that circumstances made offensive or degrading. Here Guyan first caused the loss of the Polydeck job and then offered a return after Voorhees sued. The majority regarded compelled renewed association in those circumstances as unreasonable, illustrating its conclusion with the hostility between Sulla and Marius. It affirmed the trial court’s refusal of the mitigation motions and proposed instruction. The assigned account quotes this alternative offensive-association rationale. (457; official syllabus point 4.)

## Workman’s dissent and disposition

Justice Workman rejected the punitive reasoning as inconsistent with corporate agency: Shell was the only actor identified, and his nonmalicious act was also the corporation’s act. She considered the majority’s inferred failure-to-consult-a-lawyer theory speculative and inconsistent with the required evidence of egregious conduct. She also criticized syllabus point 4’s reference to anything making renewed association offensive or degrading. She would limit the rule to relevant tortious conduct and require an objective reasonableness standard. Her dissent challenges both attribution of punitive culpability and an overbroad exception to mitigation; it does not propose reinstating the covenant as valid. (457–458.)

The court affirmed the Logan County circuit-court judgment, including the stated compensatory and punitive awards and interest. Workman’s separate dissent is preserved; no numerical vote beyond the identified majority and dissent is supplied by the source used here. (453, 457–458.)
