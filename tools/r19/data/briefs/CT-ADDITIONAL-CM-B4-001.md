# Gandell v. Pontigny
4 Camp. 375–376, 171 Eng. Rep. 119 (1816) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and dispute

Lord Ellenborough decided the legal objection at a nisi prius trial reported on February 19, 1816. A merchant’s discharged clerk sued in indebitatus assumpsit for work and labour, adding the common money counts. The issue was whether that form of action could recover the unpaid part of a quarter’s salary although the clerk had not actually worked throughout the quarter. This is a trial report ending in a jury verdict, rather than an appellate affirmance. (4 Camp. 375–376.)

## Facts and the parties’ positions

The clerk’s salary was £200 a year, payable quarterly. After a dispute on August 11 of the preceding year, the merchant discharged him and paid £25 for half the quarter, described in the report as July 1 through August 15. The following day the clerk offered to perform his duties, but the merchant declined. The report contains an apparent transposition of the parties in the sentence describing the objection to premature discharge; its surrounding narrative and Ellenborough’s ruling identify the discharged employee as the person willing to continue serving. The cause of the underlying dispute is not described. (375.)

Topping, for the merchant, argued that payment already exceeded the period of actual employment, so there was no unpaid work or labour to support these counts. He described the excess as two days, although the report’s stated dates do not independently resolve that calculation. Even if further compensation was due, he argued, the clerk needed a count specifically for salary or a special action on the contract for discharge before the quarter ended. He relied on Hulle v. Heightman, 2 East 145. The objection thus concerned the legal form of recovery as well as the absence of actual work during the remaining period. (376.)

## Rule and reasoning

Ellenborough held the action maintainable if the discharge lacked sufficient cause. A clerk who had served part of the quarter and remained willing to serve the rest could, in contemplation of law, be treated as having served the whole quarter. That constructive service made the employer indebted for work and labour in the unpaid amount. The employer’s refusal to accept the offered performance therefore did not defeat recovery on these counts. The ruling was expressly conditional on insufficient cause for discharge; it did not establish that every discharge creates a full-quarter entitlement. (376.)

The reasoning treated readiness to complete the agreed service as legal performance for purposes of the debt claim. It did not calculate a general wrongful-discharge award extending beyond this quarter, decide whether other work was available, or state a rule for deducting outside earnings. The assigned casebook presents the decision as an early refusal to apply mitigation and contrasts it with Howard v. Daly’s later rejection of constructive service. That historical contrast helps explain the decision’s place in the course, but the original report itself resolves the narrower pleading and salary question. (376; assigned C&M printed 1009–1010.)

## Exact result and source scope

The clerk obtained a verdict for £25, the remaining half-quarter’s salary. The report identifies Garrow and Dowling for the clerk and Topping and Lawes for the merchant. There is no separate judicial writing or panel vote. The short reporter’s concluding reference to Clark v. Mumford, 3 Camp. 37, was also read; it is an editorial cross-reference rather than another opinion in this case. (376.)
