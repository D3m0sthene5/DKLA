# United States v. Kimbell Foods, Inc.
440 U.S. 715 (1979), together with United States v. Crittenden | Civil Procedure

## Decision, two matters, and assigned account

Justice Marshall delivered the unanimous Court's April 2, 1979 opinion. It held that federal law governed the priority of these federal lending liens, but adopted nondiscriminatory state law as the federal rule. In No. 77-1359, Kimbell Foods, it affirmed the Fifth Circuit. In No. 77-1644, Crittenden, it vacated and remanded for unresolved Georgia-law questions. These are two consolidated matters with distinct mandates in one judicial opinion. The complete official report spans 715–740 and includes all forty-four judicial notes, the companion-case notation, counsel/amicus material, and no separate writing or appendix. The assignment is Civil Procedure PDF pages 283–284, printed pages 531–532, Note 3. [Complete official report](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-440/pdf/USREPORTS-440-715.pdf).

## Kimbell: an earlier inventory lien securing later advances

In 1968 grocery wholesaler Kimbell lent O.K. Super Markets $27,000. Two security agreements covered the supermarket's equipment and merchandise and contained clauses extending the collateral to future advances. Kimbell filed financing statements under Texas's Uniform Commercial Code. In February 1969, Republic National Bank lent the supermarket $300,000, secured by the same property. The Small Business Administration guaranteed 90% of that loan. 440 U.S. at 718–719.

The supermarket used Republic's proceeds to repay its initial Kimbell loan and an inventory debt, but continued buying inventory from Kimbell on credit. By January 1971 the balance was $18,258.57. After default on the Republic loan, Republic assigned its security interest to the SBA, and the United States paid $252,331.93 under the guarantee. The supermarket sold its equipment and inventory with creditor approval and escrowed the proceeds. Kimbell obtained a state judgment for the inventory debt, interest, and fees, then sought foreclosure against the escrow fund. Id. at 719–720.

The district court gave priority to the government by applying federal tax-lien doctrines: the first lien in time prevailed only if sufficiently definite, or “choate,” when the federal interest arose. A nonfederal lien's creditor, property, and amount had to be established. Because Kimbell's later advances had not been reduced to judgment when the federal lien arose, the court treated its interest as inchoate. It alternatively interpreted the original agreements as not securing those purchases. Id. at 720–722 & nn.6–8.

The Fifth Circuit reversed. It distinguished the government's voluntary lending from involuntary tax collection and rejected the special choateness requirement. It formulated a federal rule using UCC perfection, found the earlier agreements covered future advances under Texas law, and gave Kimbell priority. Although Texas law independently favored Kimbell, the appellate court had not simply adopted it as the governing federal rule. Id. at 722–723 & nn.9–10.

## Crittenden: a tractor repairman's later lien

Ralph Bridges obtained Farmers Home Administration loans from 1970 through 1972, secured by his crops and equipment. The agency filed a financing statement with Georgia officials. Bridges later accumulated repair debts to Crittenden, including $543.81 for a final December 1973 tractor repair. When the total $2,151.28 remained unpaid, Crittenden retained the tractor and acquired a Georgia repairman's lien. After Bridges's bankruptcy discharge, the United States sued for possession. Id. at 723–724 & nn.11–13.

The district court held that the federal financing statement inadequately described the collateral and alternatively that the repairman's lien took priority. The Fifth Circuit found the description sufficient under a federal rule drawn from the Model UCC. It also developed its own federal priority rule, favoring a repairman's lien when the repairman continuously possessed the property from the lien's creation. Under that rule, only the final $543.81 bill outranked the federal interest. It did not resolve the relevant Georgia priority rules. Id. at 724–725 & nn.14–16.

## First question: why the governing source was federal

The SBA and FHA exercised powers conferred by federal statutes in administering nationwide lending programs. Under *Clearfield Trust*, their resulting rights therefore derived from federal sources. Congress's failure to supply particular priority rules did not prevent federal courts from filling the statutory gaps. The governing source was federal law rather than state law operating independently of a federal choice. Id. at 726–727 & nn.17–20.

But that conclusion did not answer what the federal rule should say. Federal law could incorporate state commercial law rather than displace it with a uniform national priority system. Keeping those questions distinct was the opinion's central analytical step. Id. at 727–728.

## Second question: whether uniformity and federal objectives required a new rule

The Court considered three connected matters: the need for national uniformity, possible frustration of the programs' specific objectives, and disruption of commercial relationships built on state law. A genuinely uniform federal program might require a uniform rule, and state law threatening federal objectives could not simply be adopted. These lending programs, however, did not demonstrate such a need. Id. at 728–729 & nn.21–24.

The agencies already adapted loans to local commercial law. Their manuals, regulations, filing practices, and local offices incorporated state requirements, including substantive rules. They individually screened borrowers, examined proposed collateral, and negotiated loan terms. Standard forms did not compel a different result because they allowed property-specific descriptions and individualized terms. General assertions of administrative convenience could not overcome those actual practices. Id. at 729–733 & nn.25–32.

The government argued that protecting repayments served the same fiscal interest as collecting taxes. The Court rejected that equivalence. Tax collection was essential to governmental operation, and the government was an involuntary creditor of delinquent taxpayers. These agencies voluntarily made or guaranteed loans with detailed financial information, selected collateral, and set terms to protect repayment. Their programs primarily assisted businesses and farmers unable to obtain reasonable private financing. Congress had not required ordinary creditors to finance that public purpose by subordinating their existing interests to a new federal preference. Id. at 733–737 & nn.33–40.

Note 39 made the point concrete. The SBA could have investigated Kimbell's recorded agreements and negotiated subordination or termination of the future-advance coverage. The FHA likewise could have selected collateral less exposed to repair liens or demanded stronger security. The agencies' ability to negotiate protections undercut the claimed need for extraordinary judicial preferences. Id. at 736–737 n.39.

The 1966 federal tax-lien reforms further weakened the proposed analogy. Congress had protected various private liens even in that more important revenue context. The reforms did not directly control these commercial loans, but they showed why courts should hesitate to expand harsh federal preferences into a new setting without evidence that federal operations required them. Id. at 738 & n.41.

## Commercial reliance and the limits of adopting state law

Private lenders structured transactions around established state filing and priority systems. Unexpected federal preferences could defeat those expectations, especially where filings did not disclose the SBA's involvement. Developing bespoke federal rules case by case would also leave creditors uncertain about different types of liens. The Court adopted the existing body of state commercial law until Congress made a different accommodation. Id. at 739–740 & nn.42–44.

This was not unrestricted deference to every state rule. The holding concerned nondiscriminatory laws, and note 37 preserved federal courts' ability to exclude local laws that prejudiced federal interests. Note 34 also expressly withheld an opinion about federal consensual liens under statutes other than these lending programs. The result therefore rested on this identified federal interest and commercial setting. Id. at 735 n.34, 736 n.37, 740.

## Exact mandates and connection to the assignment

The Court affirmed Kimbell because the Fifth Circuit had already determined that Texas law gave its lien priority. Crittenden required further proceedings: the Court vacated the appellate judgment and remanded to determine the financing statement's sufficiency under Georgia law and whether, and to what extent, Georgia repair liens outranked the earlier federal security interest. The Court did not finally award Crittenden priority for every repair bill or simply affirm the circuit's $543.81 formula. Id. at 740.

The assigned account accurately captures the two-step distinction between the federal source of law and adoption of a state rule as federal law. The complete opinion supplies the two different transactions, the government's fiscal arguments, the agencies' existing practices, reliance concerns, preserved qualifications, and different mandates. Federal governance did not require a newly invented national rule on this record. Assigned printed 531–532; 440 U.S. at 726–740.
