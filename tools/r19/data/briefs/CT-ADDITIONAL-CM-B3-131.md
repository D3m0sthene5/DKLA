# Houston Oilers, Inc. v. Neely
361 F.2d 36 (10th Cir. 1966) | Contracts

## Decision and injunction ordered
Judge Pickett wrote for a Tenth Circuit panel of Pickett, Hill, and Seth on May 18, 1966; rehearing was denied June 23. The court held Ralph Neely’s professional-football agreements with Houston valid and enforceable, rejected the fraud, clean-hands, and statute-of-frauds grounds for denying relief, and reversed and remanded with instructions to grant an injunction. The relief sought restrained Neely from playing professional football for anyone other than Houston. It was a negative enforcement order, not a direction physically compelling him to play for Houston or an appellate award of damages. (36–38, 40, 43.)

## Competing teams and the four-year arrangement
Neely, a prominent University of Oklahoma senior, wanted a professional career and the financial benefits of competition between the AFL and NFL. On November 28, 1964, Houston acquired AFL draft rights to negotiate for him and Baltimore acquired NFL rights. Baltimore offered a signing bonus and escalating four-year salary. Neely, advised throughout negotiations by his father-in-law Robert Forte, also wanted an off-season business opportunity and preferred the southwest. Houston’s personnel director arranged negotiations with club president K. S. Adams, Jr. (38 & nn.1–2.)

Houston offered a $25,000 bonus, a four-year no-cut contract at $16,000 annually, guaranteed off-season real-estate employment paying at least $5,000 annually, and an arrangement to build and convey a Phillips 66 service station subject to specified encumbrances. On December 1, after further consultation, Neely accepted and executed the standard player agreements for 1965–1968 and the ancillary agreements. The parties stipulated that the instruments formed a single transaction. The player forms were initially undated, while the ancillary documents and bonus check bore December 1. (38–39 & n.3.)

## Secrecy, eligibility, and the Dallas contract
Neely insisted on payment during 1964 for tax reasons and confidentiality so that he could play in the January 2 Gator Bowl. The court concluded that everyone understood signing a professional agreement and accepting money destroyed collegiate eligibility. The secrecy arrangement would conceal existing ineligibility rather than legally preserve amateur status. The bonus check was made payable to Forte as trustee, and public announcement was deferred until after the bowl game. The written player agreements, however, declared themselves immediately binding upon execution and required filing with the league commissioner within ten days. Houston inserted December 1 and filed them; the forms also gave the commissioner a limited disapproval power. (39–40 & n.4.)

Dallas later acquired Baltimore’s draft rights, sought Neely’s services, and received Houston’s contracts for its lawyers to examine. Neely sent withdrawal letters dated December 29 and returned Houston’s bonus check; the letters arrived December 31. That day Dallas deposited $25,000 to his account. Neely disclosed his Houston dealings to his college coach and was declared ineligible on January 1. He signed with Dallas that evening and played there in 1965. The court found no evidentiary basis to attribute the publicity producing ineligibility to Houston. (39–40.)

## Fraud defense and immediate contractual effectiveness
Houston sued for a declaration of enforceability and an injunction. The trial court denied relief, finding fraud and a Texas statute-of-frauds problem. Neely’s fraud position rested on an asserted understanding that Houston’s agreements would not take effect or be filed until after the bowl, and that filing breached the secrecy promise. The appellate court rejected the trial court’s inference that Houston falsely represented secrecy would preserve eligibility. The express immediate-effectiveness clause, dated ancillary instruments, accepted bonus, and Neely’s understanding of the collegiate rules contradicted that account. Filing did not itself produce the publicity. (38–42 & nn.4,6.)

The court acknowledged and criticized ruthless professional-team recruitment practices; its seventh footnote reproduced another court’s condemnation of the competition. Nonetheless, it found insufficient proof of a material fraudulent representation by Houston in this transaction. It emphasized Neely’s competence, advice, negotiations with multiple bidders, and subsequent change of allegiance. Its eighth note recorded his accounting and finance education. These observations explain this evidentiary conclusion and do not establish a general rule that sophisticated athletes can never prove fraudulent inducement. (40–42 & nn.7–8.)

## Clean hands was not an automatic secrecy bar
The court recognized that equity ordinarily declines aid when the claimant’s conduct concerning the controversy is unlawful, unconscionable, or inequitable. But clean hands did not exclude every wrongdoer or mechanically resolve every case involving questionable conduct; its application allowed discretion to further justice. The court held Neely free legally to surrender amateur status at any time and Houston legally free to contract with him. Houston had no legal duty to announce the agreement publicly, and agreeing to keep confidential what it could lawfully keep confidential was not, in the court’s analysis, enough to bar enforcement here. (42.)

Neely relied on New York Football Giants v. Los Angeles Chargers, involving Charles Flowers. The court distinguished the circumstances in which that contract had been acquired and expressly disagreed with any interpretation creating a categorical clean-hands bar whenever a college player signed secretly before a postseason game. It did not repudiate the clean-hands doctrine altogether. Its reasoning retained criticism of recruiting conduct while rejecting the proposed legal consequence in Neely’s transaction. This is the contrast developed in the assigned account. (41–42.)

## Unique services and the service-station writing
Neely was over twenty-one, and the parties stipulated to his special, exceptional, and unique football knowledge, skill, and ability. The written exclusivity clause prohibited football services for another entity without the club’s and commissioner’s written consent. Under the controlling Texas law accepted by the parties, those facts supported an injunction against competing services once the contract was enforceable. (40 & n.5.)

The trial court had also treated the station agreement as too indefinite to satisfy Texas’s statute of frauds. The appellate court found sufficient written limits: specified Phillips station series, Phillips-product operation, construction and financing arranged through Ada Oil, and selection of site, type, and financing by that company within the stated terms. Those decisions did not require a further agreement. The writing therefore satisfied the statute, and the court ordered reversal and remand to grant the injunction. (42–43.)
