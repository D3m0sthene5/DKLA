# Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School District
246 Cal. App. 2d 123 (1966) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and holding

The California Court of Appeal, Second District, Division Two, decided this appeal on November 3, 1966. Justice Fleming wrote; Presiding Justice Roth and Justice Herndon concurred. The judgment dismissing Odorizzi's amended complaint after a demurrer sustained without leave to amend was reversed. Liberally construed, the pleaded circumstances supported a cause of action to rescind his resignation for undue influence. They did not adequately plead duress, menace, actual or constructive fraud, or mistake. The court made no merits finding that his will had actually been overborne and expressly reserved the propriety of his continuing to teach and the timeliness of rescission. (123, 126–130, 135.)

## Employment, arrest, and alleged pressure

Donald Odorizzi taught elementary school for the Bloomfield School District during 1964 and had a contract to continue the next year as a permanent employee. He was arrested on June 10 on charges described in the historical opinion as homosexual activity. He signed and delivered a written resignation on June 11, which the district accepted June 13. The criminal charges were dismissed in July under Penal Code section 995. In September he sought to return to his employment. The district refused, and he sued for declaratory and other relief. The reported dismissal of the charges is not described as a trial acquittal, and the appellate opinion does not adjudicate the underlying conduct. (126–127.)

His amended complaint alleged that arrest, questioning, booking, release on bail, and forty hours without sleep left him under severe mental and emotional strain, unable to think clearly or act rationally. While he was in that condition, the superintendent and school principal came to his apartment. They allegedly said that they wanted to help and had his interests at heart, that he should follow their advice and resign immediately, and that there was no time to consult counsel. If he refused, they said the district would suspend and dismiss him and publicize the proceedings and arrest, causing humiliation; if he resigned at once, publicity would be avoided and his prospects for teaching elsewhere would remain intact. He alleged that their pressure and his confidence in their representations displaced his own judgment. These were pleaded facts to be tested, not factual findings against the officials. (127.)

The legal inquiry concerned rescission under Civil Code section 1689 for consent that was not real or free under section 1567. A conclusion that a transaction was involuntary did not suffice by itself: the complaint had to supply facts supporting a recognized ground for relief. The court examined each alleged ground independently. (127–128.)

## Why the other pleaded grounds failed

The statutory duress and menace theories required unlawful conduct or threats. A threat to pursue legal action was not unlawful merely because it was unwelcome; the cited doctrine distinguished a claim known to be false. As the complaint stood, the officials announced suspension and dismissal proceedings that the applicable Education Code made their legal right and positive duty. Reputational damage incidental to good-faith performance of those duties did not supply the missing unlawfulness. The court thus rejected those theories on these allegations, rather than finding that every threat of publicity is lawful or that manner and timing of persuasion are irrelevant. Its later undue-influence analysis addressed those different features. (128.)

Actual fraud required misrepresentation, knowledge of falsity, intent to induce reliance, justifiable reliance, and damage. Although misrepresentation was charged, knowledge, inducement intent, and justifiable reliance were inadequately alleged. Constructive fraud required breach of duty arising from a confidential or fiduciary relationship. The complaint asserted such a relationship but supplied only the employer–employee connection. That status alone created no presumption of confidentiality; additional ties were needed, particularly while the parties were negotiating termination and expected to protect their respective interests. The rejection of constructive fraud did not defeat the separate undue-influence theory, which did not require a confidential relationship in these circumstances. (128–130.)

Mistake also lacked a factual basis. The participants knew the material circumstances and were principally evaluating probable consequences and predicting later events. Their inability to forecast exactly what happened—including the later disposition of charges—did not establish an actionable mistake about an existing fact or law. The complaint did not allege the sort of error about the transaction, parties, subject matter, or collateral occurrence contemplated by the doctrine. (129–130.)

## Undue susceptibility and excessive persuasion

Undue influence, in the sense applied here, involved excessive persuasion that displaced voluntary judgment by exploiting vulnerability. Civil Code section 1575 included unfair advantage of another's mental weakness or grossly oppressive advantage of necessities or distress. Misrepresentation was not indispensable, nor was a confidential or authoritative relationship required when the theory rested on unfair exploitation of weakness or distress. A party could therefore fail to plead fraud and still state an undue-influence claim. (130–131.)

The court described two interacting considerations: the target's susceptibility and the other person's excessive pressure. Susceptibility did not have to amount to total incapacity under section 38 or even the lesser incapacity covered by section 39. A still lesser weakness could support rescission under section 1575. Age, physical condition, emotional anguish, or temporary exhaustion could diminish the capacity to choose freely without creating a permanent disability. Odorizzi's alleged arrest-related turmoil and forty hours without sleep were sufficient, at the pleading stage, to put that condition in issue. The court recognized the possibility that exhaustion and distress could wholly incapacitate judgment without finding that this actually occurred. (131–132.)

Excessive pressure supplied the other side of the inquiry. The unfair mismatch could arise from ordinary force exerted on someone of diminished capacity or extraordinary force exerted on someone of normal capacity. The question was whether influence prevented the person from exercising independent judgment. Yet later dissatisfaction or regretting a bad bargain did not establish undue influence. Ordinary sales persuasion and disappointed expectations about land appreciation were given as examples of risks that contracting parties ordinarily must bear. (132.)

## The seven indicators and the actual pleading

The opinion identified a recurring pattern of overpersuasion rather than prescribing seven mandatory elements. Relevant features were an inappropriate time for the discussion, an unusual location for completion, insistence on immediate action, emphasis on dire consequences of delay, several persuaders confronting one person, lack of independent advisers, and assertions that consultation was impossible because time was too short. Several present together could support a characterization of excessive pressure; the court did not specify a numerical threshold that automatically invalidated consent. (133.)

Three cited episodes illustrated the pattern. Moore involved pressure by several relatives on a pregnant widow shortly after her husband's violent death and funeral; Weger concerned persuasion of an injured hospital patient confined in a cast, in pain and a highly nervous condition; Fyan concerned an unannounced 1 a.m. visit by a broker and others demanding execution of a commission agreement before morning, despite a request for more time. These were examples drawn from other decisions, not additional events in Odorizzi's own history. (133–134.)

The court contrasted the pleaded encounter with a hypothetical measured discussion. Had an official met Odorizzi during business hours after a short interval, explained the statutory proceedings and resignation option, permitted outside advice, and allowed overnight reflection, excessive-pressure allegations would have been much less plausible. That hypothetical did not occur as a judicially found fact. According to the complaint, officials instead pressed an exhausted person at his apartment, demanded immediate resignation, discouraged counsel, emphasized harmful publicity, and simultaneously offered assurances of help and future employment prospects. Those allegations combined subjective vulnerability with objective pressure and warranted factual inquiry. (134–135.)

## Disposition, assignment, and source scope

The ultimate distinction—ordinary influence of a free and competent judgment versus domination preventing independent choice—could not be resolved by analyzing the pleading alone. The court reversed the dismissal because the complaint stated a rescission cause of action. It did not order reinstatement, itself rescind the resignation, find the officials liable, or decide whether Odorizzi acted promptly enough under section 1691. Roth and Herndon concurred without separate writings. (135.)
