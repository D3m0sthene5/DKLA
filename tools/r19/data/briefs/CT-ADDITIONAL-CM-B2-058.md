# Johnson v. Davis
480 So.2d 625 (Fla. 1985) | Contracts

## Holding and voting alignment
The Florida Supreme Court approved the district court's decision requiring return of the home buyers' entire $31,000 deposit, with interest, costs, and fees. Affirmative misrepresentation supported recovery of the later $26,000 installment, while nondisclosure of known latent material roof problems supported recovery of the earlier $5,000. The court held that a home seller must disclose known facts materially affecting value when they are not readily observable and are unknown to the buyer, applying the duty to new and used property. Justice Adkins wrote the October 31, 1985 opinion, joined by Overton, McDonald, and Shaw. Ehrlich concurred in the result only, without a writing; Chief Justice Boyd dissented. The recorded alignment was five for the result and one opposed, with four joining the court's reasoning. (Official slip opinion 1, 3–7.)

## Contract, payment sequence, and roof dispute
In May 1982 the Davises agreed to buy the Johnsons' three-year-old home for $310,000. They paid $5,000 initially and were to pay another $26,000 within five days, with closing by June 21. The agreement allowed a licensed-roofer inspection at the buyers' expense and required the sellers to pay to correct leaks or replace damaged fascia or soffit. It also provided litigation costs and reasonable fees for the prevailing party. (Slip 1–2.)

Before paying the second installment, Mrs. Davis observed peeling and buckling plaster and ceiling stains. Mr. Johnson explained that a minor window problem had been repaired and attributed the stains to wallpaper glue and relocated beams. The parties disputed whether he also categorically denied roof or ceiling problems. The buyers paid the second installment; the sellers vacated. After heavy rain, water poured through the window frame, ceiling, fixtures, doors, and kitchen stove area. Roofers differed: two hired through the sellers' broker proposed repairs below $1,000, while three hired by the buyers said the roof was inherently defective, repairs would be temporary, and a new roof costing $15,000 was needed. The opinion does not describe a completed conveyance before the dispute. (Slip 2.)

## Trial, appeal, and rejected contract theory
The buyers sued for breach, fraud, and misrepresentation, seeking rescission and return of the deposit. The sellers counterclaimed to retain it as liquidated damages. Following trial, the judge entered judgment returning $26,000 plus interest to the buyers while awarding $5,000 plus interest to the sellers; each side bore its own fees. There were no specific factual findings. The sellers appealed and buyers cross-appealed. The Third District affirmed return of $26,000, reversed retention of $5,000, and directed return of the balance with costs and fees. The Supreme Court exercised conflict jurisdiction and approved that decision. (Slip 1–3.)

The court rejected rescission based merely on breach of the roof-inspection provision. The contract anticipated that repairs might be necessary, and the record did not show that the sellers refused them; the buyers had not demanded repair or replacement of the leakage areas before declaring breach. A non-watertight roof therefore did not itself establish breach of that provision. The court inferred, consistently with the intermediate court, that the trial court had rejected the buyers' contract theory because it otherwise could not have distinguished the two installments. Fraud remained a separate basis for relief. (Slip 3.)

## Affirmative misrepresentation and the later installment
The opinion stated the requirements of a false material factual representation, knowing falsity, intended inducement, and injury through reliance. It concluded from the trial record that after the initial agreement and $5,000 payment, the sellers affirmatively represented that the roof had no problems and then received $26,000. The majority treated this as knowing material falsity relied on to the buyers' detriment, while its initial factual account acknowledged disagreement over exactly what was said. (Slip 2–4.)

The fact that the parties had already signed did not insulate misrepresentations made before performance through conveyance. Caveat emptor did not shield actionable affirmative fraud. A recipient could rely on a representation despite the possibility of discovering falsity through investigation, unless the recipient knew it was false or falsity was obvious. These principles supported returning the second installment; they did not explain inducement of the earlier payment, made before the statements. (Slip 4–5.)

## Nondisclosure and the initial installment
To address the earlier $5,000, the court examined the distinction between action and inaction. A calculated failure to reveal material information could generate the same false belief and unfair advantage as an express lie. The majority rejected the older categorical protection of arm's-length nondisclosure where it permitted a knowledgeable home seller to exploit another's ignorance. Decisions from other jurisdictions involving latent flooding, foundation defects, termites, and infestation supported restricting caveat emptor. (Slip 5–7.)

The stated rule required the seller's knowledge, material effect on value, lack of ready observability, and the buyer's ignorance. The sellers' own testimony showed knowledge of roof problems before contracting and taking the initial $5,000. Their failure to disclose therefore warranted return of that payment plus interest. The court did not impose an unlimited warranty of all property conditions or expressly replace actual knowledge with constructive knowledge. Although its formulation said the duty applied to all forms of real property, new and used, the concrete dispute and rule began with a seller of a home. This brief records that wording without extending the decision through later authorities. (Slip 7.)

## Boyd's dissent
Boyd objected both to the evidence and the new rule. Without explicit trial findings, he considered the record insufficient to establish the roof's condition at the critical times, the seller's knowledge of a continuing defect, the exact statement, or whether promised repairs would make it watertight. Disclosure of an earlier repaired leak did not, in his view, prove knowledge of a present defect. He also disputed intended inducement and reliance because the buyers had already contracted to pay the second deposit. He would preserve the distinction between affirmative deceit and silence, rather than use uncertainty in the record to support fraud. (Slip 8–10.)

On policy, Boyd preferred legislative change and warned of expanding litigation and market burdens. He predicted that circumstantial proof of actual knowledge could evolve into constructive knowledge and ultimately implied warranties, shifting inspection risk to sellers. Those were the dissent's forecasts, not elements adopted by the majority. He emphasized buyers' and lenders' opportunity for inspection and the sellers' willingness to finance $200,000 secured by the same house as evidence against knowing exploitation. He would quash the appellate decision, dismiss rescission for fraud, and remand for findings on compliance with the repair obligation. If the sellers were willing and able to perform that obligation, he believed they should retain all $31,000. (Slip 10–12.)

## Exact disposition and assigned reading
The Supreme Court approved the Third District decision, preserving its directions to return the full deposit and award costs and fees. The $15,000 replacement-roof estimate was evidence, not an additional damages award. The court's rejection of contractual breach and approval of fraud relief must both be preserved. (Slip 2–3, 7.)
