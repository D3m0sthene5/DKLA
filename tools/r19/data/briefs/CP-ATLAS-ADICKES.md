# Adickes v. S. H. Kress & Co.
398 U.S. 144 (1970) | Civil Procedure

## Court, two claims, and judgment
Justice Harlan delivered the Supreme Court's opinion on June 1, 1970. The Court reversed the Second Circuit and remanded for further proceedings on both of Sandra Adickes's civil-rights claims: a conspiracy claim eliminated by pretrial summary judgment and a discrimination claim eliminated by a directed verdict after her trial presentation. Reversal gave her renewed opportunities to prove liability; it did not establish a conspiracy, award damages, or resolve every possible defense. (398 U.S. at 146–149, 173–174 & n.44.)

The seven participating Justices supported reversal but differed about the substantive theory. Harlan's Court opinion represents the position of Harlan, Chief Justice Burger, Stewart, and White. Black concurred in the judgment; Douglas dissented in part; Brennan concurred in part and dissented in part, expressly joining the conspiracy discussion subject to a statutory-language qualification. Marshall took no part. Blackmun had not yet begun service: the Court's official membership table gives June 9, 1970, as his starting date. Thus the result is seven votes for reversal, rather than unanimous agreement with every part of Harlan's reasoning. (Id. at 174–178, 188–190 & Brennan n.1; [Supreme Court membership table](https://www.supremecourt.gov/about/members_text.aspx?stream=top).)

## Incident and procedural separation
Adickes was a white New York schoolteacher volunteering at a Freedom School in Hattiesburg, Mississippi, during summer 1964. On August 14 she accompanied six Black students to the public library. Her complaint alleged that the students were denied access, the librarian called police, and the police chief closed the library and ordered the group out. They then went to Kress's restaurant facilities. The students were offered service, but Adickes was refused because she was white and accompanied by Black people. After they left, police arrested her for vagrancy. The complaint alleged that an officer entered the store while the group awaited service and later made the arrest. The officer's presence, communications, and any agreement remained disputed matters for proof. (Id. at 146–150.)

She sued Kress in the Southern District of New York under §1983, asserting racial discrimination violating equal protection. One count attributed the refusal to a community custom of racial segregation; the other alleged a conspiracy between Kress employees and police to deny service and arrange a groundless arrest. On the first count, the district court required proof of a state-enforced custom specifically denying service to white persons accompanied by Black persons. After her evidence, it directed a verdict because she had not demonstrated other sufficiently similar Hattiesburg instances. The Second Circuit affirmed and also required a custom both local and statewide. (Id. at 147–148.)

The conspiracy count had already failed on summary judgment. The district court thought the submitted materials did not permit a reasonable inference of conspiracy, and the Second Circuit affirmed that ruling unanimously. The Supreme Court addressed the two procedural rulings separately. It also declined to consider an attempted additional claim under the 1875 Civil Rights Act that had not been presented to the court of appeals. (Id. at 147 n.2, 148–149.)

## Why an agreement with police could create §1983 liability
Section 1983 required deprivation of a federally protected right and conduct under color of a state statute, ordinance, regulation, custom, or usage. Equal protection forbids state racial discrimination, including discrimination based on the race of one's companions. A private employee's agreement with a police officer to deny service or cause a racially motivated arrest could establish state action and joint participation under color of law. The police conduct need not have been lawful or officially authorized. Nor does a private participant escape liability merely because only the other participant holds public office. (Id. at 150–152 & nn.4–7.)

The claim rested on constitutional equal protection, rather than a damages remedy directly under the 1964 Public Accommodations Title. Harlan's lengthy fifth note distinguished that title's limited enforcement scheme from an independently available constitutional §1983 claim. Conduct violating both provisions does not lose its constitutional remedy merely because the accommodations statute does not itself provide damages. (Id. at 150–151 n.5.)

## Kress's evidentiary showing left an important gap
Kress relied on manager Powell's deposition. He denied communicating with police and said he had signaled the counter supervisor not to serve Adickes because he feared violence from customers reacting to an integrated group. That signal had been arranged beforehand with the supervisor. The police chief and two arresting officers submitted affidavits denying a request or arrangement to arrest her. Kress also relied on Adickes's admission that she lacked direct knowledge of communications and depended on circumstantial evidence. (Id. at 153–156 & nn.8–12.)

Adickes pointed to the complaint, her deposition account that a student had seen an officer enter, and an employee's unsworn statement placing Officer Hillman in the store and describing his departure shortly before the arrest. She also supplied an affidavit disputing Powell's description of an explosive situation. Her deposition account was hearsay, and the employee's statement was unsworn. The Court did not silently transform those materials into admissible affirmative proof. It held instead that Kress's own motion had not established the absence of a genuine material issue. Footnote 13 separately describes the students' subsequent trial testimony; that testimony should not be treated as an affidavit already filed in opposition to the earlier motion. (Id. at 156–159 & nn.13–19.)

Powell's denial did not exclude contact between police and the counter supervisor or waitress. No qualifying affidavits from those employees eliminated that possibility. The officers' affidavits likewise failed to rule out their presence while service was being considered or communications conveying disapproval of the integrated group. If an officer was present, the sequence of refusal and arrest could permit a jury to infer an understanding with an employee. Viewed favorably to Adickes, these unexplained gaps left a triable question. The Court did not hold that presence necessarily proved an agreement; it held that the offered showing failed to foreclose a reasonable conspiracy inference on this record. (Id. at 157–159 & nn.16–17.)

## The opponent's response obligation follows a sufficient motion
Kress invoked the 1963 amendment to former Rule 56(e), requiring an opponent of a properly supported motion to identify specific facts rather than rely on pleading allegations. Harlan agreed with the response requirement but rejected using it to excuse an inadequate initial motion. The amendment did not change the movant's obligation under Rule 56(c). The Advisory Committee explanation expressly contemplated denial when the movant's material failed to establish the absence of a genuine issue, even if the opponent presented no qualifying evidentiary response. (Id. at 159–160 & nn.18–22.)

Had officers supplied affidavits denying their presence, Adickes would have needed more than the contrary complaint allegation: a competent eyewitness affidavit or a former Rule 56(f) affidavit explaining why the needed evidence could not then be supplied. Submitting such material would have been preferable even here. The holding is therefore neither an endorsement of deficient litigation preparation nor a rule that a pleading always defeats summary judgment. It turns on the gaps in this particular moving party's showing. The subsection labels are those used in the 1970 opinion. (Id. at 160–161.)

## The discrimination count: custom must involve the State
The separate substantive count asked what makes conduct under color of a custom or usage actionable. Harlan read the statutory phrase to require a custom having the force of law through persistent practices of state officials, rather than merely widespread private prejudice. He drew support from the statute's origins in the 1866 and 1871 civil-rights legislation, proponents' and opponents' descriptions of action under color of state authority, and precedent distinguishing governmental involvement from ordinary private habits. The lengthy legislative-history discussion and notes support the interpretation; they do not establish that every discriminatory social practice is automatically state action. (Id. at 161–167 & nn.24–38.)

Official practices can have legal force without appearing in a statute book. Persistent imposition of sanctions, withholding of benefits, systematic maladministration, or refusal to enforce otherwise equal laws can turn a practice into effective state policy. Harlan distinguished that kind of official nonenforcement from a legislature's mere failure to enact corrective legislation. (Id. at 167–169 & n.39.)

Equal protection separately required sufficient state involvement in this private refusal. The Court held that a restaurant's discrimination under a state-enforced custom compelling segregation would meet that requirement. Compulsion by an official custom could be as consequential as compulsion by an express segregation statute. The Court did not decide whether less substantial involvement would also suffice. It reserved the separate question whether knowing discrimination pursuant to Mississippi's facially permissive trespass statute would satisfy the constitutional requirement on a properly developed factual record. (Id. at 161–162 n.23, 169–171.)

## Three corrections governing the new trial
First, custom need not be enforced only through a trespass statute. A false vagrancy arrest to punish interracial dining, or police tolerance of violence against those challenging segregation, could provide other proof of official enforcement. The discussion identifies possible showings on remand, rather than finding every one established in this damages action. (Id. at 171–172 & nn.41–43.)

Second, Adickes did not need to prove a longstanding practice precisely matching white patrons accompanied by Black patrons. The relevant broader practice was state-enforced segregation in public eating places. Before the 1964 Act, Black patrons generally lacked the opportunity to be served in the formerly white restaurants at all; requiring numerous earlier instances of the newly possible configuration was artificially narrow. (Id. at 173.)

Third, a custom operating in Hattiesburg could be a custom of the State for these purposes without operating throughout Mississippi. A municipal ordinance can violate equal protection, and an effective municipal custom can do so as well. Adickes had to prove the local state-enforced custom and that it motivated Kress's refusal. The Court expressly left remedies and possible defenses for further development below. (Id. at 173–174 & n.44.)

## Black, Douglas, and Brennan
Black concurred in reversal on both counts without deciding the proper statutory construction. He emphasized live testimony, confrontation, cross-examination, and the jury's role. On the custom count, he thought the evidence of discriminatory incidents sufficient to reach a jury even assuming the trial judge's narrow interpretation. His rationale therefore did not join Harlan's entire statutory holding. (Id. at 175–178.)

Douglas opposed making official enforcement indispensable to statutory custom. He described Mississippi's broad network of segregation measures and argued that socially entrenched communal norms can bind behavior as powerfully as written law. The 1866 statute's Thirteenth Amendment origins and the phrase protecting constitutional and statutory rights, he reasoned, undermined treating every aspect of §1983 as limited to official complicity. He agreed that the narrow definition of the relevant practice was wrong and that the evidence warranted jury consideration, while dissenting from the Court's restrictive construction. (Id. at 178–188 & nn.1–7.)

Brennan expressly separated two inquiries: whether the asserted underlying right requires state action, and whether the statutory color-of-authority element requires the same thing. He accepted state action as necessary for the equal-protection right alleged here and joined the conspiracy analysis with a qualification about the statutory wording. For the other count, he considered Mississippi's statutes, read against their segregationist context, sufficient governmental encouragement to implicate equal protection without requiring actual coercion or influence in each private decision. A private person's conscious reliance on supportive statutory authority would separately satisfy the statutory requirement. (Id. at 188–203, 210–212 & nn.1–14, 21.)

Brennan also interpreted statutory custom to include enduring community norms supported by unofficial sentiment or sanctions. He argued from the remedial design of the Reconstruction statutes and their concern with organized and customary private interference, while excluding isolated private prejudice. He disputed Harlan's treatment of ambiguous legislative remarks as definitive. That broader reading did not eliminate the separate constitutional state-action inquiry for this equal-protection claim. (Id. at 203–210, 212–231 & nn.15–33.)

Finally, Brennan proposed federal remedial standards, compensation for proved loss from deliberate racial discrimination, and punitive damages for knowing or reckless violation; commercial fear of losing customers would not excuse the wrong. These were views in his separate writing, not damages rules adopted or applied by the Court. Harlan expressly reserved relief and defenses. (Id. at 231–234 & n.34; Court, 174 n.44.)
