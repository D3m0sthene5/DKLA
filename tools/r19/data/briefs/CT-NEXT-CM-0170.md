# Transatlantic Financing Corp. v. United States
363 F.2d 312 (D.C. Cir. May 27, 1966) | Contracts

## Voyage and the claim for extra payment
Transatlantic operated the SS Christos under an October 2, 1956 voyage charter to transport a full cargo of wheat from a United States Gulf port to a safe Iranian port. The charter identified endpoints but no route. Egypt had nationalized the Suez Canal Company in July; international tension remained when the bargain was made. The ship sailed October 27 from Galveston for Bandar Shapur on the usual route through Gibraltar and Suez. Israel invaded Egypt October 29, British and French forces entered the Canal Zone October 31, and Egypt obstructed and closed the canal November 2. (363 F.2d 314–315.)

Around November 7, Transatlantic’s representative sought instructions and additional compensation from an Agriculture Department employee. Transatlantic conceded that employee lacked authority to bind the government. He said performance was expected according to the charter, disputed entitlement to added payment, and said a claim could be filed. The ship went around the Cape of Good Hope and delivered the wheat December 30. The alleged extra expense was $43,972 beyond the $305,842.92 contract price, with roughly 3,000 miles added to an expected 10,000-mile voyage. (Id. 315,319.)

The District Court dismissed Transatlantic’s admiralty libel seeking the diversion costs. On appeal, Transatlantic argued that admiralty’s usual-route and deviation principles implied a charter specifically requiring Suez; closure made that contract impossible; and performing the Cape voyage in response to the government’s demand conferred a benefit recoverable in quantum meruit. The case concerned added payment after completed performance, rather than an unperformed contract or an authorized government modification.

## Test and holding
Judge Wright stated three requirements for an impossibility or commercial-impracticability excuse: an unexpected contingency; risk of that occurrence not allocated by agreement or custom; and performance made commercially impracticable by the contingency. Physical possibility does not itself defeat the doctrine, and footnote 1 says a party need not refuse performance before invoking impracticability: such a requirement would encourage disruption of expectations. But disruption of an expected method raises the inquiry rather than resolving it. (Id. 315–316 & n. 1.)

The court accepted that Suez closure defeated the expected method, declined to deem the specific risk allocated solely from foreseeability, and nevertheless held the Cape voyage commercially practicable. Transatlantic was not entitled to recover the additional expense. The court also rejected its proposed remedy even assuming an excuse, because it attempted to preserve its contract profit while shifting all added expense to the government. (Id. 318–320.)

## Expectation of a route versus a condition of performance
The usual and customary route supplied a reasonable expectation. It did not establish that continued availability of that route was a condition without which the charter disappeared. Neither express terms, trade usage, surrounding circumstances, nor the admiralty doctrine supplied that further allocation. The cases treating the Cape as an alternative reinforced the distinction. The full notes explain that even identifying the customary route at formation or performance does not automatically answer impracticability: an alternative may be suitable for one cargo or point in the voyage and unsuitable for another. (Id. 316–318 & nn. 4–10.)

The vessel-fitness and deviation clauses showed anticipated use of the customary route, but did not condition performance on Suez remaining open. Clauses compensating emergency destination changes, and carefully specifying demurrage and despatch for loading and unloading, likewise did not allocate the risk of the voyage becoming longer. The deviation doctrine primarily adjusts insurance consequences of unreasonable departures; its qualified operation did not make every route substitution a discharge of the transportation bargain. (Id. 317–318 & nn. 8–10.)

## Foreseeability, risk, and practicability
The October crisis made abnormal risks apparent. The court considered an inference that freight negotiations had incorporated them, but expressly refused to equate knowledge of a possibility with agreement to assume that specific contingency. Parties may recognize risks they cannot settle or do not expressly address. Nationalization and an implied threat of force did not necessarily predict obstruction with sunken ships. Even so, entering the bargain against that background showed willingness to accept some abnormal risk and justified a stricter assessment of claimed impracticability than would a wholly unforeseen situation. (Id. 318–319 & nn. 11–12.)

The alternative journey did not endanger the wheat, and vessel and crew were fit for it. The carrier was at least as able as the government to insure against the event and better situated to assess alternate-route costs and maritime war hazards. Against those facts, added expense alone did not rise to commercial impracticability. The court did not declare all cost increases insufficient: it required a greater disparity than this record showed, considering the venture’s background. Footnote 13 frames practicability objectively, while acknowledging known limitations shared by both parties may matter to a complete assessment. Footnote 15 likewise preserves the possibility that an unforeseen cost increase could alter performance’s essential nature. (Id. 319 & nn. 13,15.)

UCC§§ 2-614 and 2-615 supplied comparisons and policy guidance; this voyage charter was not a sale of goods governed directly by Article 2. The notes discuss commercially reasonable substitute delivery and the relation of sale contracts to charters. Those comparisons should not be restated as a holding applying an Article 2 seller’s statutory defense to this carrier.

## Independent remedial objection
Transatlantic had already collected the charter price. Its theory treated the contract as a nullity yet used it to retain compensation and profit while claiming quantum meruit only for the extra leg. If discharge justified compensation for valuable performance, the relevant restitutionary claim would concern the entire performance and its value, not an automatic add-on preserving one party’s bargain while placing the entire loss on the other. The court distinguished that approach from an equitable accommodation of loss and observed that the advantageous contract price apparently discouraged Transatlantic from taking a remedial position consistent with its liability theory. (Id. 319–320 & n. 17.)

## Disposition and assigned material
The D.C.Circuit affirmed dismissal. J.Skelly Wright wrote for the panel of Danaher, Wright, and McGowan; the report has no separate writing or stated division, and contains 17 judicial footnotes, all included in the full-source reading. The affirmance denies this additional-compensation claim; it does not hold that performance by any conceivable alternate route is always required.
