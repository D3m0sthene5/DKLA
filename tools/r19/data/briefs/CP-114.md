Parties
Thaddeus Donald Edmonson, a construction worker, was the negligence plaintiff, appellant, and Supreme
Court petitioner. Leesville Concrete Company, Inc., was the private corporate defendant and respondent.
Edmonson sought compensation for a job-site injury and challenged Leesville’s exclusion of two prospective
jurors through peremptory challenges. The constitutional rights directly implicated by the exclusions belonged
to the prospective jurors, although Edmonson asserted those rights in his own litigation. This relationship
between the litigant and the excluded nonparties made third-party standing a separate question from whether
Leesville’s conduct was attributable to government. 500 U.S. at 616–618, 628–630.
Material Facts
Edmonson was injured at Fort Polk, Louisiana, a federal enclave. He alleged that a Leesville employee allowed
a truck to roll backward and pin him against construction equipment. He sued in the Western District of
Louisiana and invoked his Seventh Amendment right to a jury trial. The underlying case concerned a private
company’s alleged negligence; the government was not a litigating party. The relevant governmental
involvement arose in the selection and operation of the jury. Id. at 616.
During voir dire, Leesville used two of its three statutorily authorized peremptory challenges to exclude Black
prospective jurors. A peremptory challenge permits a party to remove an otherwise qualified prospective juror
without establishing a particular ground for disqualification. Edmonson, who was Black, asked the judge to
require a race-neutral explanation under Batson v. Kentucky. The judge declined because he understood Batson
to apply only in criminal proceedings. The jury ultimately contained eleven white jurors and one Black juror.
Id. at 616–617, 620–621.
The jury found for Edmonson, assessed his total damages at $90,000, and attributed eighty percent of the fault
to him, producing an $18,000 recovery. Thus, the challenged proceeding did not end in a complete defense
verdict. Edmonson’s objection concerned the fairness and constitutional integrity of the process producing the
comparatively limited award. The Supreme Court did not infer from the racial composition of the resulting jury
alone that Leesville had discriminated. The threshold evidentiary question remained for the lower courts. Id. at
617, 631.
Procedural History
A divided Fifth Circuit panel reversed, reasoning that private litigants become government actors when
exercising peremptory challenges and that Batson’s prohibition therefore extends to civil trials. The panel
would have remanded for a determination whether Edmonson had established a prima facie case of
discrimination. The court then reheard the case en banc, displaced the panel decision, and affirmed the district
court. The en banc majority concluded that private civil litigants’ strikes did not constitute state action and
therefore were not subject to the constitutional restriction. Id. at 617–618.

The Supreme Court granted certiorari to resolve the disagreement among the circuits. It reviewed two legal
prerequisites to considering the objection: whether the private company’s use of peremptories implicated
governmental action, and whether Edmonson could assert the excluded jurors’ rights. It resolved both in
Edmonson’s favor and remanded for the Batson inquiry that the trial court had refused to undertake. Id. at 618,
628–631.
Issue
Does a private party’s racially discriminatory use of peremptory challenges in a federal civil trial constitute
governmental action subject to the equal-protection component of the Fifth Amendment’s Due Process Clause?
If it does, may the opposing litigant assert the excluded prospective jurors’ rights, and had the record already
established the discrimination necessary for relief? Id. at 616, 619–620, 628–631.
Rule and Holding
A private litigant in a civil case may not exclude prospective jurors on account of race through peremptory
challenges. Under the state-action framework applied by the Court, the asserted deprivation must arise from a
right or privilege supplied by governmental authority, and the private participant must fairly be treated as a
governmental actor for the challenged activity. Federal statutory authorization, indispensable judicial
participation, and the delegation of a governmental jury-selection function satisfied that framework here. The
holding concerns the particular act of exercising a peremptory challenge; it does not transform every litigation
choice by a private attorney into government action. Id. at 619–628.
The opposing civil litigant may assert the excluded jurors’ equal-protection rights because discriminatory
selection causes a concrete injury to the litigant, the litigant has a sufficiently close relationship with
prospective jurors, and practical obstacles hinder the jurors’ ability to vindicate their own rights. The Court
held that these requirements were satisfied. It did not decide whether Leesville’s two strikes in fact established
a prima facie case of discrimination; that determination was left for further proceedings. Id. at 628–631.
Judgment
The Supreme Court reversed the en banc Fifth Circuit’s judgment and remanded for proceedings consistent
with its opinion. The lower courts were to determine whether the circumstances established a prima facie case
requiring Leesville to provide race-neutral explanations. The Court did not itself find purposeful
discrimination, order an unconditional new trial, reallocate fault, or increase Edmonson’s damages. Its
judgment removed the categorical civil-case exemption that had prevented consideration of his objection. Id. at
631.
Reasoning
The Court began by identifying the constitutional claimant and the applicable constitutional provision.
Excluding someone from jury service because of race injures that person’s equal-protection rights. That injury
is equally real in civil and criminal proceedings. Because this case originated in federal court, the Court applied
the equal-protection component of the Fifth Amendment rather than directly applying the Fourteenth
Amendment’s restriction on states. The ordinary state-action vocabulary nevertheless supplied the analytical
framework for determining whether private conduct could be attributed to government. Id. at 616, 618–620.
Governmental attribution remained necessary even though racial exclusion was objectionable. The majority
expressly recognized that constitutional limits ordinarily do not govern private conduct. The restriction
preserves private freedom and prevents government from being held responsible for actions that cannot fairly
be attributed to it. Consequently, the conclusion that a prosecutor could not make the same strike did not by
itself answer the question about Leesville. The Court applied the two-part framework drawn from Lugar v.
Edmondson Oil Co. to this particular use of governmental authority. Id. at 619–620.

The first part was straightforward. Peremptory challenges have no operation outside a court’s jury-selection
system and are available only because law authorizes them. Section 1870 supplied Leesville with three
challenges in this federal civil action. Although peremptories have a long history, the Court explained that the
Constitution does not require government to provide them. The claimed exclusion therefore depended on a
privilege conferred by governmental authority. Id. at 620–621.
For the second part, the Court examined governmental assistance, the nature of the function performed, and the
way governmental authority intensified the alleged injury. The government located and summoned prospective
jurors, established qualifications and selection procedures, required attendance, and regulated voir dire. The
judge controlled questioning and challenges for cause, allocated challenges among multiple parties, and
discharged a juror when a peremptory strike was exercised. Those acts made the private choice legally
effective. In the majority’s view, the court’s direct participation was sufficiently substantial to distinguish this
process from a private party’s mere use of a generally available legal remedy. Id. at 621–624.
Jury selection also constituted the selection of a governmental decision-making body. A civil jury determines
facts, assesses credibility, and returns a verdict that can become an enforceable judgment and produce
preclusive consequences. Government could not avoid constitutional restrictions on the composition of that
body by delegating part of the selection process to private litigants. A party’s private motive for a strike did not
alter the public character of the authority being exercised. Id. at 624–627.
The majority addressed Polk County v. Dodson, which had treated a public defender as a private actor while
representing a criminal defendant. Functional analysis supported that conclusion because the defender’s
professional role was independent of, and adversarial to, the government. Here, the government and litigants
participated in the shared institutional task of selecting the jury. The Court confined its attribution to that
delegated task. It expressly acknowledged that deciding whether to sue, selecting counsel, and making many
discovery or trial choices could retain their private character. Id. at 626–628.
The governmental setting compounded the exclusion’s injury. Prospective jurors were summoned under public
authority and then exposed to discrimination as a condition of participating in the judicial system. Permitting
racial exclusion in a courtroom undermined both the dignity of the excluded persons and confidence in the
resulting adjudication. This institutional harm supported the attribution analysis together with the government’s
assistance and the public function; the opinion did not rest solely on the physical location of the conduct. Id. at
628.
Third-party standing required its own justification. Prospective jurors faced substantial practical obstacles to
bringing separate lawsuits over exclusion. Voir dire established a relationship between litigants and prospective
jurors, and the exclusion severed that relationship through the challenged classification. The litigant also
sustained a cognizable injury because discriminatory selection cast doubt on the fairness and integrity of the
proceeding. The majority did not justify standing on an assumption that a juror of a particular race would favor
a corresponding litigant. Its analysis concerned neutral selection and a legitimate adjudicative process. Id. at
628–630.
Finally, extending the constitutional rule to civil cases did not establish that every challenged strike was
unlawful. The trial court still had to consider all relevant circumstances, including any pattern of strikes, in
determining whether an initial showing warranted race-neutral explanations. This separation between legal
applicability and factual proof explains the remand. The Court left trial courts to develop evidentiary
procedures in the first instance rather than announcing a finding against Leesville on the existing record. Id. at
630–631.

Separate Opinions
Justice O’Connor, joined by Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justice Scalia, disputed the state-action conclusion.
She treated peremptory challenges as a deliberately preserved sphere of private, adversarial choice within a
government-managed trial. The public activities on which the majority relied—summoning jurors, setting
qualifications, and supervising questioning—would occur whether or not private peremptories existed. In her
view, those activities created the setting for the strike without making government responsible for the
attorney’s reason for exercising it. The judge’s act of excusing the juror amounted to acquiescence rather than
coercion or significant encouragement. Id. at 631–638.
O’Connor also argued that the public-function analysis must concern a function traditionally exclusive to
government. The longstanding private role in peremptory challenges defeated that characterization in her
account. She regarded Dodson’s treatment of adversarial representation as controlling and rejected the
majority’s claim that opposing counsel and the government worked toward the same end. Her dissent
condemned racial discrimination while maintaining that constitutional attribution required more than its
occurrence in a courtroom. Id. at 638–644.
Scalia joined O’Connor and separately emphasized practical consequences. He anticipated additional litigation
over jury selection, burdens on trial courts, and constraints on minority litigants’ own use of race-conscious
strikes to obtain a desired jury composition. He also anticipated implications for criminal defendants’ strikes.
These were his objections and predictions, not additional holdings of the Court. The majority’s actual judgment
concerned a private defendant’s challenges in this civil action. Id. at 644–645.
Notes
The assigned excerpt emphasizes the state-action dispute and includes portions of O’Connor’s dissent. The full
opinion supplies the earlier panel proceedings, the detailed Lugar framework, the omitted third-party-standing
analysis, and Scalia’s separate dissent. Those additions explain why recognizing a constitutional limit did not
itself establish that Leesville violated it. Casebook pp. 1100–1103; 500 U.S. at 617–631, 644–645.
For a problem involving jury selection, identify the challenged actor and conduct, the right asserted, the person
entitled to assert it, and the stage of the discrimination inquiry. Edmonson supplies the civil-case and
government-attribution answers for peremptory challenges. It does not make a litigant entitled to jurors of a
particular race or convert the jury’s racial composition into conclusive proof of unlawful selection. The
comparison with the dissent turns on whether government is merely providing a forum for private choices or
delegating and effectuating part of the selection of its own adjudicative body. Id. at 619–631, 633–643.
