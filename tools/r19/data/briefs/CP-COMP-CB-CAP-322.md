# Warner v. Pacific Telephone & Telegraph Co.

**121 Cal. App. 2d 497, 263 P.2d 465 (1953). Decided November 24, 1953. Second District, Division Three; Civ. No. 19586.** Presiding Justice Shinn wrote the opinion, with Justices Wood (Parker) and Vallée concurring. Assigned account: Civil Procedure casebook, physical PDF page 392, printed pages 751–752, Note 2 on required parties.

## Facts alleged and relief sought

Pacific Telephone published three successive directory listings: one for attorney Caryl Warner, one for “Mrs. Caryl Warner” at a separate address, and another for “Mrs. Caryl Warner” at the attorney’s address and number. The separate-address listing identified Jeanne, his divorced wife; the other identified Barbara Ruth, his current wife. Jeanne’s final divorce decree was entered September 24, 1950, and Barbara married Caryl four days later. Jeanne’s use of the former husband’s name appeared in several directory editions, including after the divorce. Caryl and Barbara objected, but the telephone company refused to change or delete the listing. **121 Cal. App. 2d at 498.**

Barbara sued only the company. Her five causes alleged that Jeanne intentionally used the name to claim public recognition as Caryl’s wife and injure Barbara, and that the company knowingly assisted her. Barbara claimed emotional distress and physical effects, including asthma attacks, and sought $10,000 on the first cause. Other causes asserted ownership and diminished prestige of the title, a secondary meaning associated with Caryl’s professional and social standing, invasion of privacy by depicting Barbara as part of a bigamous marriage, and the company’s knowledge or negligent disregard of resulting harm. She also sought exemplary damages. These were pleaded accusations, not findings that Jeanne had a malicious purpose, that the company acted tortiously, or that the name belonged exclusively to Barbara. **Id. at 498–499.**

## Procedural posture and issue

The company demurred to Barbara’s third amended and supplemental complaint. One ground was her failure to join Jeanne as an indispensable defendant. An earlier ruling had identified that defect, and Barbara amended twice afterward without adding Jeanne. The final demurrer was sustained without leave to amend, and she appealed the ensuing dismissal. **Id. at 499–500, 502.**

The issue was whether Barbara could litigate her damages claims against the directory publisher without joining the person whose right to use the disputed name was central to those claims. The company also raised defenses that might defeat recovery even if Barbara established an exclusive naming right, but the court declined to resolve those alternative grounds once the party defect prevented further proceedings. **Id. at 501–502.**

## Historical rule and holding

Under California Code of Civil Procedure § 389 as then applied, the court had to order absent persons brought into the action when a complete determination could not be made without them. The opinion described that requirement as mandatory and jurisdictional. It treated a person whose rights inevitably would be affected by judgment as indispensable. It also discussed the trial court’s discretion to require joinder to resolve the company’s duty conclusively and avoid unnecessary later litigation. These are the court’s historical formulations, not an assertion that every modern required-party question is a subject-matter-jurisdiction defect. **Id. at 500–501.**

The court held that Jeanne had to be joined and that dismissal was proper after Barbara repeatedly refused. The action’s requested form—damages against the company—did not eliminate its consequences for Jeanne’s naming rights. **Id. at 500–502.**

## Reasoning: the absent person’s rights

The immediate dispute asked whether the directory listing injured Barbara. The underlying dispute was whether Barbara had the sole right to use “Mrs. Caryl Warner,” excluding Jeanne. Barbara could not prevail on the theory identified by the court without establishing that exclusivity. If she established it and recovered damages, the company would have to stop the challenged listing to protect itself. Conversely, if Jeanne could continue using her former husband’s name, the court reasoned that she could obtain a directory listing under that name. **Id. at 500–501.**

A favorable damages judgment thus would practically restrict Jeanne’s use of the name. The court would not permit a substantial restriction of those rights without her opportunity to be heard. The opinion’s earlier statement that the allegations could support preventing the use was conditional on a judicial determination of Barbara’s sole ownership and the alleged detriment; it did not decide those propositions on the merits. **Id. at 499–501.**

## Reasoning: the company’s exposure

The absence also threatened the existing defendant. If the company stopped Jeanne’s listing in response to a damages judgment, Jeanne could bring a later action challenging that refusal, possibly without Barbara as a party. The first action then would fail to resolve the company’s obligations between the competing claimants. The opinion credited the trial court’s explanation that the company furnished public services without discrimination and its subscribers had corresponding listing rights. **Id. at 499–501.**

The court considered protection against that additional litigation independently persuasive. It characterized the company as neutral and uninterested in the naming contest, despite Barbara’s allegations that it assisted Jeanne. That was the court’s procedural assessment of why a complete determination was appropriate, not an adjudication that every liability allegation against the company was false. Joining both claimants would settle the listing duty in one case rather than force piecemeal litigation. The trial court did not abuse its discretion by insisting on that joinder. **Id. at 501.**

## Judgment and issues left open

The Court of Appeal **affirmed the dismissal**. Barbara’s repeated refusal to join Jeanne prevented the litigation from proceeding; the court did not choose which woman possessed the naming right or decide the company’s other defenses. It recorded the concurrence of both other panel members and no separate writing or judicial footnote. The report notes denial of rehearing on December 10, 1953, and denial of a California Supreme Court hearing on January 20, 1954, with Justice Carter favoring a hearing; it reproduces no separate reasoning for those later orders. **Id. at 501–502.**

## Assigned significance

The assigned note describes the competing listings and causes, then asks whether the demurrer should be sustained in light of Bank of California. Warner supplies the answer through the effect of the requested judgment on the absent person and the existing defendant. Calling a lawsuit one for damages against a publisher did not prevent it from resolving another person’s disputed right in practical terms. The neighboring editorial question about whether indispensability really is jurisdictional is a teaching question; the historical Warner opinion itself used that characterization.
