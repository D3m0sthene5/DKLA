# Patrick v. Sedwick

**413 P.2d 169 (Alaska 1966).** April 21, 1966; consolidated Nos. 587 and 602. Rabinowitz wrote for a panel with Chief Justice Nesbett and Justice Dimond; no separate opinion. Assigned source: CivPro.pdf physical pages 597–598, printed pages 1178–1180, Patrick excerpt and the complete following editorial Note 1.

## Facts and successive appeals

Dr. Sedwick performed a subtotal thyroidectomy on Mary Patrick July 10, 1957. Patrick sued July 3, 1959. After a five-day nonjury trial in October 1961, the trial court’s February 1962 memorandum found postoperative negligence but did not adjudicate damages. Its October order refused a new liability trial but permitted further damages proceedings. The first appeal was dismissed as premature. The parties then stipulated to a final judgment for Sedwick, entered September 25, 1963, nunc pro tunc to February 1962. On the second appeal, the supreme court set aside the adverse liability findings and directed findings for Patrick and determination of damages. These earlier appeals, reported at 387 P.2d 294 and 391 P.2d 453, are distinct from this 1966 decision. 170–171, nn.1–8.

On remand, Sedwick sought three medical examinations and related depositions in November 1964. He asserted that a Teflon injection technique perfected by Dr. Robert Lewy in May 1963 could improve Patrick’s paralyzed vocal cord and reduce prospective damages. The trial judge reviewed the earlier evidence, considered the technique, and concluded its uncertain permanence did not justify more delay. January 12, 1965 judgment awarded $42,500 damages plus $2,500 attorney’s fees, explaining the opinion’s opening $45,000 total. Sedwick sought a new trial January 22. Both sides appealed the amount of damages; Sedwick also challenged denial of further discovery/new trial and sought reconsideration of liability. 171–173.

## Questions and governing standards

Could a treatment developed after trial qualify as newly discovered evidence? Were the damages findings specific enough for review, and was the unexplained attorney’s-fee departure proper? Could the earlier liability decision be reopened?

Law of the case barred re-examining liability absent exceptional circumstances; none were shown. The supreme court also refused to make independent damages findings from evidence it had not heard. Alaska Civil Rule 52(a) required sufficiently detailed findings to explain the award and permit appellate review. New-trial and discovery decisions were reviewed for abuse of discretion; exceptional circumstances or a miscarriage of justice would justify intervention. 173–177.

## Newly discovered evidence and discovery

The five requirements were probable effect on the result, discovery since trial, inability to discover before trial through due diligence, materiality, and evidence beyond merely cumulative or impeaching material. An additional temporal requirement applied: the evidence must concern facts **existing at the time of trial**. Lewy’s technique came into existence after the October 1961 trial, so it failed that requirement even though developed before the eventual 1965 damages judgment. 177.

The earlier mandate also gave the trial court broad discretion in how to determine damages. It could use the existing record, and it had carefully considered the uncertain permanence of the suggested improvement. Denial of further discovery and a new trial therefore was not an abuse whether the motions were characterized under Rules 59, 60(b)(2), or 60(b)(6). This reasoning did not universally forbid considering later developments in all procedural settings. Note 27’s quotation concerning unexpected improvement expressly recognizes a fraud qualification; no fraud finding supplied relief here. 177–178, nn.27–29.

## Damages findings and attorney’s fees

The trial court awarded $6,000 physical-impairment damages for the four years before trial and $15,000 for future impairment, despite Patrick being forty with a twenty-eight-year life expectancy and permanent voice/breathing difficulties. It allowed $3,000 for past lost time and $8,000 present value for future lost earning capacity. The findings did not explain the differing amounts well enough. The court required more specific findings, with earning capacity and degree of impairment particularly useful. It reserved the parties’ opposite claims that the overall $42,500 was inadequate or excessive. 175–176.

A separate limitation appears in **note 21**: the court rejected Patrick’s inadequacy objection to $3,000 past and $7,500 future pain-and-suffering damages, finding no clear error. Thus the remand did not declare every damages component defective. 176 n.21.

The $2,500 attorney’s-fee award also lacked an explanation for departing from the Rule 82(a) schedule, which would have produced $5,100 on that recovery. The rule partially compensated prevailing litigants and did not permit arbitrary departures. The court directed recomputation after remand and reasons on the record if the judge again departed from the schedule; it did not itself award $5,100. 178–179.

## Exact disposition and textbook reconciliation

**Judgment vacated; remanded for appropriate damages findings and a resulting judgment, including recomputation of attorney’s fees.** The denials of further discovery and a new trial based on Lewy’s technique were upheld, and liability was not reopened. The textbook accurately states the newly discovered evidence rule and that component’s affirmance, but its short excerpt omits the overall vacatur, damages remand, fee issue, and note 21 limitation.

The following textbook Note 1 uses ACLU v. Department of Defense to compare federal Rule 60(b)(2) and (6): the cited media reports were cumulative and would not change the ruling, and grounds within clause two could not simply be relabeled under clause six. That is editorial discussion of a distinct federal case, not an additional holding by Patrick. The complete note continues onto physical page 598 and was read there.
