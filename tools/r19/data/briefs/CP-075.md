# Hadges v. Yonkers Racing Corp.

**48 F.3d 1320 (2d Cir. 1995) · Civil Procedure · CP-075**

The Second Circuit affirmed rejection of a harness driver's effort to reopen an earlier judgment for fraud on the court, but reversed a $2,000 Rule 11 sanction against him and censure of his lawyer. The unsuccessful merits claim did not justify disregarding the 1993 rule's separate-motion, safe-harbor, notice, and evidentiary-support requirements. This divided outcome is central: the driver did not obtain a new adjudication of his exclusion from Yonkers Raceway, while both challenged sanctions were reversed.

George Hadges worked as a harness driver, trainer, and owner. His state racing license, first issued in 1972, was revoked in 1974 for incomplete disclosure of his arrest record, restored in 1976, and suspended for six months in 1989 following a determination that he had improperly passed wagering information at a 1986 race. After license reinstatement in September 1989, Yonkers Racing Corporation independently denied him permission to work at its track. He sued under §1983, claiming deprivation of due process. In 1990 the federal court granted YRC summary judgment because its conduct was not state action, and the judgment was affirmed. YRC manager Galterio had stated that other regional tracks remained available, while the district court suggested that concerted exclusion by other tracks might establish state action. (Opinion ¶¶10–13.)

Subsequent litigation complicated that record. A 1992 New York state action alleging blackballing and other claims failed. A separate federal action against the state-run Meadowlands began in 1993 and settled. Meadowlands manager Garland explained that its 1992 resolution made exclusion at another track a basis for excluding a driver there. Hadges then brought this independent action seeking relief from the 1990 judgment, arguing that the Garland affidavit exposed Galterio's earlier representation as fraud on the court. The assigned editorial introduction dates the Meadowlands suit to 1992; the complete appellate opinion places the suit in 1993 and the exclusion resolution in 1992, which are the dates used here. (¶¶14–17.)

More than one year had passed since the judgment, foreclosing a timely motion under Rule 60(b)(3). The rule's then-existing savings clause nevertheless preserved an independent action for fraud upon the court. That category was narrower than ordinary fraud against an adversary: it required misconduct seriously impairing adjudicative integrity, not merely a later development undermining a prior representation. Reviewing for abuse of discretion, the court found no adequate showing. Meadowlands adopted its resolution and excluded Hadges in 1992, after the 1990 litigation. Galterio denied contrary knowledge, and Hadges supplied no persuasive evidence otherwise. One state track's later reliance on a private track's decision did not establish their conspiracy or concerted blackballing by all New York tracks. Although the action was not frivolous, its asserted fraud did not approach the necessary impairment of adjudication. The court therefore affirmed denial of relief without needing disputed res judicata or abstention grounds. (¶¶34–41.)

The sanctions arose from different conduct within that unsuccessful action. Hadges said that he had not worked for more than four years. YRC produced records of five races at Monticello in 1991 and seven in 1993. He subsequently admitted those appearances but explained their minimal earnings. He also supplied an undated scratch sheet to support an alleged 1989 exclusion by state racing judges; YRC showed that it concerned a 1987 event. Hadges corrected the date and supplied affidavits about another asserted 1989 exclusion. Lawyer William Kunstler said he had not known the original date was wrong. The district court nevertheless fined Hadges for misrepresentation and censured Kunstler for inadequate investigation and failure to disclose the pending state litigation. (¶¶19–27.)

YRC had requested sanctions inside its dismissal papers, rather than by a separate motion served twenty-one days before presentation to the court. The 1993 Rule 11 amendment had taken effect five days before this complaint was filed and fifteen days before the request, so its safeguards governed. Those safeguards allowed correction or withdrawal during the safe harbor and required reasonable inquiry and evidentiary support rather than the older certification formulation. The separate-motion requirement also prevented sanctions from being folded casually into another litigation request. (¶¶21, 44–49.)

That failure mattered concretely. Hadges admitted his Monticello racing in an affidavit sworn twelve days after the sanctions request. He and Kunstler also corrected the scratch-sheet date shortly after the court indicated that it was considering sanctions; the court did not rest sanctions on whether the newly described 1989 incident happened. The record supported the conclusion that a proper correction opportunity could have avoided the sanctions. Judicial footnote 3 notes an apparently incorrect filing stamp and a gap between swearing and filing the affidavit; the appellate analysis relies on the correction and procedural context without silently treating the stamp as an accurate 1993 filing date. (¶¶50–51 & n.3.)

The district court also used an allegedly unwarranted disqualification motion as part of its basis for fining Hadges. The amended rule prohibited monetary sanctions against a represented party for the specified legal-contention violation. Nor could the result simply be recast as court-initiated sanctions: the court said it was acting on YRC's request, and court initiation required a specific show-cause order. The panel doubted that the conduct approached the contempt-like circumstances contemplated for that route. It reversed the client's sanction. The actual refusal to recuse was not itself appealed, as judicial footnote 2 explains. (¶¶52–54 & n.2.)

Kunstler likewise lacked the safe harbor. On the substance, a lawyer could reasonably rely on objectively reasonable client information, assessed in context. The record contained the client's sworn account, the undated scratch sheet, another lawyer's affidavit, and Kunstler's experience litigating the Meadowlands exclusion. Much of the material establishing the scratch sheet's actual date belonged to YRC. These circumstances supplied an evidentiary basis even though the representations were incorrect; the decision does not grant unconditional immunity for repeating a client's assertion. Judicial footnote 1 also corrects YRC's own misleading presentation of earnings: its documents suggested nearly $2,000, while the uncontroverted record showed that Hadges as driver earned less than $100. (¶¶57–66 & n.1.)

The state-case nondisclosure was also an improper sanctions ground. The court's own notice of potential sanctions omitted it, while YRC's request was procedurally defective. The appellate panel agreed that the state blackballing issue had not been decided on its merits, observed that YRC could disclose its own litigation, and said disclosure would have been wiser without treating omission as sanctionable here. Finally, the trial judge's remarks appeared personally directed at Kunstler and activist lawyers; criticism of his partner's unrelated conduct was especially unwarranted. These considerations together supported reversal of censure. (¶¶67–70.)

Judge Feinberg wrote for Judges Feinberg, Kearse, and Altimari, with no separate writing. The exact disposition affirmed denial of Rule 60(b) relief and reversed the client sanction and attorney censure. The full opinion, all three judicial footnotes, and complete assigned principal passage were read. This brief applies the 1993 rules as the 1995 opinion describes them, without substituting later renumbering or claiming an independently checked present-day treatment result. (¶73.)
