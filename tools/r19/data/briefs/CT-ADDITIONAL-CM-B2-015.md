# Rash v. J.V. Intermediate, Ltd.
498 F.3d 1201 (10th Cir. 2007) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and mixed disposition
Judge Tymkovich wrote for a panel with Judges McConnell and McKay in this diversity action applying Texas law. The court reversed the determination that branch manager W. Clayton Rash owed no fiduciary duty to his employer, remanded for a jury determination of fiduciary-duty damages and a judicial determination of possible compensation forfeiture, and affirmed the rejection of the employer’s statute-of-frauds defense. The employment-renewal holding therefore did not conclusively protect all of Rash’s compensation from the separate fiduciary and equitable issues. No separate opinion appears. (1205–1206, 1215–1216.)

## Employment, outside company, and trial
JVIC operated industrial-plant maintenance businesses. It hired Rash to establish and manage its Tulsa division under a written two-year contract running from 1999 to 2001. He received a $125,000 base salary, 20 percent of the division’s net profits, and a termination bonus of 20 percent of its equity; he promised to devote his full working time and efforts to JVIC. He continued managing the division until resigning in July 2004, without a signed extension. He claimed that understated profits and equity had reduced his bonuses. (1205–1206.)

JVIC alleged that, beginning in 2001, Rash owned or participated in several undisclosed businesses. The central established conflict involved Total Industrial Plant Services, a scaffolding company in which he held a significant interest. TIPS bid for JVIC subcontracts, and Rash was instrumental in selecting subcontractors. TIPS received more than $1 million from JVIC between 2001 and 2004; JVIC’s own scaffolding services were never used by the Tulsa division. The parties disputed when Rash learned about JVIC’s scaffolding business, but the appellate holding did not depend on resolving that dispute. It depended on his undisclosed ownership in a company contracting with the employer he represented. (1205 & n.1, 1208–1211 & nn.2–4.)

The district court rejected JVIC’s statute-of-frauds argument before trial. At trial it granted Rash judgment as a matter of law on the fiduciary-duty counterclaim, leaving a duty-of-loyalty counterclaim for the jury. Rash requested $564,993 and received $444,933 on his contract claim. JVIC requested $143,000 and received $71,500 for breach of loyalty. The court denied its posttrial motions, including equitable forfeiture. The appellate court reviewed the summary-judgment and judgment-as-a-matter-of-law rulings de novo, construing factual inferences in the nonmovant’s favor, and separately reviewed the construction of Texas law. (1206.)

## Agency created a fiduciary duty
Texas recognizes informal fiduciary relationships arising from a preexisting special relationship of confidence, but also formal relationships—including principal and agent—that create duties as a matter of law. The district court relied on Meyer v. Cathey, which refused to impose an informal fiduciary relationship on business associates based on past dealings, friendship, and subjective trust. That case did not eliminate the independent duties arising from agency. Rash managed essentially every aspect of the Tulsa startup, solicited and reviewed subcontract bids, set customer rates, tracked costs, and contractually undertook to act for JVIC. Central-office review did not negate that role; an agent can negotiate or transmit information even without final authority to bind the principal. On the undisputed facts he was JVIC’s agent. (1207–1209 & n.2.)

The scope of an employee’s fiduciary duty must be tied to the transaction, rather than treated as an absolute prohibition on every outside pursuit. Preparations for later competition can be permissible. But an agent must disclose personal interests affecting the principal’s business and deal fairly with it. Even accepting Rash’s argument that scaffolding was outside his specific assignment or that the president generally approved outside ventures, those assertions did not establish informed consent to his ownership in TIPS while he selected it for JVIC subcontracts. His significant ownership, participation in subcontract selection, and nondisclosure were uncontroverted. The court held that this conduct breached his fiduciary duty as a matter of law. Footnote 4 also explains why the competing accounts of generalized permission did not amount to unrestricted consent to that conflict. (1209–1211 & nn.3–4.)

## Damages and forfeiture remained for remand
The loyalty verdict did not render dismissal of the fiduciary claim harmless. The loyalty instruction focused on competition without consent and placed the burden on JVIC. The proposed fiduciary instruction imposed broader duties of fairness, candor, and priority for the principal’s interests, and required Rash to demonstrate compliance. Because the jury had awarded only half the requested loyalty damages, the court could not conclude that the stronger fiduciary framework would produce the same result. A jury therefore had to determine damages on remand. JVIC had treated the two claims as alternatives, so the fiduciary award had to account for the $71,500 loyalty recovery; total collection on those alternative damages theories could not exceed $143,000. The appellate court did not authorize adding a second $143,000 award to the first recovery. (1211–1212.)

Compensation forfeiture presented an additional equitable question. Texas law permits forfeiture for a clear and serious breach, protecting relationships of trust and discouraging disloyalty rather than merely compensating proven losses. Relevant factors include seriousness, timing, willfulness, effect on the value of services, other threatened or actual harm, and adequacy of other remedies. Full damages do not categorically exclude forfeiture, although their adequacy remains significant. The district court had not performed that analysis because it erroneously found no fiduciary relationship. The appellate court declined JVIC’s request to order total forfeiture itself. The trial judge must decide whether forfeiture is appropriate and its amount, while a jury may need to resolve disputed underlying facts concerning culpability, service value, or harm. (1212–1214 & n.6.)

Footnote 5 separately limits the claim that the jury should have received breach of fiduciary duty as an affirmative defense to Rash’s contract action. Any instructional relief would duplicate the forfeiture analysis, and JVIC failed to supply authority or analysis establishing that proposed defense under Texas law, waiving the issue. Thus reversal of the fiduciary counterclaim did not itself decide every disputed affirmative-defense instruction. (1213 & n.5.)

## One-year provision and implied renewal
Texas Business and Commerce Code § 26.01(b)(6) requires a writing for an agreement not to be performed within one year. The original two-year contract was within the statute. But the district court found that the parties’ later conduct renewed it on an indefinite or month-to-month basis. The president testified that they kept going under the agreement, and Rash described an assurance that it remained effective while he worked for JVIC. The appellate court found the month-to-month construction reasonable, not clearly erroneous. (1214–1215 & n.7.)

JVIC argued that Farone v. Bag’n Baggage barred any implied renewal of an original contract subject to the statute. The court read that decision more narrowly, as involving renewal of the entire contract including its term exceeding one year. Its broader language made sense in light of that term, rather than as a rule that a previous two-year agreement permanently taints every subsequent arrangement. The statute itself does not require every extension to be written. Texas law excludes indefinite employment agreements from the one-year provision, and ordinary continuation of employment after a written term expires can continue the prior arrangement. Applying the statute to an indefinite or short renewal solely because the original term was longer would increase uncertainty rather than advance its antifraud purpose. (1214–1215.)

The court therefore affirmed the ruling that the statute did not bar Rash’s post-2001 contract claims. It did not hold that every implied renewal is enforceable, that renewed fixed terms exceeding a year avoid the statute, or that the surviving bonus claim is immune from forfeiture. The casebook’s account illustrates this distinction; its quoted proposition appears on actual reporter page 1215, although the assigned quotation cites 1214. (1215–1216.)
