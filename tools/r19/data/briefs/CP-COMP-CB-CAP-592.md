# Moses H. Cone Memorial Hospital v. Mercury Construction Corp.

**Citation:** 460 U.S. 1 (1983). **Court and date:** United States Supreme Court, February 23, 1983; argued November 2, 1982; No. 81-1203. **Opinion:** Brennan, joined by White, Marshall, Blackmun, Powell, and Stevens. Rehnquist dissented, joined by Chief Justice Burger and O'Connor. **Course:** Civil Procedure — parallel state litigation, appealability, and enforcement of arbitration agreements. **Assigned:** CivPro.pdf, physical pages 704–705, printed page 1400, Moses H. Cone paragraph under Changing Judicial Attitudes toward ADR.

## Facts and procedural history

The North Carolina hospital and Mercury, an Alabama construction contractor, agreed in July 1975 to hospital additions. Their contract required initial presentation of disputes to an independent architect, then permitted binding arbitration under a broad clause, with specified exclusions and timing conditions. The exclusions for artistic effect and final-payment waiver were not asserted here. During construction, Mercury agreed at the architect's request to defer delay-and-impact claims until substantial completion. The hospital disputed the architect's authority to make that arrangement, rather than denying that the arrangement existed. (4–6 & nn.1–3.)

Mercury submitted claims in January 1980. After months of discussions and an August presentation, the hospital announced it would pay nothing and filed a North Carolina declaratory suit against Mercury and the architect on October 8. It disputed liability and arbitrability and sought indemnity from the architect if liable. Mercury mailed its arbitration demand the next day. The hospital obtained an ex parte injunction against steps toward arbitration; on Mercury's objection, it was dissolved October 27. (6–7.)

Mercury then sought a federal order compelling arbitration under § 4 of the Federal Arbitration Act, invoking diversity jurisdiction. Its attempted removal of the state action was separately remanded for lack of complete diversity because the hospital and architect were both North Carolina corporations; that remand was not before the Supreme Court. The district court stayed the federal petition pending the state suit because both presented the same arbitrability issue. Mercury sought ordinary appeal and mandamus. The Fourth Circuit en banc reversed and effectively directed entry of an arbitration order. The Supreme Court affirmed. (7–9 & nn.4–6; 29.)

## First holding: this stay was immediately appealable

**The stay was a final decision under § 1291 because it effectively surrendered the federal suit to the state court.** Arbitrability was the federal action's only substantive issue. A state decision would bind the federal court, leaving no further substantive litigation there. The stay therefore operated as a dismissal, even though formally subject to revision. (8–11.)

Footnote 11 limits this reasoning: ordinary stays are not generally final. It distinguished a court's refusal to permit federal litigation from an interlocutory decision that makes litigation economically unattractive, as in the rejected death-knell theory. Merely allowing a state court to decide a shared issue first did not make an order final; the sole purpose and effect here were to surrender federal jurisdiction. (10–11 n.11.)

Alternatively, the order met Cohen's collateral-order requirements. It conclusively resolved the refusal to proceed, presented an important question separate from the merits, and would escape effective later review once the state judgment became preclusive. Formal revisability alone did not make it inherently tentative: the district judge did not appear to anticipate reconsideration. With an ordinary appeal available, the appeals court did not need to apply mandamus's more demanding standard first. (8 n.6, 11–13 & nn.12–14.)

## Second holding: no exceptional circumstances justified deferral

**The district court abused its discretion under Colorado River.** Parallel state litigation alone supplied no bar to a federal action. Deferral for judicial administration required exceptional circumstances, with careful balancing heavily favoring exercise of jurisdiction. The factors were contextual, not a mechanical checklist: jurisdiction over property, convenience, piecemeal litigation, and the progress of the concurrent proceedings, together with other relevant considerations. None of the traditional abstention categories was asserted to apply. (13–16 & n.16.)

Will v. Calvert Fire did not dilute that standard. Its four-Justice opinion did not displace Colorado River, and Blackmun plus the four dissenters supported that test. Calvert also concerned mandamus, whose stringent standard differed from review on an ordinary appeal. District-court discretion remained reviewable under the governing legal standard. (16–19 & nn.19–21.)

There was no property under either court's control and no claimed inconvenience of the federal forum. Separate proceedings against the architect were a consequence of an arbitration agreement that did not bind the architect, rather than a reason to defeat Mercury's agreed arbitration. The Act required separate resolution when necessary to enforce an agreement. Note 23 nevertheless recognized discretion to stay related nonarbitrating litigation pending arbitration. (19–20 & nn.22–23.)

The state complaint's nineteen-day lead did not establish controlling priority. Mercury's § 4 claim required refusal to arbitrate, which occurred less than a day before the hospital filed; an intervening state injunction further explained the sequence. More fundamentally, actual progress mattered. Apart from the dissolved injunction, the state case had made little progress, while federal briefing and evidence had substantially prepared the arbitrability issue for resolution. Deferral frustrated the Act's contemplated summary and speedy procedure. The Court did not need to decide that the hospital acted vexatiously or in bad faith. (17–18 n.20, 21–23 & nn.24–27.)

Federal arbitrability law also weighed against surrender. The Court described § 2's policy favoring enforcement of covered agreements and resolving doubts about the scope of arbitrable issues in favor of arbitration. **That substantive federal policy did not itself create federal-question jurisdiction.** Note 32 required diversity or another independent jurisdictional basis for a federal enforcement proceeding. Concurrent state jurisdiction did not eliminate the federal-law consideration. (23–26 & nn.29–33.)

Finally, substantial doubt existed that the state action could give Mercury an affirmative order compelling arbitration. A stay of litigation alone might let the party resisting payment do nothing, forcing Mercury back to federal court. The Court did not disparage state competence or resolve whether § 4 itself required state courts to issue such orders. The exceptional-circumstances analysis applied to this stay as to a dismissal; it reserved which form should ordinarily be preferred when deferral is proper. (26–28 & nn.34–37.)

## Arbitration direction and exact judgment

The hospital did not contest the appellate holding's substantive correctness on arbitrability in the Supreme Court, but challenged the procedure of deciding an issue the district court had not reached. The Court acknowledged that appellate courts ordinarily should not decide such issues. Here, full briefs and evidentiary submissions, no factual dispute requiring a jury, the statutory interest in prompt arbitration, and apparent absence of prejudice supported the Fourth Circuit's exercise of latitude under § 2106. (29.)

**Affirmed the Fourth Circuit's judgment reversing the federal stay and directing proceedings effectively requiring an arbitration order.** The Court did not award Mercury construction damages, resolve the architect's indemnity liability, or establish federal jurisdiction solely from the FAA. (29.)

## Rehnquist's dissent

Rehnquist regarded the stay as tentative docket management, comparable to postponing trial. Possible preclusion from a state decision did not turn it into a final order. He distinguished Idlewild and argued that Coopers & Lybrand's rejection of the death-knell theory undermined the majority's approach. The Cohen requirements were not met: the stay could change with circumstances, and forum selection lacked the requisite independent importance. He emphasized disruption from piecemeal appeals. (30–35.)

He would vacate and remand with instructions to dismiss the appeal for lack of jurisdiction. Failing that, he would reverse the arbitrability direction and return that issue to the district court. Section 2106 did not permit an appellate court to act as a trial court or bypass the district court's factual and jury-trial responsibilities. His sole footnote doubted that appellate intervention had actually accelerated arbitration. He did not decide whether the stay was substantively proper because his jurisdictional conclusion made that unnecessary. (34–36 & 34 n.*.)

## Textbook and verification

The textbook accurately summarizes the federal court's ability to adjudicate despite the state litigation and identifies the policy passage at 24. The full decision concerns whether this particular stay was appealable and justified; it does not announce that every arbitration petition defeats parallel litigation. The nearby Wilko, Southland, and later arbitration cases describe separate stages in the textbook's account. (Assigned 704–705/1400.)
