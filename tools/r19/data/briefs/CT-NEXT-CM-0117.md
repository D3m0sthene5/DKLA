# In the Matter of Baby M
109 N.J. 396, 537 A.2d 1227 (1988) | Contracts

## Agreement, events, and proceedings
William Stern wanted a biological child; his wife Elizabeth feared that pregnancy might worsen possible multiple sclerosis. The court distinguished her genuine anxiety from medical evidence indicating a comparatively small pregnancy risk. Through the Infertility Center of New York, Stern arranged for Mary Beth Whitehead to be inseminated with his sperm, carry a child, surrender custody, and cooperate in permanently terminating her parental rights so Elizabeth could adopt. Mary Beth’s husband Richard also undertook to surrender any rights and rebut presumed paternity. Elizabeth was not a contracting party. William promised Mary Beth $10,000 upon delivery and completion of her obligations and separately promised the center $7,500. The court regarded the parties’ initial motives as sincere: the Sterns sought a family, and Mary Beth described helping an infertile couple while also seeking money for her own family. 109 N.J. at 411–414.

The underlying agreement also allocated pregnancy risks and control. It made the Whiteheads assume risks including death, provided specified medical-expense payments with time limits, and required examinations and compliance with medical instructions. It promised custody to Elizabeth if William died. A fetal-abnormality provision required abortion on William’s demand, with refusal ending his contractual obligations except statutory paternity duties; he remained responsible for abnormalities not detected before birth. The parties acknowledged voluntary execution, and the document included a severability clause. These were contractual provisions, not judicial rulings that each provision was independently enforceable. Id. at 470–474, Appendix A ¶¶4(C), 5–6, 9, 11, 13–18; Contracts Selections, printed pages 703–705.

The child was born March 27, 1986. Mary Beth surrendered her on March 30 but experienced severe distress and asked to have her back for a week. Concerned about her expressed despair, the Sterns agreed. She then refused to return the child. William sued to enforce the contract and obtained an ex parte transfer order. Richard escaped with the baby through a window, and the Whiteheads fled to Florida, moving among numerous lodgings. Recorded calls included threats of suicide, killing the child, and falsely accusing Stern of abusing another daughter. Florida authorities eventually enforced an order returning the child to the Sterns. The trial court continued their temporary custody and allowed Mary Beth limited visitation. Id. at 414–417.

After a thirty-two-day trial involving fact and expert witnesses, the court upheld the contract, terminated Mary Beth’s parental rights, awarded sole custody to William, and permitted Elizabeth’s adoption. Although it considered the child’s best interests, it treated that inquiry as a prerequisite to specific performance of a valid contract. Mary Beth appealed, and the Supreme Court granted direct certification. She challenged the contract under adoption law, public policy, and constitutional protections and argued for custody, including as a deterrent to surrogacy. The Sterns defended the contract and asserted reproductive privacy. The child’s guardian advocated her best interests without taking a position on contractual validity. Id. at 417–421.

## Holding and statutory conflicts
Chief Justice Wilentz’s unanimous court held the paid, irrevocable surrogacy agreement illegal and unenforceable under the existing New Jersey statutes and public policy. It reversed termination and adoption, restored Mary Beth’s legal motherhood, affirmed custody in William on the child’s best interests, and remanded visitation. These are distinct results: invalidating the contract did not require transferring custody to Mary Beth. Id. at 410–411, 468–469.

Three statutory conflicts independently supported invalidity. First, the adoption law prohibited payment in connection with placement for adoption, subject to approved-agency fees and specified medical expenses. Describing the $10,000 as compensation for services and omitting Elizabeth as a contracting party did not change its substance. Payment depended on surrender and completion of parental-rights termination, and markedly smaller or no compensation followed fetal death despite services rendered. The center’s arrangements also facilitated adoption. The court characterized the payment as illegal and possibly criminal; it did not convict anyone. Id. at 421–425 & n.4.

Second, contractual consent could not replace statutory grounds for terminating parental rights. Outside a qualifying formal surrender to an approved agency or the public agency, the law required stringent proof of abandonment, serious neglect, or comparable harm, rather than merely a finding that another home would be better. Stepparent adoption did not relax that substantive protection, and the Parentage Act’s best-interests language did not authorize a different termination standard. No such grounds existed here: Mary Beth had not abandoned the child, the trial court did not find her unfit, and it described her as a good mother to her older children. Her struggle to retain the baby could not support a finding that she had forsaken parental obligations. Without a valid termination, Elizabeth’s adoption could not stand. Id. at 425–429 & nn.6–7, 444–447.

Third, the agreement made surrender irrevocable before conception. The statutory scheme carefully confined irrevocable surrender to prescribed post-birth procedures involving authorized agencies, formal acknowledgment, and protective counseling. In private placement, consent did not itself establish the grounds for termination and could be retracted. The contract bypassed those safeguards. These provisions were central to the transaction, rendering the entire bargain unenforceable. Id. at 429–434 & n.8.

## Public policy and constitutional limits
The contract also predetermined custody and permanent separation without assessing the future child’s interests, the proposed custodians’ suitability, or the mother’s wishes after birth. It subordinated the mother’s equal parental status and used payment and a binding promise to secure relinquishment before she could know the strength of her bond. The center’s psychological evaluation had warned that surrender might be difficult, yet the warning was not disclosed or pursued. The court considered the profit incentive, unequal financial circumstances, and risks of exploitation reasons to resist treating consent as sufficient. It acknowledged that some surrogacy arrangements produced satisfaction, but concluded that private agreement could not override the protective policies expressed by existing law. Id. at 434–444 & nn.9–11.

Stern’s constitutional right to procreate did not include a right to exclusive custody or enforcement of a contract extinguishing the other natural parent’s rights. Nor did the sperm-donor statute require equivalent treatment of a woman who underwent pregnancy and birth. Because state law restored Mary Beth’s rights, the court did not decide whether their termination also independently violated her constitutional right to companionship, or resolve related state-action and Thirteenth Amendment questions. It also reserved whether a hypothetical future statutory scheme could constitutionally validate surrogacy. Id. at 447–453 & nn.12–16.

The comparative judicial note recognized a different Kentucky approach: that court read its own baby-selling statute as inapplicable to a preconception arrangement, but still allowed the mother to withdraw during pregnancy or immediately after birth. The note also distinguished the United Kingdom’s commercial-broker prohibition from unpaid, revocable arrangements. Those comparisons described other legal systems; they did not relax New Jersey’s statutory protections. Id. at 442–444 n.11; assigned judicial note 2, printed page 800.

## Custody, visitation, and exact judgment
Custody between the natural parents depended on the child’s best interests, with no automatic preference based solely on sex. The court rejected awarding custody to Mary Beth merely to deter illegal contracting. It likewise declined to decide on a hypothetical record excluding consequences of the initial transfer order. The child’s actual circumstances mattered even if judicial error had contributed to them. Credible expert testimony and the demonstrated stability, nurturing, and openness of the Stern household supported custody in William. The court criticized excessively harsh judgments of Mary Beth’s understandable attachment and cautioned that education or wealth did not define an ideal life. It affirmed custody without finding either parent unfit. Id. at 452–462 & nn.17–18.

For future temporary-custody disputes of this kind, the contract furnished no basis for removal from the mother. A highly persuasive showing of unfitness, danger, or similar circumstances would be necessary; threatened flight ordinarily called at most for an injunction against leaving. Id. at 462–463.

Visitation had not been adequately litigated because the original termination made it unnecessary below. Mary Beth was entitled to visitation at some point; the trial court was to determine its form, conditions, and commencement, considering both parental interests and the child’s paramount interests. The Supreme Court ordered a different judge on remand, permitted an abbreviated but reliable evidentiary process, required a decision within ninety days, reduced the appeal period to ten days, and certified any appeal directly to itself. It did not order an immediate unrestricted schedule. Id. at 463–468 & nn.19–20.

The judgment was affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded. All seven justices—Wilentz, Clifford, Handler, Pollock, O’Hern, Garibaldi, and Stein—joined; none opposed. The court found no existing prohibition on a voluntary unpaid arrangement permitting the mother to change her mind, and left statutory reform to the legislature within constitutional limits. Id. at 468–469.
