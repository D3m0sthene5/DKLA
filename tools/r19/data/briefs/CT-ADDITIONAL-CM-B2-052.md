# Gengaro v. City of New Haven
118 Conn. App. 642, 984 A.2d 1133 (2009) | Contracts

## Holding and scope
The Connecticut Appellate Court affirmed summary judgment for the city and union because Gengaro ratified his resignation settlement through accepting benefits, seeking continued payments, and waiting ten months before challenging it. Even assuming an initial factual issue about undue influence, subsequent ratification foreclosed avoidance as a matter of law. The appellate court expressly did not decide defendants' alternative claim that the original evidence was insufficient to establish undue influence. DiPentima wrote for a unanimous panel with Bishop and Berdon; no separate writing appears. (642–644, 650–654 & n. 3.)

## Suspension and settlement
The city employed Gengaro in its livable cities initiative department, and he belonged to Local 3144, AFSCME. In February 2004 it suspended him indefinitely while investigating allegations of sexual harassment and inappropriate personal conduct. The opinion did not adjudicate whether he committed the alleged harassment. Following notice and a March 26 pretermination hearing, city and union representatives negotiated a confidential settlement. He would resign in exchange for $7,500, accrued vacation and sick pay, two additional months of medical coverage, and the city's promise not to oppose unemployment compensation. (644.)

The agreement limited disclosure and public disparagement. Gengaro acknowledged advice to consult an attorney, at least twenty-one days to consider the settlement and release, and seven days after signing in which to revoke. He signed June 9, 2004. He alleged that the city threatened termination and loss of negotiated benefits unless he signed, and that the union threatened to withdraw representation, including in arbitration. He also alleged serious financial and medical problems, medical bills, responsibility for an elderly stroke survivor, and exposure of embarrassing allegations. He claimed these circumstances left no reasonable alternative. Those surrounding circumstances were allegations in the complaint, not facts established by a full evidentiary trial. (644–646.)

## Complaint, affidavits, and trial ruling
Gengaro sued the city for declaratory relief on April 8, 2005, adding the union by amended complaint that September. Thus the first suit was against the city alone; the assigned problem compresses the later addition into the ten-month filing account. He also alleged that department director Rizzo breached the settlement by offering staff an open door to discuss his situation. (645–646.)

Defendants moved for summary judgment in May and July 2007, arguing both absence of a factual basis for undue influence and ratification by accepting benefits. They filed the agreement and affidavits from labor-relations director Hibson and Rizzo. Hibson stated that Gengaro could have pursued a termination grievance in arbitration, that the city was ready to proceed, that Gengaro repeatedly contacted it to confirm payments, and that he received the agreed benefits. Gengaro's opposing affidavits concentrated on not personally seeing investigation evidence and on the settlement-or-termination ultimatum. They did not substantiate the financial, medical, and family circumstances asserted in his complaint. (646–649.)

The trial court granted both motions in May 2008. It considered the threatened termination insufficient by itself to establish destruction of free agency and regarded the alleged disclosure breach as unsupported by affidavit or documentation, while the city had submitted a rebuttal affidavit. Note 1 also recounts the trial judge's concern that a purported understanding extending the employee's unambiguous confidentiality obligation to the city would violate the parol-evidence rule. These are the reported trial rulings and concerns, not a new appellate construction of every confidentiality provision. (645–647 & n. 1.)

## Governing principles
Summary judgment required no genuine material factual issue and legal entitlement to judgment. Evidence was viewed favorably to the opponent, but mere assertions could not refute a supported motion; appellate review was plenary. A compromise free of fraud, mistake, or undue influence was conclusive between the parties. Undue influence meant sufficient control to destroy free agency and compel an act contrary to the person's normal will. Its four general elements concerned susceptibility, opportunity, disposition to influence, and a result indicating influence. Relevant circumstances included health and age, independent advice, consideration, distress and necessities, predisposition, relative extent of the transfer, persuasion, and the parties' relationship. (647–650.)

The decisive issue was defendants' alternative ratification argument, raised below and on appeal. Young v. Data Switch had addressed a severance agreement challenged as coerced eighteen months after execution. Applying Restatement sections 380 and 381, it distinguished initial voidability from losing the power to avoid once the coercive circumstances ceased. Unreasonable delay, especially while retaining benefits, and conduct inconsistent with disaffirmance could bar avoidance. Ratifying intent could be inferred from affirmative conduct or silence; the relevant question was opportunity to disaffirm after the pressure ceased rather than the person's explanation for not doing so. The opinion did not prescribe ten months as an automatic deadline for all cases. (650–652 & n. 2.)

## Applying ratification to undue influence
Although Young involved duress, Gengaro alleged undue influence. The court recognized their distinctions but applied the same ratification reasoning because both involve compromised free assent when the agreement is made. Their shared premise did not mean that their initial substantive elements were identical. (652–653.)

Gengaro had a contractual seven-day revocation opportunity, repeatedly sought continued payments after signing, accepted and enjoyed negotiated benefits, and waited at least ten months to sue. Together these circumstances established ratification as a matter of law. His original claim of compulsion therefore could not void the settlement. The appellate court sustained the correct judgment on this alternative ground and expressly left the sufficiency-of-undue-influence argument unaddressed in note 3. The opinion's earlier description of missing evidence and the trial judge's reasoning must not be confused with the dispositive appellate holding. (653–654 & n. 3.)

## Exact disposition and source checks
The judgment for both city and union was affirmed, leaving the settlement enforceable against the requested avoidance. The court did not award new damages, determine the truth of the harassment allegations, or hold that every employment ultimatum can never be undue influence. It also did not order restoration of benefits as an alternative remedy. (654.)
