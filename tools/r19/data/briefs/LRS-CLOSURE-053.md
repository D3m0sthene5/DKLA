# Coleman v. Miller
307 U.S. 433 (1939) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Facts and the state-court proceeding

Congress proposed the Child Labor Amendment in June 1924. It would authorize Congress to limit, regulate and prohibit the labor of persons under eighteen while preserving state power except where necessary to give effect to congressional legislation. Kansas rejected the proposal in January 1925 and sent its rejection to the Secretary of State. In January 1937 the Kansas Senate considered a resolution ratifying it. The forty senators divided twenty to twenty. The Lieutenant Governor, presiding over the Senate, cast a vote in favor, and the House of Representatives subsequently adopted the resolution by a majority. (435–436 & n. 1.)

Twenty-one senators, including all twenty who voted against ratification, and three House members brought an original mandamus proceeding in the Kansas Supreme Court. They sought to replace the Senate secretary’s endorsement that the resolution had passed with an endorsement that it had not passed, and to prevent the legislative officers and state Secretary of State from authenticating and forwarding the resolution. They claimed the Lieutenant Governor was not entitled to vote in an Article V ratification, that Kansas’s previous rejection exhausted its power to act, and that the amendment had expired through widespread rejection and the passage of time. An alternative writ issued; the State appeared through its Attorney General and was made a defendant. On undisputed facts the Kansas Supreme Court upheld the Lieutenant Governor’s vote and the amendment’s continuing vitality, and denied mandamus. The U.S. Supreme Court granted certiorari. (436–438 & n. 2.)

## Questions and disposition

The federal questions were whether these legislators could invoke Supreme Court review, whether Article V permitted the Lieutenant Governor to break the tie, and whether prior rejection or the lapse of nearly thirteen years prevented Kansas from ratifying. Chief Justice Hughes’s opinion, announced by Justice Stone, sustained jurisdiction and rejected judicial interference with certification on the latter two grounds. The Court expressly stated that it was equally divided on whether the Lieutenant Governor question was justiciable or political and expressed no opinion on that point. That statement does not supply a merits holding that Article V affirmatively authorizes the tie-breaking vote. (437–438, 446–454.)

The actual judgment **affirmed** the Kansas Supreme Court’s denial of mandamus, on the grounds stated in Hughes’s opinion. Black, Roberts, Frankfurter and Douglas concurred in the result on a broader political-question theory while opposing the Court’s standing ruling. Butler, joined by McReynolds, dissented and would reverse because the proposal had expired. The affirmance therefore must be distinguished from both unanimity on standing and agreement on a single theory of Article V. The official report’s prefatory disposition at 434 says “reversed”; that contradicts the judicial opinion’s express conclusion and judgment at 456. This brief follows the actual judicial judgment. (456–460, 470–474.)

## Why the senators had standing

The decisive interest was the effectiveness of the twenty senators’ votes. If their challenge to the Lieutenant Governor’s participation were correct, their votes would have defeated the ratification resolution. Instead those votes had been overridden and rendered ineffective. Hughes treated this as a direct, adequate interest in vindicating a claimed federal constitutional right, rather than the undifferentiated interest of a citizen in constitutional government. State recognition of their entitlement to seek mandamus supplied the setting for review, and their federal claim had been rejected. Because they did not challenge the validity of a state statute, certiorari under the then-existing Judicial Code § 237(b), rather than the statute’s appeal provision, was the appropriate route. The opinion ultimately rested on the interest of **at least the twenty opposing senators**, without needing to establish an independent interest of every plaintiff. (437–438, 446.)

Hughes compared cases reviewing state-court judgments at the request of officials charged with enforcing statutes and cases involving electors or local taxpayers. Those litigants did not invariably show private monetary damage. Hawke and Leser supported review of state-court decisions concerning ratification; Leser’s recognition of a state-authorized voter challenge was especially pertinent despite Fairchild’s rejection of a generalized federal suit concerning the Nineteenth Amendment. The senators’ claim was stronger than a mere voter’s general concern because their particular votes would have controlled the legislative outcome. This reasoning does not confer standing on legislators simply because they lost a vote or dislike subsequent executive action. (438–446 & nn. 3–12.)

## Ratification after rejection and the passage of time

Hughes examined the political branches’ treatment of the Fourteenth Amendment, including ratification after prior rejection and attempts to withdraw earlier ratifications. Congress had declared the amendment adopted and directed its promulgation notwithstanding those objections. From that accepted history he concluded that the efficacy of ratifications in light of earlier rejection or attempted withdrawal was a political question ultimately for Congress in its control of promulgation. Article V referred to ratification, not rejection, and neither the Constitution nor the implementing statute authorized courts to prevent certification merely because Kansas had previously rejected the amendment. The judgment allowed the political departments to receive and address the ratification; it did not turn every issue concerning Article V into an ordinary judicial merits question. (447–451 & nn. 13–27.)

The elapsed-time challenge required a separate explanation. Dillon v. Gloss had upheld Congress’s authority to prescribe a reasonable deadline, but Congress had set no deadline for this proposal. Dillon’s discussion of reasonably contemporaneous ratification did not mean courts should themselves decide when an undated proposal expired. Assessing the continuing relevance of a child-labor amendment required consideration of changing political, social and economic conditions, not merely counting years or averaging the periods used for earlier amendments. There were no satisfactory judicial criteria for that assessment. Congress, when considering promulgation after the requisite ratifications, could make the final determination of continuing vitality, and its decision would not be judicially reviewable. Hughes identified both the appropriateness of political-branch finality and the absence of workable judicial standards as reasons for treating these questions as political. (451–456 & nn. 28–31.)

## The separate writings

Black’s concurrence, joined by Roberts, Frankfurter and Douglas, went further than Hughes. After unsuccessfully opposing standing, these Justices participated in the remaining questions and agreed with the result. They regarded the entire amending process, from submission through adoption, as exclusively political and controlled by Congress. They objected to dividing that process into some judicially interpretable questions and some conclusively political ones, and would disapprove Dillon insofar as it purported to impose a judicial limitation on Congress. Their blanket exclusion of judicial intervention is a separate position, not the full content of Hughes’s reasoning. (456–460 & Black nn. 1–5.)

Frankfurter separately explained the same four Justices’ standing objection. State willingness to entertain the proceeding could not confer federal judicial power over plaintiffs without an individualized legal interest. The previous-rejection and elapsed-time claims belonged to citizens generally. The three House members and the senator who had voted for ratification lacked even the vote-nullification contention. As to the twenty opposing senators, Frankfurter distinguished the personal voting right protected in cases like Ashby v. White from disputes over the parliamentary voting procedures of political representatives. Legislative effectiveness was not a private injury of those representatives. He would dismiss certiorari before reaching political-question issues. His acknowledgment that state-created legal interests can support federal review did not make every state-court standing determination binding on the Supreme Court. (460–470 & Frankfurter nn. 1–6.)

Butler and McReynolds read Dillon as establishing an Article V requirement of ratification within a reasonable time. They would hold that more than thirteen years was excessive and reverse the Kansas judgment. Butler’s chronological footnote distinguished formal rejection from refusal to ratify and traced the proposal’s state-by-state history. He also objected that the newly decisive nonjusticiability theory had not been raised by the parties or amicus or identified when reargument was ordered; the Court should not abandon judicial determination without hearing argument on that point. (470–474 & Butler’s asterisked chronology, including its continuation at 474.)

## Assigned account and scope

The assigned account is the Coleman paragraph on November-b PDF 124, printed 1181, within the editors’ discussion of Raines. It emphasizes outcome-determinative vote nullification. Its reference to the senators’ “votes to ratify” is imprecise: the twenty senators establishing the interest had voted **against** ratification. The editors’ Raines comparison is not an additional Coleman opinion. This brief uses the complete 1939 judicial report, all three separate writings and all judicial notes, while separating the contradictory prefatory disposition from the actual affirmance. It describes the historical decision rather than certifying current citator status.
