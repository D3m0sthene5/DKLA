# Elsevier, Inc. v. Siew Yee Chew
287 F. Supp. 3d 374 (S.D.N.Y. 2018) | Civil Procedure

## Decision, source, and assigned account

United States Magistrate Judge Gabriel W. Gorenstein issued this opinion and order on March 12, 2018. It authorized email service of an amended complaint on thirteen foreign defendants. The assigned account is the Elsevier quotation in Civil Procedure PDF page 158, printed page 268, Note 4. The full ten-page judicial opinion includes two footnotes and no separate writing or appendix. ## Counterfeit-textbook allegations and the discovery of identities

Cengage, Elsevier, McGraw-Hill, and Pearson alleged that foreign retailers sold counterfeit copies of their textbooks in the United States through eBay storefronts. The alleged listings used the legitimate books' names, authors, cover images, trademarks, and identifying ISBNs, competing for the same customers. These were allegations underlying the service request, rather than an adjudication of copyright or trademark liability. Opinion pages 1–2.

The sellers received proceeds through PayPal or another payment system. Although many allegedly gave eBay fictitious names or physical addresses, they supplied working email addresses to operate their accounts. Before filing suit, the publishers made purchases from the storefronts and obtained associated email addresses. They filed the original complaint against twenty unidentified defendants on August 17, 2017, and obtained an ex parte temporary restraining order, expedited discovery, and an order to show cause. A preliminary injunction followed in September. Pages 2–3.

Under the earlier court orders, the publishers emailed the original complaint, application, and injunction materials to addresses associated with the storefronts. No foreign defendant responded. The publishers also subpoenaed eBay and PayPal, which supplied account information in December 2017. That information identified the defendants but showed multiple changing or incomplete physical addresses. Eight of the named foreign defendants, including the corporate entity Akitrade, were based in Malaysia; five were based in China. The publishers represented that they could not determine the defendants' true locations. Pages 2–3.

On January 29, 2018, the publishers amended their complaint to replace the previously unknown names with the newly identified defendants. Their February 2 motion requested permission to serve that amended complaint by email to all addresses identified by eBay and PayPal as associated with the defendants' business accounts. They proposed tracking delivery through a service such as ReadNotify. The request thus concerned authorization for this amended pleading, rather than a declaration that the defendants had actually read the earlier emails. Pages 3–4; page 1 n.1.

## Alternative service is an independent method, subject to limits

Rule 4(f) provided several methods for serving individuals abroad, including internationally agreed means, certain other methods reasonably calculated to give notice, and means not prohibited by international agreement that a court orders under subsection (3). Rule 4(h)(2) incorporated the Rule 4(f) methods for foreign corporations, subject to its exception for personal service; that provision supplied the route for Akitrade. Page 4.

Following Rio Properties and related decisions, the court rejected a mandatory hierarchy requiring exhaustion of subsections (1) and (2) before subsection (3). Alternative service was an available method in its own right. But an order still had to respect pertinent international agreements and satisfy due process, and permission remained a matter of judicial discretion. Page 5.

The court acknowledged that some judges imposed threshold requirements of reasonable service attempts and demonstrated need for intervention to discourage casual resort to alternative service. Those were discretionary safeguards, not a categorical command in the rule to exhaust Hague Convention service in every case. The court then examined why Convention attempts were inappropriate for these defendants. Page 6.

## Malaysia and China required separate treaty analysis

Malaysia was not a Hague Service Convention signatory at the time identified in the opinion. No other international agreement barred email service on the Malaysian defendants. The Convention route consequently was not available for them. This statement describes the court's 2018 analysis, without claiming verification of current treaty status. Page 6.

China and the United States were Convention parties. But service through China's Ministry of Justice required a defendant's address, and the defendants' incomplete, shifting address information frustrated identification of their true locations. The court also observed that Article 1 excludes cases where the recipient's address is unknown. It therefore declined to require the publishers to attempt the Convention process. Page 7.

Footnote 2 supplies a separate compatibility rationale. China had objected to service through postal channels under Article 10, but the cited district-court decisions treated that objection as not extending to email. The court accepted that distinction, including the view that email can be more reliable and easier to track than distant postal delivery. The ruling should therefore be understood as this court's treaty interpretation and application to the particular record, rather than an unconditional authorization of every electronic method in every foreign country. Pages 7–8 n.2.

## Why these email addresses were reasonably likely to give notice

The constitutional question was whether the proposed method was reasonably calculated, under the circumstances, to inform these defendants of the suit and afford them an opportunity to object. The court did not presume that any discovered email address would satisfy that standard. It relied on the particular defendants' online business operations and regular communication with customers through functional addresses tied to their eBay and PayPal accounts. Those ties were supported by the publishers' purchases, account discovery, and declaration. Pages 8–9.

Because the defendants used those channels to conduct the very business implicated in the lawsuit, the court concluded that email was the method most likely to notify them. The absence of responses to earlier emailed materials did not become a finding that the addresses were nonfunctional or that the defendants had actually received and understood the amended complaint. The prospective order rested on the demonstrated business connections and expected effectiveness of the chosen means. Pages 3, 8–9.

## Exact order and practical result

The court granted the alternative-service motion and authorized email service on the thirteen specifically listed foreign defendants, including Akitrade. It directed use of an email tracking service to preserve the maximum information about effectiveness in case that became relevant later. The order resolved the permitted service method; it did not enter a default judgment, establish infringement liability, or award damages. Pages 9–10.

The assigned quotation accurately emphasizes the connection between functioning business email accounts and the likelihood of meaningful notice. The complete opinion adds the prior anonymous pleading, identification through platform discovery, separate Malaysia and China analyses, absence of mandatory exhaustion, and preservation of tracking evidence. Those steps explain why the authorization depended on this record rather than on email's availability alone. Assigned printed 268; opinion pages 2–9.
