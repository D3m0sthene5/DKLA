# National Council for Adoption v. Blinken

**Course:** Legislation, Regulation & the State  
**Citation:** 4 F.4th 106 (D.C. Cir. 2021)  
**Court and date:** United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit, July 9, 2021  
**Opinion:** Judge Walker for the unanimous panel of Judges Pillard and Walker and Senior Judge Randolph; no separate writing appears.  
**Assigned source:** LRS November pages 1–165 scan, PDF pages 82–85, printed pages 657–660; principal excerpt ends before the Note on National Council, with judicial footnote 5 retained below editorial material.  
**Brief ID:** LRS-MAX-ADOPTION

## Case orientation

The State Department's website guidance categorically banned two forms of preliminary adoption referral. The general existing requirement to protect a child's best interests did not compel those categorical bans, so the guidance made new law rather than merely interpreting existing obligations. The court reversed the dismissal for lack of standing and remanded for the district court to enter an order vacating the guidance. It did not decide whether the agency could adopt the same substantive policy after proper notice and comment.

## Facts and guidance

In February 2018, a State Department webpage about international-adoption fees stated that a soft referral was unacceptable and might lead to adverse action. Confusion followed because some adoption providers had never encountered that term. A March notice defined two prohibited practices. First, a provider could not inform prospective parents about a specific child before the child's country determined eligibility for intercountry adoption, even without disclosing the child's name. Second, it could not match an eligible child to parents without an approved home study in a manner that removed the child from other families' consideration—sometimes called holding the child. (Slip opinion at 3–4.)

A May FAQ clarified that some preliminary matching before a home study was permitted. The provider could not hold the child's file in a way that prevented other providers from referring the child, discouraged other parents, or prevented authorities from considering alternatives. The court collectively called the February, March, and May publications the Guidance. It did not characterize the operative policy as a ban on every conceivable preliminary referral.

The National Council for Adoption, whose members help prospective parents adopt, alleged that the Guidance required notice and comment and was substantively unlawful. State argued it merely clarified policies and regulations in place since 2006. Providers faced potential adverse accreditation consequences if they engaged in the prohibited practices. (At 4–6, 14–15.)

## District-court proceedings and supplemental declarations

State moved to dismiss, asserting that the Council had not identified a member harmed by the two specifically prohibited practices. The Council filed initial member declarations with its opposition. State responded that general references to matching before home-study completion did not establish that the declarants engaged in prohibited holding or pre-eligibility referrals.

Under the district court's schedule, both sides also moved for summary judgment while dismissal remained pending. The Council submitted two supplemental declarations with its summary-judgment reply. State moved to strike them as late and alternatively attacked their sufficiency. The district court struck them, dismissed for lack of standing, and denied both summary-judgment motions as moot. The Council appealed associational standing; it did not appeal the separate rejection of organizational standing. (At 4–7; notes 2–3.)

## Standing and the decision to reach the merits

The court reviewed standing de novo and the striking of declarations for abuse of discretion. It held that the supplemental declarations should have been considered. Existing precedent permitted delayed material that shored up a reasonably substantial earlier showing and made standing patently apparent, particularly without prejudice. It did not create an unrestricted right to prove standing for the first time in any reply. (At 7–11; notes 3–4.)

Four considerations favored admission. The Council reasonably believed its initial submissions established standing and had gone a long way toward doing so. The supplements strengthened the same theory rather than introducing a new one. Injury and causation were apparent: Nightlight Christian Adoptions' president specifically stated that the agency previously made both prohibited types of referrals and would have continued appropriate instances but for the ban. State suffered no demonstrated prejudice; it attacked the declarations, supplied no refuting evidence, identified no further material it wanted to submit, and did not reply to the Council's no-prejudice argument. Striking the declarations was an abuse of discretion in these circumstances.

The Council also satisfied redressability. A procedural challenge did not require proof that comments would actually change the ultimate policy. The possibility that State would reconsider after lawful procedure was enough, given the established underlying injury. Protecting completion of adoptions was germane to the association, and the claims and requested relief did not require individual members to participate. Associational standing was established. (At 11–13.)

Although reversal of a threshold dismissal usually calls for remand on merits, the circuit exercised its discretion to decide the purely legal rule-classification question. The Council requested that course; State agreed there was no harm if standing existed. The issue was fully briefed, review was de novo, its answer was clear, and another appeal was likely. The opinion therefore did more than revive the complaint for an undecided merits proceeding. (At 13.)

## A legislative rule rather than an interpretation

A legislative rule has legal effect or exercises delegated legislative power with the force of law. An interpretive rule instead explains a proposition compelled or logically justified by an existing statute, regulation, or judicial decision. APA § 553 exempts the latter from notice and comment. (At 13–14.)

The Guidance intended to impose prohibitions enforceable through accreditation. State did not seriously deny those consequences. The existing regulations required substantial compliance with adoption standards and allowed adverse action, so the new categorical restrictions mattered to providers. Labeling the publication guidance did not remove that effect.

State had not previously announced a categorical prohibition on these two practices. The relevant guidance did not identify a specific legal provision compelling its restrictions. On appeal, State relied primarily on the best-interests-of-children standard. Even assuming that the prohibited practices often harmed children's interests, a context-specific standard did not compel a ban in every case. The court illustrated the issue with biological relatives nearly finished with a home study. It did not find as a fact that every such referral was beneficial; the example showed why a categorical rule required consideration through rulemaking. (At 14–16.)

The surrounding framework required eligibility and a home study before an adoption was completed or the child placed in the home. Those deadlines did not themselves require completion before every preliminary referral. Preliminary referrals could coexist with later satisfaction of the statutory requirements. The court therefore rejected the claimed derivation of a new categorical ban from the existing framework.

Note 5 preserved the distinction between earlier individual enforcement decisions and this categorical rule. The parties disputed whether State or its accrediting entity previously took adverse actions involving soft referrals. Even if such actions occurred, State did not claim they themselves compelled a universal prohibition or established a legal effect the Guidance merely explained. They did not bridge the gap between the existing individualized standard and the new categorical restrictions. (At 16, note 5.)

## Judgment and limits

The unanimous court reversed the district court's judgment and remanded for that court to enter an order vacating the Guidance and take other action consistent with the opinion. Because the defect was procedural, it did not reach the Council's substantive challenge. The court expressly contemplated that, after notice and comment, State might adopt a rule similar to the Guidance consistent with the APA. It neither compelled approval of particular adoptions nor held that all forms of holding or pre-eligibility matching had to be permitted. (At 16–17; note 6.)

## Exam use

Ask whether the asserted interpretation follows from the preexisting legal text, rather than assuming that every binding statement is necessarily legislative. A categorical statement might interpret a categorical underlying rule; the problem here was deriving universal new prohibitions from a context-dependent standard. Separately identify associational standing, the standards governing late jurisdictional evidence, and the appellate court's decision to reach legal merits after reversing a threshold dismissal. Do not describe a procedural vacatur as a final judicial rejection of the agency's policy objective.
