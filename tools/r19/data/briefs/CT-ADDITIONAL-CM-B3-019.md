# Armendariz v. Foundation Health Psychcare Services, Inc.
24 Cal. 4th 83 (2000) | Contracts

## Court, judgment, and question
The Supreme Court of California decided this case on August 24, 2000. Justice Mosk wrote for a five-justice majority joined by Chief Justice George and Justices Kennard, Baxter, and Werdegar. Justice Brown concurred separately, joined by Justice Chin, disagreeing with the majority’s allocation of arbitration costs. The court reversed the Court of Appeal and remanded with directions to affirm the trial court’s refusal to compel arbitration. The dispute concerned enforceability of a mandatory employment arbitration agreement; the decision did not adjudicate the employees’ discrimination or wrongful-termination claims. (24 Cal. 4th at 83, 91–93, 127–129.)

The court held that California Fair Employment and Housing Act (FEHA) claims could be arbitrated if the forum preserved the employees’ unwaivable statutory rights. But the particular agreement unlawfully restricted remedies and unjustifiably required arbitration of employee claims while leaving corresponding employer claims outside the required forum. Its defects could not be cured through permissible severance. (Id. at 99–104, 117–127.)

## Facts and agreement
Foundation Health hired Marybeth Armendariz and Dolores Olague-Rodgers in its Provider Relations Group in July and August 1995. They later became supervisors earning $38,000 annually. On June 20, 1996, the employer told them their positions were being eliminated and terminated their employment. They alleged sexually based harassment and discrimination during employment and termination because of their perceived or actual heterosexual orientation. These were allegations from the complaint and the documents supporting the motion to compel, not findings that discrimination had occurred. Footnote 1 noted that same-sex harassment had been held unlawful under FEHA. (Id. at 91–92 & n. 1.)

Both employees signed application forms containing a wrongful-termination arbitration clause and later signed separate agreements repeating it. As a condition of employment, each promised to arbitrate a contention that termination was wrongful, violated employment conditions or an express or implied term or covenant, or otherwise violated her rights. The agreement incorporated California’s arbitration statutes. It then limited the employee’s exclusive remedy to wages she would have earned between discharge and the arbitration award, excluding any other legal or equitable remedy, including reinstatement or injunctive relief. (Id. at 91–92.)

The clause therefore did more than select a forum. It limited the remedies available there. It also did not require the employer to arbitrate its own claims against the employees arising from the same termination. Although the provision said that employee and employer agreed to submit the described matter to arbitration, the described matter was the employee’s wrongful-termination claim. The agreement did not encompass all conceivable employee claims either. Its one-sided scope must be understood at that level of precision. (Id. at 120–121.)

## Procedure and opposing positions
The complaint alleged a FEHA violation and three additional tort and contract causes of action for wrongful termination, seeking general and punitive damages, injunctive relief, attorney fees, and costs. Foundation Health moved to compel arbitration under Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.2. After declarations from both sides, the trial court found an adhesion contract and substantively one-sided provisions, particularly the employee-only arbitration obligation and backpay-only remedy. It also identified a supposed discovery deficiency. Because it considered the overall arrangement unfair, it declined to strike individual terms and refused to enforce the agreement. (Id. at 92–93.)

The Court of Appeal agreed that the contract was adhesive and its damages limitation unlawful, but concluded that the remaining agreement should be enforced. It also believed incorporation of the California Arbitration Act supplied adequate discovery. On review, the employees argued both that mandatory arbitration of antidiscrimination claims was prohibited and that this agreement failed the safeguards necessary to preserve their statutory rights. They challenged discovery, review, forum costs, remedies, and mutuality. The employer maintained that arbitration was permissible, that the disputed terms could be construed or severed, and that the employees’ relatively low positions in the organization explained why employer-initiated disputes had not been included. (Id. at 93–113, 121–122.)

## Arbitrability and unwaivable rights
The court rejected the Ninth Circuit’s Duffield interpretation that the Civil Rights Act of 1991 prohibited mandatory arbitration of Title VII and equivalent state claims. Congress had encouraged arbitration where appropriate and authorized by law without express prohibitory language. Gilmer, decided before enactment, made it implausible to treat that language as silently incorporating a categorical bar based on the earlier collective-bargaining cases. Those cases involved different issues, including the tension between collective representation and individual statutory rights. The majority found no legislative prohibition of FEHA arbitration either. (Id. at 93–99 & n. 2.)

The employees separately asserted that the Federal Arbitration Act’s employment exemption covered their contracts. The court expressly declined to decide the FAA’s applicability because the California Arbitration Act, which did not contain the corresponding employment exemption, also enforced agreements subject to general contract defenses. Footnote 3 identified the then-pending Circuit City Supreme Court review; this opinion must not be presented as deciding that later federal question. Footnote 7 likewise declined to decide whether the wording sufficiently notified the employees that statutory claims were covered, an issue outside the matters accepted for review. Footnote 6 preserved resort to the state enforcement agency and that agency’s independent statutory functions. (Id. at 97–99 & nn. 3, 5–7.)

Arbitration could change the forum without eliminating FEHA’s substantive protections. Civil Code sections 1668 and 3513 prohibited private arrangements exempting parties from violations of law or overriding laws established for a public reason. FEHA’s antidiscrimination protections served the public, so an employer could not use arbitration to obtain a waiver of those protections. Drawing on Cole, the court endorsed safeguards addressing neutral arbitrators, adequate discovery, a written award, all relief available in court, and access without improper arbitration costs. The neutral-arbitrator requirement was not disputed here. Footnote 8 confined the general endorsement to mandatory employment arbitration, distinguishing genuinely voluntary postdispute bargaining over procedural tradeoffs. (Id. at 99–104 & nn. 8–9.)

## Remedies, discovery, and review
The backpay-only clause was unlawful because it excluded statutory remedies, including punitive damages and attorney fees. The employer argued that the limitation applied only to contract claims because one sentence referred to employment terms and covenants. The court rejected that construction: the following exclusion of every other remedy made the limitation comprehensive. (Id. at 103–104.)

The discovery discussion did not supply another incurable defect. Employees needed access to essential documents and witnesses to vindicate statutory claims, but the court did not decide whether FEHA fell within the personal-injury provision automatically incorporating the statute’s full civil discovery machinery. Instead, agreement to arbitrate FEHA claims implicitly included discovery sufficient to vindicate them. The arbitrator, subject to limited review, could reconcile that need with arbitration’s intended simplicity. Thus the present employer had impliedly consented to adequate discovery, and lack of express discovery language did not render these claims inarbitrable. (Id. at 104–106 & nn. 10–11.)

Similarly, the arbitrator had to provide a written decision revealing the essential findings and conclusions sufficiently for judicial review. The agreement did not prohibit such a decision and was interpreted to require it for FEHA claims. The court was reviewing a motion to compel, not an award, and expressly reserved the precise standard of review necessary to protect statutory rights. Its ruling required a usable written decision; it did not announce general appellate review of every arbitral legal error. (Id. at 106–107.)

## Costs and Brown’s concurrence
The majority held that mandatory employment arbitration could not generally impose expenses employees would not have incurred by bringing their claims in court. The employer had to bear costs unique to arbitration, such as forum and arbitrator expenses. The risk of substantial forum costs could deter the assertion of statutory rights even if a reviewing court might cancel those costs after arbitration. The issue therefore should be addressed when compelling arbitration rather than deferred until award review. (Id. at 107–113.)

The majority rejected a case-by-case comparative-cost mini-trial as burdensome and speculative. Nor did employer payment itself necessarily destroy arbitrator neutrality: the court distinguished payment from the repeat-player concerns already associated with the system and identified institutional safeguards. The statutory pro rata cost rule was a default, displaced by the implied undertaking to preserve unwaivable FEHA rights. Consequently, the agreement’s silence about costs was construed to require employer payment and was not itself grounds to deny enforcement. The rule did not shift every ordinary litigation expense or attorney fee to the employer. (Id. at 110–113.)

Brown, joined by Chin, disagreed with this bright-line allocation. Arbitration might remain less expensive overall, some employees could afford a share, and the actual costs varied. Unless the agreement required advance payment or a specified employee share, she would leave apportionment to the arbitrator, considering unique costs, employee means, and overall comparative expense. Judicial review would then guard against costs that would have deterred effective vindication. She concurred in the judgment and most of the reasoning; there was no dissent from the disposition. (Id. at 127–129.)

## Unconscionability and mutuality
The court separately examined ordinary unconscionability principles applicable to mandatory employment arbitration regardless of whether a particular claim was statutory. Procedural unconscionability concerned oppression or surprise; substantive unconscionability concerned overly harsh or one-sided terms. Both were required, operating on a sliding scale rather than in equal degrees. Adhesion began the inquiry but did not alone establish unenforceability. Here the agreement was imposed as an employment condition without a negotiating opportunity, satisfying the procedural concern. (Id. at 113–116.)

The substantive problem was unjustified asymmetry. A stronger party could not force its weaker counterpart into arbitration for claims arising from a transaction while reserving corresponding claims for litigation merely to maximize its own advantage. Reasonable business realities could justify a particular asymmetry, but the employer had to establish them. The court distinguished this doctrine from lack of consideration: a nonmutual arbitration clause might have consideration and still be unconscionable. It also defended the doctrine as an application of general contract law rather than a categorical hostility to arbitration. (Id. at 116–120.)

The court illustrated the defect with a termination for alleged trade-secret theft: the employee’s wrongful-termination claim would be arbitrated while the employer’s trade-secret claim could be litigated. The employer’s assertion that these supervisors were unlikely to possess proprietary information or generate employer claims did not justify categorical exemption of even rare corresponding claims. Moreover, backpay through the award excluded front pay, an ordinary component of wrongful-termination contract damages, while imposing no comparable limit on the employer. Removing only the FEHA damages restriction would not solve that imbalance. (Id. at 120–121.)

## Severance and exact result
Civil Code section 1670.5 allowed refusal of an unconscionable agreement, severance of offending clauses, or restriction of their application. The court read that authority alongside illegal-contract principles: preserve lawful contractual purposes where the illegality was collateral and curable, but do not enforce a transaction permeated by an unlawful purpose. The interests of justice governed the choice. (Id. at 121–124.)

Two features supported refusal of the whole arbitration arrangement. The unlawful remedies restriction and unjustifiably one-sided forum obligation together indicated a systematic inferior forum favoring the employer. More importantly, the lack of mutuality could not be cured simply by deleting a provision: the court would have to add an employer obligation to arbitrate. Section 1670.5 did not authorize that augmentation. The employer’s later willingness to accept mutual arbitration and full remedies was at most an unaccepted modification offer, not a retroactive cure. Cases replacing a biased arbitrator rested on a distinct statutory appointment power; cases deleting post-award escape clauses involved removable terms and circumstances where nonenforcement would reward the party that imposed them. (Id. at 124–127.)

Footnote 13 reserved whether the damages restriction alone would justify rejecting the whole agreement and whether the clause had been drafted in bad faith given the law when it was signed. The court reversed the Court of Appeal and remanded to that court with directions to affirm the trial court’s judgment. It did not impose damages, decide the discrimination allegations, or require the parties to arbitrate under a judicially rewritten contract. (Id. at 124–125 & n. 13, 127.)
