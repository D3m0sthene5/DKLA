# Allstate Insurance Co. v. Charneski

**286 F.2d 238 (7th Cir. 1960) — United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit, December 19, 1960.** Chief Judge Hastings wrote for a panel with Judges Enoch and Castle; no dissent or separate writing is reported. The assigned account is CivPro, physical PDF page 254, printed pages 468–469, Note 4 following Byrd.

## Facts and the district-court judgment

Allstate, an Illinois insurer, brought a diversity action in the Western District of Wisconsin for a declaration that it did not cover a 1950 Ford driven by Leonard Charneski when it collided with LaVerne Gehrt's automobile. Leonard's brother Edwin claimed ownership of the Ford. Allstate joined the brothers, passengers in Gehrt's automobile, and that automobile's insurer as defendants. The opinion does not decide the accident participants' negligence or damages. (238–239.)

The defendants moved to dismiss for failure to state a claim; the district court denied the motion. Their answers disputed ownership of the Ford, and a jury found that Leonard owned it when the collision occurred. The court entered the requested declaration of no coverage. After denial of a motion to alter or amend the judgment or obtain a new trial, the defendants appealed. They challenged the availability of this separate declaratory action, the evidence of ownership, and the trial judge's comments and instructions. The Seventh Circuit decided the first issue and did not reach the others. (239, 244–245.)

## Wisconsin's particular substantive policy

The parties conceded that Allstate could not maintain this independent action in a Wisconsin state court. Wisconsin did have a general declaratory-judgment statute; the obstacle was a specific rule implementing its direct-action insurance policy. Under Wisconsin law, an injured person could proceed directly against the insurer, and the insurer could present both negligence and policy-coverage defenses in that action. (239–240 & n.1.)

New Amsterdam Casualty Co. v. Simpson had held that an insurer could not isolate its coverage defense for advance adjudication through a separate declaratory suit. An injured person was a direct adversary of the insurer, rather than someone merely seeking an asset belonging to the insured. An advance coverage declaration could leave negligence unresolved, cause repeated litigation between the same parties, and delay complete disposition of the injury claim. The State's policy favored resolving the insurer's, insured's, and injured person's connected rights in one proceeding. (239–240.)

## Issue and holding

Could the insurer avoid Wisconsin's restriction by invoking the federal Declaratory Judgments Act in a diversity case? The Seventh Circuit held that the district court should have dismissed the complaint. Erie required consideration of the specific state substantive policy, and the federal interest in an optional declaratory remedy did not outweigh it. The holding did not abolish federal declaratory relief generally or establish that every state procedural preference controls diversity litigation. (240, 243–245.)

## The court's development of the Erie problem

The court reviewed Erie and subsequent decisions because simply labeling declaratory relief “procedural” would not resolve the conflict. Erie discouraged differences attributable merely to the choice between neighboring state and federal courts. Klaxon extended that concern to state choice-of-law rules. York explained why conventional labels of substance and procedure could fail to identify whether disregarding a state rule would materially alter enforcement of a state-created right. The court also discussed Ragan, Angel, and Woods as instances in which federal diversity courts could not simply provide access or relief the State withheld. (240–243.)

Those discussions are the panel's historical explanation of the doctrine as it stood in 1960. They are not a certification that every quoted formulation remains controlling without qualification. Footnote 2 also distinguished the possible federal common law applicable in federal-question cases from Erie's prohibition on general federal common law governing ordinary diversity rights. (240 n.2.)

The court then considered Byrd's recognition of an important federal interest in allocating functions between judge and jury. It did not use outcome determination as an automatic answer. Although different access to declaratory relief would encourage forum shopping, the court believed the proper inquiry also examined the competing state and federal policy goals. (243–244.)

## Applying the competing interests

On Wisconsin's side, the restriction was not a lack of any state declaratory remedy. It was an authoritative implementation of the substantive direct-action scheme. Allowing the insurer to separate one coverage defense into a federal lawsuit would weaken the State's effort to determine all relevant rights in one action. The policy was concrete and connected to the underlying insurance and injury relationships. (244.)

The federal interests were comparatively slight. Federal courts had a general interest in their own procedure, and Congress had supplied declaratory relief. But the federal Act made the remedy discretionary: the court could grant a declaration, not necessarily must grant one. Nothing comparable to the distinctive federal judge–jury allocation concern in Byrd demanded this separate coverage action. The panel's observation that no Seventh Amendment right was involved in the conflict does not mean no jury had been used below; the coverage issue had actually gone to a jury. Its point concerned the absence of an overriding jury-related federal policy requiring this separate action. (239, 244.)

The underlying accident, policy coverage, and affected parties' rights were connected to Wisconsin law. Apart from providing a fair diversity forum, the Federal Government had no comparable substantive connection identified by the court. Permissive declaratory relief need not be furnished when doing so would create unnecessary conflict with the specific state policy. (244.)

## Allstate's contrary precedents

The court distinguished Tillman because the negligence and policy-defense issues there were litigated together in a single action, consistent with Wisconsin's objective. An earlier Allstate decision, Moldenhauer, had affirmed declaratory relief in a similar setting, but its opinion did not indicate that the Erie question had been presented. Chase National Bank concerned the validity of declaratory pleadings accompanied by later coercive relief; it did not identify a comparable specific state substantive policy or discuss Erie. The panel therefore did not treat those authorities as resolving the present conflict. (244.)

## Exact judgment and significance

The Seventh Circuit **reversed and remanded with directions to sustain the defendants' motion to dismiss the complaint**. It did not uphold the ownership verdict, determine coverage anew, or resolve the alleged trial errors. (244–245.) The assignment's quotation accurately captures the balance the panel struck. The lesson is that a federal procedural vehicle may interact with a specific state-created arrangement of rights; calling the vehicle procedural does not finish the analysis.
