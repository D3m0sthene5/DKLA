# Kauders v. Uber Technologies, Inc.
486 Mass. 557, 159 N.E.3d 1033 (2021) | Contracts

## Decision and assigned setting
The Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court held that Uber's rider registration interface did not create an enforceable agreement with Christopher and Hannah Kauders. Neither reasonable notice of the extensive terms nor a reasonable manifestation of assent existed. The dispute therefore was not arbitrable, and the case was remanded for further proceedings. Justice Kafker wrote the court's opinion; no separate writing appears. The listed participating justices were Lenk, Gaziano, Lowy, Budd, Cypher, and Kafker. Lenk participated in deliberations before retirement. 486 Mass. at 557 n.3, 581.

The assigned excerpt occupies printed pages 304–314, crossing the C&M 1–350 scan, PDF pages 349–350, and the 351–700 scan, PDF pages 1–9. Its online formation discussion is central, but the complete opinion also contains an important arbitration review problem largely omitted from the excerpt. The court found that the trial judge had used the wrong postaward procedure, then reached the preserved formation question itself rather than requiring a pointless round trip through confirmation and another appeal.

## Facts and chronology
Christopher Kauders was blind and used a guide dog. He and Hannah sued Uber Technologies and its wholly owned subsidiary Rasier, alleging that three Uber drivers refused Christopher rides because of his disability and guide dog. The appellate formation ruling did not itself adjudicate those discrimination claims. In the earlier compelled arbitration, the arbitrator found discriminatory acts by drivers but ruled for Uber on the ground that they were independent contractors rather than employees. The Supreme Judicial Court recited undisputed registration facts drawn from the complaint and motion filings. 486 Mass. at 558–559, 563–564.

Christopher registered on June 27, 2014; Hannah registered around October 2015. Christopher's mobile registration involved three dark-background screens. The first, CREATE AN ACCOUNT, requested an email address, telephone number, and password and explained that these would be used for ride confirmations and receipts. The second, CREATE A PROFILE, requested a name and optional photograph to help drivers identify the rider. Each used a NEXT button at the upper right. Nothing yet directed the user to contractual terms. Id. at 559–561.

The third screen, LINK PAYMENT, focused on payment information. A white credit-card field appeared at the top; scan-your-card and promo-code options appeared below it; a prominent PayPal button occupied the middle. At the bottom was a sentence stating that creating an account meant agreeing to the Terms & Conditions and Privacy Policy. The introductory words explaining the legal significance of account creation were less prominent than the linked terms. Clicking the link opened another screen with further buttons; accessing the terms required another click. Entering a credit-card number caused a keypad to appear and moved the notice toward the middle. The final button said DONE, rather than I agree, and did not expressly identify its click as acceptance of contractual terms. Id. at 560–561.

The terms were much broader than arrangements for an individual ride. They purported to permit amendments without notice, impose broad liability and warranty disclaimers, require indemnification, permit extensive collection or disclosure of user information, and govern user conduct. An arbitration clause near the end covered disputes relating to the agreement and tied recoverable damages to the liability limitation. Uber actually counterclaimed in arbitration for the costs of the Kauderses' court litigation, invoking indemnification. The court described these terms to explain why ordinary riders needed meaningful notice of the relationship they were entering; it did not decide the enforceability of every individual provision. Id. at 561–563 & nn.9–13.

## Procedural history and the parties' arguments
The plaintiffs filed in Superior Court in July 2016. Uber moved to compel arbitration in June 2017, relying in part on the federal district court's Cullinane decision. The plaintiffs objected that no agreement had formed, among other defenses. The judge compelled arbitration without analyzing formation and retained jurisdiction over the eventual award. Arbitration ended with an award for Uber on June 4, 2018. Three weeks later, the First Circuit reversed the district court in Cullinane, concluding that the same registration process did not provide adequate notice under Massachusetts law. The plaintiffs did not immediately bring that decision to the judge's attention or move to vacate the award. Id. at 563–565 & nn.14–15.

Uber moved to confirm in September. At an October hearing, the plaintiffs invoked Cullinane and renewed their objections. At the judge's invitation, they moved for reconsideration in November. On January 2, 2019, over six months after the award, the judge vacated his original order, denied arbitration, and denied confirmation because no contract existed. Uber appealed, arguing that the mandatory confirmation statute left the judge no choice after the thirty-day challenge period; that no changed law or fact justified reconsideration; and that registration nevertheless formed a contract. The Supreme Judicial Court transferred the appeal to itself. Id. at 564–566 & n.17.

## Arbitration review: preservation differs from trial-court reconsideration
The court reviewed statutory interpretation de novo. Under the Massachusetts Arbitration Act, an order denying arbitration is immediately appealable, while an order compelling it is not. A resisting party can preserve its objection and obtain appellate review after confirmation of the eventual award. That structure promotes uninterrupted arbitration after the initial judicial decision without eliminating review of consent. Id. at 566–568.

The thirty-day procedures for vacating, modifying, or correcting an award principally address defects in arbitration or the award itself. Section 12(a)(5) permits vacatur for lack of agreement only when the issue was not already decided adversely in the proceedings to compel and the party did not participate without objection. Here it had been decided adversely. The plaintiffs could not relitigate formation under that provision, and their participation pursuant to the order did not erase the objection they had preserved. Their route was appeal following confirmation. Id. at 567–568.

Uber was therefore correct that the trial judge should have confirmed the award. The court rejected its broader argument that a judge can reconsider an interlocutory ruling only after changed facts or law: judges ordinarily retain authority to correct errors before final judgment. But after completed arbitration and expiration of the award challenge period, the mandatory statutory scheme constrained that authority. Allowing reconsideration in these circumstances was an abuse of discretion. Id. at 568–570.

That procedural error did not make the original formation ruling correct. A remand simply to confirm, followed by the plaintiffs' inevitable appeal, would return the same fully briefed issue to the same court. For judicial economy the court decided the preserved formation question immediately. This explains why the ultimate result favored the plaintiffs even though Uber prevailed on its objection to the trial judge's procedural route. Id. at 570.

## Formation rule and application
Arbitration policy cannot supply missing consent. Ordinary, generally applicable state contract principles determine whether a contract exists. The court adopted two requirements for online agreements: reasonable notice of the terms and a reasonable manifestation of assent. Uber, as the party enforcing the alleged contract, bore the burden on both. Unlike certain other jurisdictions' formulations, the court did not impose a separate requirement that notice be conspicuous. Id. at 571–572 & nn.24–25.

Actual notice satisfies the first requirement. Without it, the court examines the totality of circumstances: the kind and size of transaction, whether the interface looks contractual, the placement and presentation of terms, the steps needed to access them, and clarity about the consequences of the user's action. Express affirmative agreement, such as an I agree button, is a strong and easily implemented signal of assent. It is not categorically indispensable, but an alternative action must reasonably communicate acceptance. Id. at 572–575.

Both requirements failed here. Registration described practical arrangements for future small ride transactions; it did not naturally alert users to sweeping liability, data, indemnity, and arbitration obligations. The interface steered attention toward the upper payment field and prominent payment alternatives. Other equally prominent or larger features diminished the terms link's ability to attract attention. Uber's assertion that the notice occupied the middle of the screen ignored that this happened only when a particular payment field activated the keypad. For other options, the limited record did not establish that the notice would be meaningfully presented. The court did not convert its uncertainty about those screens into a factual finding that every possible payment path hid the link. Id. at 575–579.

Assent also remained obscure. DONE signaled completion of payment information; no adjacent instruction clearly stated that this click accepted the terms. The two-part sentence highlighted the terms' label while downplaying the words explaining legal acceptance. A user could complete registration without opening or acknowledging the terms. By comparison, Uber's driver interfaces described in other cases required affirmative acceptance and a second confirmation, showing that clear assent was technically achievable. This comparison supported the interpretation of the rider process; it did not apply drivers' contracts to riders. Id. at 579–581.

## Result, limits, and checked sources
The court concluded that no enforceable agreement existed and that the dispute was not arbitrable, then remanded for proceedings consistent with its opinion. It neither awarded discrimination damages nor resolved Uber's ultimate liability for the drivers. The holding concerns the interface and record before the court, not a universal prohibition of online terms or arbitration. The objective notice analysis also should not be recast as a disability-specific accessibility ruling simply because Christopher was blind.
