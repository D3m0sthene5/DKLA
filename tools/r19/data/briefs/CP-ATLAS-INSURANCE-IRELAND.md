# Insurance Corporation of Ireland, Ltd. v. Compagnie des Bauxites de Guinee

**456 U.S. 694 (1982) · Supreme Court of the United States · June 1, 1982**

## Insurance for a Guinean operation arranged through Pennsylvania

Compagnie des Bauxites de Guinee operated bauxite mines and processing facilities in Guinea but was incorporated in Delaware. Guinea owned 49 percent; Halco Mining owned 51 percent. Halco operated in Pennsylvania and provided administrative services, including insurance procurement. In 1973 it directed a broker to obtain $20 million in business-interruption coverage. Insurance Company of North America supplied the first $10 million, while 21 foreign insurers subscribed to the excess layer through the London insurance market. Fourteen of those excess insurers were the Supreme Court petitioners. 456 U.S. at 696–697 & nn.2–4.

The insurers initialed placing slips effective February 12, 1974, indicating their respective shares. A cover note followed; they issued no separate policy but adopted INA’s policy insofar as applicable. CBG allegedly later suffered mechanical problems causing a business-interruption loss exceeding $10 million. The insurers refused indemnification. In December 1975, CBG sued in the Western District of Pennsylvania, invoking diversity jurisdiction and pleading separate counts against INA and the excess insurers. INA did not dispute jurisdiction, but the foreign insurers challenged personal jurisdiction. The insurance coverage and loss remained disputed; the Supreme Court addressed jurisdictional discovery rather than liability under the policies. Id. at 697–698 & n.5.

## Repeated discovery orders and an expressly warned sanction

CBG sought insurance policies to establish the foreign insurers’ Pennsylvania business contacts. The Court’s initial factual account dates the first request to August 1976. Insurers objected to burden, then claimed that relevant documents were controlled by London brokers. CBG narrowed its request to policies delivered in Pennsylvania or covering Pennsylvania risks. The court overruled objections, directed the insurers to request the information from brokers, and repeatedly extended the production deadline. An offer to make approximately four million files available for inspection in London did not satisfy the ordered production. Id. at 698–699.

In December 1978, the district judge found that no conscientious production effort had occurred and expressly warned that continued noncompliance would lead to a Rule 37 sanction establishing personal jurisdiction. The judge also invited statistics or other information showing that jurisdiction was absent. When the requested material still did not arrive, the court imposed the sanction in April 1979: for this litigation, the insurers’ business contacts would establish personal jurisdiction. Independently, it found actual contacts sufficient under Pennsylvania law and inferred submission to jurisdiction from adoption of the INA contract. Id. at 699, 707–708 & n.12.

The Third Circuit affirmed for the petitioning insurers solely on the sanction ground. It reversed as to three other insurers who had complied and lacked sufficient contacts, and it reversed an injunction against the parallel English litigation. Those additional rulings were not decided by the Supreme Court’s limited grant here. The Court granted the insurers’ cross-petition specifically to review the Rule 37 sanction, against a conflict with the Fifth Circuit. Id. at 696 n.1, 697 n.3, 700–701 & nn.6–8.

## Issue, holding, judgment, and vote

Could a court take jurisdictional facts as established under Rule 37 when insurers resisting personal jurisdiction disobeyed orders seeking those facts? And was that sanction properly imposed in this case?

The Court answered both questions affirmatively. A properly related and just discovery sanction could establish the facts supporting personal jurisdiction without violating due process. On this record, the district court did not abuse its discretion. The Supreme Court **affirmed the Third Circuit’s judgment**; it did not enter an insurance-damages judgment or decide the distinct English-litigation injunction issue. Id. at 705–709.

Justice White wrote for eight Justices: Burger, Brennan, Marshall, Blackmun, Rehnquist, Stevens, and O’Connor joined. Justice Powell concurred only in the judgment, favoring a narrower factual basis and objecting to the majority’s theoretical explanation. The result was unanimous; the complete reasoning was not. Id. at 695, 709–716.

## Why personal jurisdiction differs from subject-matter jurisdiction

The insurers argued that a court lacking jurisdiction could not create it by assertion and that they owed no obedience to its orders until personal jurisdiction had been independently proven. The majority thought this argument conflated two distinct kinds of jurisdiction. Federal subject-matter jurisdiction limits judicial power under Article III and congressional statutes. Parties cannot create it by agreement, waive its absence through silence, or ordinarily prevent a court from examining it on its own initiative. Footnote 9 nevertheless recognizes judgment-preclusion limits when parties had already had an opportunity to litigate a jurisdictional determination. Id. at 701–702 & n.9.

Personal jurisdiction instead protects an individual liberty interest through due process. It can be waived or affected by a defendant’s procedural conduct. The Court cited contractual submissions, stipulations, participation in certain proceedings, and failure to raise a timely Rule 12 objection as examples. Those examples illustrated why legal consequences of a party’s conduct need not amount to an arbitrary assertion of judicial power. Id. at 702–705.

Footnote 10 is especially important to the assigned account. The majority treated the federalism aspect of earlier state-jurisdiction cases as ultimately serving the individual liberty protected by due process, rather than as an independent sovereign limit that no individual could waive. It expressly denied abolishing minimum contacts: the holding concerned how the facts demonstrating those contacts could be established after noncompliance with discovery. Powell disputed the broader implications of that explanation. Id. at 702–703 n.10, 713–714.

## The evidentiary connection that made the sanction permissible

The Court distinguished a sanction grounded in withheld evidence from punishment unrelated to the issue being determined. Under *Hammond Packing*, refusal to produce material proof could support a presumption that the asserted defense lacked merit. By contrast, *Hovey* rejected striking a defense simply to punish disobedience of an unrelated money-deposit order. Rule 37’s proper application implements the evidentiary presumption, rather than merely depriving a party of a hearing for being disobedient. Its historical Advisory Committee support appears in footnote 11. Id. at 705–706 & n.11.

A defendant submitting the jurisdictional objection for determination agrees to abide by that determination and the procedural rules used to reach it. The limited appearance does not automatically admit the underlying contacts or waive every objection; the court may determine the contested issue through valid factual presumptions as well as direct proof. The majority contrasted that course with declining to participate, risking default, and later contesting jurisdiction collaterally. Once jurisdiction has been litigated and decided, preclusion limits repeating the issue. Id. at 706–707.

## Why this particular sanction was just and appropriately limited

The applicable Rule required both justice and a specific relationship between the sanction and the claim addressed by the discovery order. The Supreme Court reviewed abuse of discretion rather than deciding whether it personally would have selected the same sanction. Id. at 707.

Several circumstances supported the district court’s choice. The insurers delayed despite repeated orders, promised compliance without delivering it, received ample warning, and declined opportunities to provide contrary information. CBG’s jurisdictional position was substantial rather than frivolous; the district court had independently found contacts sufficient under Pennsylvania’s statute. The judge’s invitation for countervailing statistics effectively placed a burden on the insurers that their conduct did not meet. Id. at 707–708 & n.12.

The factual relationship was equally direct. CBG needed the discovery to establish the extent of the insurers’ Pennsylvania contacts—the same facts relevant to the defense they had raised. Their refusal prevented that proof, and the sanction established those contacts. The resulting legal consequence, personal jurisdiction, did not make the evidentiary inference unrelated or impermissible. This reasoning does not authorize a court to invent unrelated contacts as punishment for any discovery failure. Id. at 708–709.

## Powell’s narrower concurrence

Powell emphasized that a diversity court’s personal jurisdiction depended on the forum State’s long-arm statute and applicable constitutional limits. Rule 37 was not a separate jurisdictional grant, and Rule 82 warned against interpreting procedural rules to extend district-court jurisdiction. His notes addressed the injunction’s jurisdictional prerequisite, the district court’s independent contact finding, the significance of forum-state law, and the service rules. He worried that the majority’s individual-right account could either loosen the minimum-contacts requirement for an unconsenting defendant or imply a new federal jurisdictional basis neither the Rule’s text nor its history supplied. Id. at 709–715 & concurrence nn.1–7.

Those concerns did not require a different outcome here. CBG had made a prima facie showing of minimum contacts, and the district court considered its proof sufficient independently of the sanction. Powell would permit discovery on that basis and treat the unrebutted showing as sufficient when insurers refused compliance. He warned against speculative foreign fishing expeditions, while concluding that the actual evidence justified these orders and this result. The Third Circuit had not reviewed the district court’s alternative contact holding, as his eighth note clarifies. Id. at 715–716 & concurrence n.8.
