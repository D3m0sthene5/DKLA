# Livesey v. Copps Corporation
90 Wis. 2d 577, 280 N.W.2d 339 (Ct. App. 1979) | Contracts

## Court, procedure, and result
Wisconsin’s Court of Appeals decided Livesey on May 29, 1979. Presiding Judge Gartzke wrote for a panel also including Judges Bablitch and Dykman; no separate opinion is reported. John Livesey and Dwight Ziegler, doing business as Livesey Enterprises, sought specific performance of an option to purchase Copps Corporation’s real estate. On undisputed affidavit evidence, the Portage County circuit court denied the buyers’ summary-judgment motion, granted Copps’s motion, and dismissed the complaint. The appellate court affirmed because the exercise notice arrived after the option expired. The reporter also records denial of a petition for review, without reproducing a further opinion. (577–580, 584.)

## Transaction and the one-day delay
In 1975 the parties negotiated development of a shopping center on Copps’s land. Their attorneys exchanged proposed and revised agreements by mail, but the initial four documents were executed in Stevens Point as of October 31, 1975. Copps conveyed one parcel to Livesey, Livesey leased it back to Copps, and Copps granted Livesey an option on the adjoining parcel. The option ran for twelve months from signing and required written notice to Copps during its term. (579.)

The parties extended the option in 1976. Livesey retyped the original option, retaining that term and notice language while changing another provision, and mailed the revision to Copps. Copps returned executed copies by mail; the new option was signed November 15, 1976. Livesey mailed an exercise notice by certified mail on November 15, 1977. Copps received it on November 16 and refused to convey. The dispute thus required both a calculation of the expiration date and a decision about whether mailing or receipt completed exercise. (579–580.)

## Expiration at midnight November 15
The court distinguished Wisconsin’s statutory computation provisions from the binding common-law decisions governing the agreement. The common-law rule excluded the first day when time was measured from a specified date, but included it when measured from an event. The Court of Appeals regarded itself as bound by the state supreme court’s distinction and examined precedents applying both formulations. (580–581.)

This option measured its twelve-month term from the date of signing. The specified-date rule therefore excluded November 15, 1976 and included the corresponding final day. The option expired at midnight November 15, 1977, rather than a day earlier. Mailing on November 15 was consequently within the term; receipt on November 16 was not. The buyers lost on the notice rule, not on an erroneous assumption that November 15 itself was too late to exercise. (581–582.)

## Permitted use of mail versus effective notice
Time was ordinarily essential to an option, whose holder had to accept within its term and according to its conditions. The court nevertheless recognized an option as a continuing offer and acknowledged that communication of an offer by mail can imply permission to accept by mail. Because Copps had returned the revised option by mail, the court stated that mailed exercise could be effective on dispatch unless the option’s own terms required otherwise. It did not initially treat every option as categorically outside all postal-acceptance principles. (582.)

The specific requirement of giving written notice to Copps during the option term supplied that qualification. Wisconsin notice law generally required actual receipt absent custom, statute, estoppel, or an express contractual stipulation to the contrary. The court also relied on Corbin’s explanation that exercise notice can operate as a condition of an existing contractual duty, as well as acceptance of a continuing offer. It adopted the majority view that option-exercise notice must arrive unless the parties agree otherwise. The buyers’ California authority was distinguishable because California had codified a dispatch rule; Wisconsin had no comparable statute. (583–584.)

Another document in the same transaction reinforced this interpretation. The lease-back expressly deemed notice delivered when registered or certified and deposited in the mail. The option lacked that stipulation. The trial court inferred that mail was not the intended exercise method, but the appellate court’s stated agreement focused on effectiveness: when these parties wanted notice to take effect on mailing, they expressly said so. That comparison supported enforcing receipt during the option term without confusing permission to use mail with the moment notice became effective. (584.)

## Application and assigned account
The November 16 receipt failed the condition although dispatch occurred on the last permissible day. Copps therefore had no obligation under the unexercised option to convey, and the judgment dismissing the specific-performance action was affirmed. The court did not award damages or order forfeiture of a stated deposit. (582, 584.)
