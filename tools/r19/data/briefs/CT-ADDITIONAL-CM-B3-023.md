# Bolter v. Superior Court of Orange County
87 Cal. App. 4th 900, 104 Cal. Rptr. 2d 888 (2001) | Contracts

## Court, amended stage, and result
The California Court of Appeal, Fourth District, Division Three, granted the franchisees’ petition for a writ of mandate. Justice O’Leary wrote; Acting Presiding Justice Crosby and Justice Bedsworth concurred without separate writings. The original decision was March 9, 2001; rehearing was denied March 30 and the opinion modified to the reported text. The court directed the superior court to vacate its judgment and enter a new order striking the provisions requiring arbitration in Utah. It dissolved its prior stay and awarded petitioners appellate costs. It preserved an otherwise valid arbitration agreement rather than voiding arbitration altogether. Supreme Court review was denied June 13, 2001, with Justice Baxter not participating in that later proceeding. (87 Cal. App. 4th at 900, 902, 910–911.)

The issue was whether distant arbitration requirements imposed on established, financially constrained California franchisees were unconscionable and, if so, severable. The parties also disputed a California statute prohibiting out-of-state franchise venue and its possible FAA preemption. The court expressly avoided that statutory dispute and decided under generally applicable contract principles. (Id. at 906–911.)

## Businesses, evolving contracts, and underlying allegations
Florence Bolter, Sandra Valdez, and Stephen Knight purchased Chem-Dry carpet-cleaning franchises in the early 1980s and operated in Orange County. Harris Research was the franchisor. The franchisees periodically signed renewals or replacement agreements. Initially Harris was headquartered in California and the agreements contained no arbitration provision. Harris later moved to Utah and required dispute proceedings there. The franchisees claimed that increasingly restrictive terms were imposed under threats of franchise termination. (Id. at 902–903, 907, 909.)

Their April 1998 complaint sought damages and declaratory relief for contract and good-faith breaches. They challenged, among other things, surrender of customer lists and telephone numbers upon termination, access to tax returns, selective enforcement of advertising restrictions, saturation of franchise territories, and an obligation to buy an allegedly overpriced water-extraction system and accessories. The original report calls that system Velda; the CAP transcription misreads its first letter. Their initial cleaning method had used a chemical applied and lightly brushed onto the carpet, allowed to dry, and vacuumed away, rather than bulky water-extraction equipment. These underlying grievances explain the suit but were not finally adjudicated by the writ decision. (Id. at 902–903 & n. 1.)

## Procedural path and arbitration objections
Harris commenced separate Utah arbitrations and sought removal of the lawsuit to federal court. That court issued an order to show cause concerning whether Valdez’s claim should be severed and dismissed for arbitration in Salt Lake City and whether the other franchisees were similarly bound. Harris sought a litigation stay. Counsel objected to the AAA that Business and Professions Code section 20040.5 prohibited out-of-state franchise venue. The franchisees then dismissed their federal action and filed an identical state complaint adding A.Y.S., Inc. (Id. at 903–904.)

The AAA held a May 1998 telephone conference on locale and circulated a list of twenty proposed arbitrators. Counsel reiterated the statutory objection and explicitly disclaimed consent or waiver by participating in selection. After another objection that courts should decide arbitrability, the AAA arranged hearings before three appointed arbitrators. The franchisees declined to participate, and the arbitrators found the disputes arbitrable. Those were arbitrability rulings, not merits awards resolving the contractual grievances. (Id. at 904.)

On March 2, 1999, Judge Firmat denied Harris’s requested litigation stay and temporarily stopped arbitration pending a hearing on who should decide arbitrability, whether the AAA rulings bound the parties, adhesion, and unconscionability. Judge Watson later vacated the scheduled hearings and trial date and granted a motion to vacate A.Y.S.’s dismissal. Harris moved to confirm the arbitrators’ rulings, compel completion of arbitration, and stay litigation, asserting that the rulings were preclusive and the time to challenge them had expired. The court initially granted that relief. (Id. at 904–905.)

An earlier appellate alternative writ required an independent judicial determination of arbitrability. The trial court vacated its confirmation order and set a February 2, 2000 hearing, rendering that writ moot. After accepting additional declarations and documents but refusing a requested evidentiary hearing, the court ordered arbitration in Utah and dismissed the case on April 18. It signed findings prepared by Harris on May 1 stating that the provisions, even if adhesive, were neither unconscionable nor unreasonably burdensome. The franchisees filed this further writ petition, and the Court of Appeal temporarily stayed arbitration. The ultimate judgment addressed that later ruling, not the already-vacated confirmation order. (Id. at 905–906.)

## Agreement and general contract standard
The clause required disputes to be submitted to the Salt Lake City AAA office, heard there by one arbitrator under commercial rules. It selected the FAA for arbitration matters and Utah law for the franchise agreement. It excluded exemplary or punitive damages, required individual rather than class proceedings, and barred consolidation with other arbitrations. Harris alone retained an election to litigate claims relating to its marks; footnote 2 specifically noted this exception. (Id. at 908–909 & n. 2.)

The court rejected the premise that arbitration enjoyed immunity from ordinary contract defenses. Both federal and California arbitration statutes preserved grounds applicable to contracts generally. Civil Code section 1670.5 permitted rejection or limitation of unconscionable terms. Procedural and substantive unconscionability both had to be present, but on a sliding scale. Evaluation required the commercial setting, purpose, and effect, not just inspection of contract wording. Adhesion alone did not make arbitration unconscionable. (Id. at 906–908.)

Here Harris did not refute its characterization as a wealthy international franchisor, and the franchisees’ limited means were undisputed. Existing owners had to accept the newer terms to continue their businesses. Their position differed from a prospective purchaser able to reject a franchise at the outset: they had already committed to ongoing small operations and could not realistically negotiate the new place and manner requirements. That supplied the procedural concern. (Id. at 907.)

## Burden of the Utah forum
The substantive assessment rested on concrete business circumstances. Valdez personally handled advertising, administration, sales, customer calls, and scheduling from home. Knight employed cleaners but personally administered three franchises and feared losing business if forced to leave California. Bolter was sixty-six, solely responsible for running her franchises, and cared for a severely ill husband who could not manage without her. All described serious financial strain; Bolter had recently declared bankruptcy, and Valdez said a franchise bought for $32,500 might sell for only $10,000–$15,000. These were declarations concerning access and burden, not damages findings awarding lost franchise value. (Id. at 909–910.)

Utah proceedings would require travel and lodging, interruption of daily operations, and additional expense for counsel familiar with Utah law. The consolidation prohibition prevented franchisees from sharing those increased costs; the damages restriction limited possible recovery. In that setting the court regarded the place-and-manner arrangement as oppressive, with no justification beyond maximizing Harris’s advantage and potentially preventing claims. The opinion did not declare every out-of-state arbitration clause, every individual-proceeding clause, or arbitration itself unconscionable. Its conclusion depended on the combined restrictions and these franchisees’ circumstances. (Id. at 908–910.)

## Severance and precise relief
The franchisees argued that the agreement was specifically to arbitrate in Utah and could not survive removal of that element. The court disagreed. Section 1670.5 authorized severance or restriction of offending terms where unconscionability did not permeate the agreement. Here the requirement to arbitrate was not intrinsically unfair. The provisions placing all arbitrations in Salt Lake City could be removed while leaving a complete agreement to arbitrate. This differed from an arrangement requiring addition of obligations to cure its essential one-sidedness. (Id. at 910–911.)

The final mandate specifically required striking the Utah-location provisions. Although the opinion considered consolidation and damages restrictions in assessing burden, its operative relief did not separately order their deletion or expressly authorize class arbitration. It ordered vacation of the judgment, entry of the narrower order, dissolution of the appellate stay, and petitioners’ recovery of appellate costs. The decision also left section 20040.5’s application and FAA preemption unresolved. (Id. at 906, 910–911.)
