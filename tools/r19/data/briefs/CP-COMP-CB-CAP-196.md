# Nemariam v. Federal Democratic Republic of Ethiopia

**315 F.3d 390 (D.C. Cir. 2003). January 24, 2003.** Chief Judge Ginsburg wrote for a panel consisting of Ginsburg, Henderson, and Senior Judge Williams. No separate writing is reported. Assigned account: CivPro PDF 237, printed 431–432, Note 5; the developed Nemariam account appears at printed 432.

## The alleged taking and the competing compensation process

Hiwot Nemariam and other plaintiffs sought recovery from Ethiopia and the Commercial Bank of Ethiopia for property damage arising from the Ethiopia–Eritrea border war. Armed conflict began in May 1998. Nemariam alleged that Ethiopia expelled Ethiopian citizens of Eritrean descent, including her, and confiscated property they left behind. Her bank-account example illustrated the practical deprivation: regulations permitted withdrawal only when the holder presented a passbook in person, which expulsion prevented. These were claims of expropriation, rather than findings that a taking had occurred. In June 2000 she filed a two-count complaint in federal district court in Washington, D.C., invoking the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act’s international-takings exception, 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(3). (391–392.)

In December 2000 the two countries signed a peace agreement creating a Claims Commission in The Hague. Its five arbitrators would decide specified war-related claims under international humanitarian and other international law. Only the governments could appear before it, although each could bring claims on behalf of nationals and persons of Ethiopian or Eritrean origin. Its exclusive-jurisdiction provision excepted claims filed elsewhere before the agreement’s effective date, as Nemariam’s had been. The agreement also allowed the governments to negotiate settlements or select another mechanism. Individual claimants could neither independently appear before the Commission nor require it to award money directly to them. (391–392, 394.)

Ethiopia sought forum non conveniens dismissal in favor of the Commission. The district court found an adequate alternative, emphasizing the distinguished arbitrators, their authority to hear claims, and Eritrea’s incentive to present meritorious claims for affected persons. It rejected the concern that the governments might offset awards against each other: nothing in the record showed that they contemplated doing so. The court also found the convenience factors strongly favored the Commission. Its dismissal was without prejudice and allowed reinstatement for good cause if Eritrea failed to submit the plaintiffs’ claims despite a timely affirmative request. By the appeal, Eritrea had submitted claims covering the plaintiffs and the Commission had scheduled an evidentiary hearing. Thus, the problem was not simply that nobody had placed their injuries before the arbitrators. (392–393.)

## Issue and governing standard

Was this Commission an adequate alternative when a successful individual claim might yield only an award controlled by a government, with no enforceable individual entitlement to payment? The D.C. Circuit held that it was not adequate on this record. Despite deferential review for clear abuse of discretion, the district court’s adequacy determination could not support dismissal. (393–395.)

The opinion recited the circuit’s forum non conveniens sequence: identify an adequate forum possessing jurisdiction over the whole case, consider private interests with a presumption protecting the plaintiff’s choice, assess public interests, and ensure reinstatement abroad without undue prejudice. It expressly noted that Piper had displaced the older formulation limiting consideration of public interests to cases in which private interests were approximately balanced. The adequacy prerequisite remained dispositive here. A less generous remedy does not itself defeat adequacy; a remedy so unsatisfactory that it amounts to none can. (392–394.)

## Why participation through Eritrea was insufficient

The Commission could award relief to Ethiopia or Eritrea, not directly to Nemariam. Eritrea would control the disposition of any award made on her behalf. Under international law a state could abandon, settle, retain, or barter its claim. The agreement also expressly permitted direct negotiations between the governments. Eritrea therefore could offset the claim or an award against Ethiopian claims, even if Nemariam’s individual grievance proved entirely meritorious. Whether the Commission currently intended a setoff was not the controlling question: the governments possessed that option and no legal barrier prevented it. Ethiopia had not negated these possibilities. (394–395.)

Ethiopia responded that Eritrea had announced an intention to pass compensation for private property directly to victims. The court distinguished a voluntary governmental intention from an enforceable right. The Commission could not compel Eritrea to honor that intention. Eritrea’s own memorandum also acknowledged circumstances in which practical distribution difficulties might outweigh individualized compensation. Although those circumstances apparently differed from Nemariam’s allegations, the memorandum did not supply the assurance Ethiopia asserted. Depending on Eritrea’s goodwill left the plaintiff without a guaranteed remedy for a successful claim. (394.)

Ethiopia also cited Gonzalez, where limited Mexican wrongful-death recovery made litigation economically unattractive. The court explained that a modest legal entitlement differs from potentially receiving nothing despite success. Its comparison with a United States award was expressly conditional: **assuming the district court had jurisdiction**, success there would produce relief enforceable for the plaintiff herself. The panel did not decide that the FSIA exception actually established jurisdiction or that Nemariam would prevail. The complete quotation’s jurisdictional qualification is essential; the assigned account omits it through editorial ellipses. (395.)

## Judgment and the limits of the decision

The panel **reversed the judgment dismissing the case for forum non conveniens**. It acknowledged that adequacy was a close question under the deferential standard and agreed that the record did not suggest an actual planned setoff. The absence of any legal barrier, combined with the lack of a personal right to payment, nevertheless made the alternative inadequate. (395.)

The court did not reach the plaintiffs’ separate objections about inability to control presentation of their claims, the district court’s private/public balancing, or Ethiopia’s alleged waiver under the peace agreement. It did not categorically disqualify international tribunals or resolve the merits of the alleged takings. The case turns on the particular Commission’s remedy structure, rather than the arbitrators’ competence or the prospect of lower damages alone.
