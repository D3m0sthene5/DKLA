# National Nutritional Foods Association v. FDA
504 F.2d 761 (2d Cir. 1974) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Court, contested action, and disposition

On August 15, 1974, Friendly, Smith, and District Judge Bartels, sitting by designation, unanimously resolved fifteen petitions challenging FDA regulations for vitamin and mineral supplements sold as foods. Friendly wrote the sole opinion. The court broadly sustained FDA's power to prescribe standards of identity and much of the accompanying labeling scheme, but required a meaningful exceptions procedure, invalidated particular restrictions, and reopened the record for wrongly denied cross-examination. It granted the petitions in part and denied them in part, stayed enforcement on specified terms, remanded to FDA, and awarded no costs. It did not simply invalidate all nutrition regulations or require unrestricted cross-examination. (767–768, 783–789, 792–799, 805–808.)

## Regulatory program and long hearing

The challenged final rules, published August 2, 1973 for full implementation January 1, 1975, were Part 80, definitions and standards of identity, and Part 125, label statements concerning special dietary uses. Section 401 authorized reasonable food identity standards promoting honesty and fair dealing for consumers; § 403(j) authorized information needed to disclose foods' value for special dietary uses. The proceeding began with a June 1962 proposal. Revisions attracted objections and requests for an evidentiary hearing under § 701(e). The hearing lasted more than twenty-two months, producing 32,405 transcript pages and hundreds of exhibits. After proposed findings and tentative regulations appeared in January 1973, FDA considered a final round of exceptions before issuing the rules. (767–768.)

Part 80 standardized an extraordinarily diverse supplement market. It permitted specified combinations of all vitamins and minerals, all vitamins, all minerals, or all vitamins and iron, as well as single nutrients. It defined mandatory and optional ingredients, required appropriate names, and imposed minimum and maximum quantities for designated consumer groups. FDA replaced earlier minimum-daily-requirement labeling with U.S. Recommended Daily Allowances derived from National Academy of Sciences recommendations. Certain conventional foods, restored foods, and low-dose products not represented as supplements were exempt. (768–771 & nn. 2–4.)

Part 125 complemented that scheme but also contained substantive prohibitions. Section 125.1(c) barred specified essential nutrients for which no allowance had been established from general-purpose foods and supplements, subject to narrow exceptions. Section 125.1(h) treated preparations above the maximum allowance as drugs. Additional provisions prohibited misleading claims about therapeutic efficacy, the adequacy of ordinary diets, soil and processing effects, unnecessary ingredients, and natural vitamins' supposed superiority. The fact that a provision appeared in the labeling part did not make it merely a label requirement. (771; 786–789; 799–806 & n. 68.)

## Jurisdiction over the integrated scheme

Some petitioners challenged the appellate court's jurisdiction over eight provisions while attacking their validity. They reasoned that § 701(f) review covered regulations under §§ 401 and 403(j), whereas other rules required a district-court action. The court distinguished jurisdiction from whether FDA's statutory construction ultimately succeeded. An arguable invocation of the specified sections supplied jurisdiction, even if the court later rejected it or found an alternative statutory basis. (771–773 & nn. 5–6.)

The drug-classification provision presented the closest question. Standing alone, a general rule interpreting drug status would ordinarily require district-court review, as had the separate vitamin A and D prescription rules. Here it formed an integral part of regulations colorably resting on §§ 401 and 403(j). Judicial economy supported reviewing the entire program rather than sending one issue elsewhere. Note 8 subjected this integrated provision to the substantial-evidence standard governing the proceeding, while distinguishing ordinary informal rulemaking review. The court held that it could decide every issue tendered. (773–774 & nn. 7–9.)

## Standards authority and consumer confusion

Petitioners argued that identity standards and special-diet labeling were mutually exclusive alternatives; that “dietary supplement” was a functional class rather than a food's common name; that § 401 addressed economic adulteration alone; and that FDA could not ban wholesome products or excessive but harmless quantities. The court rejected those categorical objections. Unlike another labeling subsection, § 403(j) did not exclude standardized foods. The “so far as practicable” qualification allowed flexibility in names, and dietary supplement had become a common market designation. (774–775, 779–781.)

FDA had substantial evidence of bewildering variation in ingredients and dosages, consumers unable to determine their needs, and formulations bearing little relation to nutritional needs. Labels alone might not cure that confusion. Quaker Oats established that identity standards could exclude beneficial ingredients and protect consumers from variable compositions rather than merely dilution. Congress's examples of cheapened jam and butter did not confine the statute to those examples. Whether standardized dosages raised costs for sophisticated consumers had to be weighed against benefits for ordinary consumers; that balance primarily belonged to FDA. Narrow exemptions did not make regulation of the larger problem irrational. (775–783 & nn. 10–24.)

The court did not decide that every total ban on separately sold, nontoxic nutrients would fall within statutory authority. Nor did it decide whether marketing products as “imitation” supplements would avoid the standards, since no party sought that commercially unattractive option. Constitutional objections were insufficiently substantial to require discussion. These reservations keep the broad authority ruling within the actual dispute. (778–781 & nn. 11, 18, 20–21.)

## Exceptions, balancing, and enforcement stay

Broad authority did not justify FDA's all-or-nothing approach to every combination and maximum. Vitamin B complex might be understandable without all other vitamins, and a plainly identified 500-milligram vitamin C tablet might serve real demand without creating appreciable confusion. The court did not conclusively approve those products; it required FDA to consider them seriously. The existing combination-exceptions mechanism lacked sufficient assurance that FDA would balance the lost freedom against actual additional confusion, and it did not permit requests to increase individual maxima. (783–785 & nn. 25–26.)

FDA had to broaden § 80.1(b)(4), allow applications for higher dosages, and explicitly consider meritorious applications before enforcement. Relevant considerations included consumer demand, significant expert support, warning labels' effectiveness, and the reliability of the particular allowance. The agency could preserve a manageable standardized market and need not grant numerous exceptions. An avalanche seeking effectively to reopen everything could justify denial without prejudice, except that vitamin C and B-complex applications had to be considered. To avoid forcing disruptive label changes or withdrawing products while exceptions remained undecided, the court stayed the entire scheme until six months after its judgment became final or June 30, 1975, whichever was later. Further extensions remained possible. (785–786 & n. 27.)

## Essential nutrients and unsupported drug classification

The absence of an established recommended allowance did not itself justify banning an acknowledged essential nutrient. FDA had not articulated an adequate basis or marshaled sufficient evidence for that extreme restriction, especially while allowing separate sale of substances considered much less valuable. The court enjoined restrictions on including § 125.1(c)'s essential nutrients in general foods or as optional supplement ingredients, including separately sold supplements. FDA could determine their treatment in combinations on remand. Note 31 also required specific attention to omitted ingredients, integration of additional essential nutrients pending allowances, and clarification of the intended treatment of nonessential substances. The court could not review an uncertain, unexplained prohibition. (786–788 & nn. 30–31.)

The blanket drug designation in § 125.1(h) likewise failed. Pharmacopoeia listing was appellate counsel's post hoc justification and would classify lower-dose vitamins as drugs too, contrary to the program itself. The actual agency rationale—that high doses had no food or nutritional use—mischaracterized the record. Some people, including women taking oral contraceptives, had nutritional needs above the general limits. Section 201(g)(1)(B) turned on therapeutic intent. Objective evidence could expose a falsely claimed nutritional purpose, but uselessness as food for most people did not alone establish that intent. The court invalidated § 125.1(h); properly labeled high-dose products could still be marketed as over-the-counter drugs. It did not hold that every vitamin preparation was necessarily a food. (788–789 & nn. 32–35.)

## Recommended allowances as maxima

Informational allowance labeling and minimum quantities were relatively uncontroversial. Maximum quantities prompted objections that population estimates were imperfect measures of individual needs, excluded some unusual needs, and were not designed to cap consumption. The court distinguished limits on tablet strength from a prohibition on ingestion. Consumers remained free to take multiple tablets, although at additional expense, and the exceptions procedure could accommodate particular products. Experts disagreed about the allowances' suitability, but FDA could resolve that conflict and use the best available, revisable instrument. The maxima, generally 150 percent of U.S. allowances with specified exceptions, were substantively sustainable subject to the procedural defect discussed next. (789–792 & nn. 36–43.)

## Denied cross-examination required a limited reopened record

This was formal rulemaking governed by § 556 because the statute required on-the-record proceedings. Section 556(d) protected cross-examination necessary for full and true disclosure; it did not guarantee unlimited questioning. Concern about eighteen months of hearings justified measures controlling repetition. The examiner nevertheless erred by treating all nongovernmental participants as sharing an interest merely because each opposed some regulation. The National Health Federation opposed virtually the whole program, while the American Medical Association generally supported it. (792–795 & nn. 44–51.)

Dr. Sebrell, former chair of the committee producing the 1968 allowances, was the critical witness. FDA had initially listed him, and its representations caused participants reasonably to defer questions to him. It later did not call him because he disagreed with some proposals. When the AMA called him, the examiner refused NHF representative Dr. Miles Robinson permission to cross-examine and denied an interlocutory agency appeal. Other questioners did not cure the exclusion: manufacturers focused on therapeutic preparations, and a consumer representative lacked the scientific skills needed to probe the allowances. Robinson's persistence could have been controlled through limits rather than total exclusion. (793–797 & nn. 46–56.)

Sebrell's personal role and testimony uniquely supported several key findings. No effective questioning had explored the underlying scientific process, doubts, potential biases, or intended uses of the figures. The court could not honestly rule out a significant probability that vigorous examination would change FDA's decision or the record supporting review. Calling Sebrell as Robinson's own witness was no adequate answer to the denied statutory opportunity. The court required reopening for reasonable cross-examination by Robinson or a similarly interested representative, using another qualified Board member if Sebrell was unavailable. This was a limited remand, not a fresh unrestricted hearing; it need not delay exceptions applications. (797–799 & nn. 57–66.)

## Labeling, produce, and remaining procedural objections

The court upheld informational allowance labels. It also sustained prohibitions on misleading therapeutic claims; unsupported generalizations about soil or processing creating dietary deficiencies; nutritionally unnecessary ingredients in combinations; and claims that an identical vitamin was superior merely because natural. Literal truth could mislead, but the rules allowed supported specific nutrient claims, factual statements of origin, and acknowledgment that unbalanced diets might be deficient. Nutrient loss in particular foods was not equivalent to a deficiency in an overall varied diet. The major qualification concerned iron: strong uncontradicted evidence showed that women of childbearing age and children could have difficulty obtaining enough from a balanced ordinary diet. FDA had to produce further evidence or appropriately qualify that prohibition on remand. (799–806 & nn. 67–86.)

Sunkist obtained protection for fresh fruits and vegetables in their natural state. Section 401's provisos sharply limited identity standards for produce, and nutritional promotional claims did not eliminate those restrictions. Part 125 was also ill-suited to natural variations. Enforcement of Parts 80 and 125 against natural fresh produce was enjoined. Fortification that transformed produce into a fabricated product was different; no demonstrated intended practice required relief on that theory. (806–807 & nn. 87–88.)

The court rejected challenges to hiring and assigning Examiner Harris when the sole existing examiner was occupied and borrowing another agency's examiner for a lengthy proceeding was impracticable. Meetings with FDA officials documented in a public file concerned expedition rather than merits influence, with no demonstrated prejudice. Written direct testimony, longer schedules, evidentiary rulings, and intemperate exchanges did not warrant reversal. Other denied cross-examinations lacked a sufficient showing of possible effect on the agency decision or judicial review. The final result remained partial relief, the specified stay, and remand without costs. (807–808 & nn. 89–91.)
