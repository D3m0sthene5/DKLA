# Nunley v. Pettway Oil Co.

**346 F.2d 95 (6th Cir. 1965) · United States Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit · May 27, 1965**  
**Civil Procedure · Judicial comment on evidence and the jury's factfinding role**  
**Assigned:** CivPro PDF 573–574, printed 1130–1132, complete own-caption account, plus all four accompanying notes through printed 1133.

## Facts and the trial result

Kenneth Jerry Nunley was injured when a truck rolled forward off an elevated grease rack at a gas station. Pettway Oil Company owned the station, and Owens leased and operated it. Nunley alleged that Pettway negligently maintained the rack. His status on the premises mattered under the Tennessee law applied in this diversity action: an invitation connected to business dealings could support ordinary-care duties, whereas Pettway argued that a licensee could not recover absent gross or willful negligence, which it said had not been shown. 346 F.2d at 96–97.

Nunley regularly drove trucks for Gibson, who operated eight trucks from a lot behind the station and purchased gas and oil from Owens. Drivers used the station to receive work-related telephone messages and sometimes reported there on Saturdays to learn whether work was available. Nunley also occasionally worked for Owens changing oil on Saturdays. On the particular Saturday, however, he learned there was no work. Those employment relationships therefore did not make him an invitee as a matter of law merely because he had an employment duty to be present. They remained relevant background for the jury. Id. at 96.

Nunley also testified that he was a customer who bought fuel and repair parts at the station. That day, he intended to repair his car's starter and buy whatever parts the repair required from Owens. With Owens and his employees aware of what he was doing, Nunley brought the starter into a work area just forward of and below the rack. A truck then rolled off it and injured him. This proposed purchase and permitted use of the work area could support a business-purpose invitation, but they did not compel a single classification. Id. at 96–97.

The jury returned $25,000 liability verdicts against both Owens and Pettway. It also answered a special interrogatory by classifying Nunley as a licensee rather than an invitee. The trial judge left the judgment against Owens intact but granted judgment notwithstanding the verdict for Pettway based on that answer. Nunley's appeal concerned the owner, Pettway; the appellate mandate did not order a new trial against Owens. Id. at 96, 98–99.

## Why the jury instruction became decisive

During deliberations, the jurors reported that they had agreed on three questions but could not agree on the first, concerning status on the premises. The judge conferred with counsel, then explained that an unresolved issue might require a mistrial, bringing in another jury and repeating an expensive proceeding. He urged further deliberation, said this jury was as capable as another would be, and announced his own assessment: the evidence established that Nunley was a licensee, not an invitee. Id. at 97–98.

The judge repeatedly cautioned that the jurors remained the exclusive judges of fact and need not accept his assessment. He distinguished binding legal instructions from nonbinding evidence comments. He also told jurors to respect one another's views, change positions if persuaded by reason, and avoid surrendering a firm conviction solely to obtain unanimity. Shortly afterward, the jury answered the status question in the way he had recommended, while returning damages verdicts against both defendants. The Sixth Circuit therefore evaluated the instruction in its actual deliberation context rather than treating the disclaimer as conclusive protection. Id. at 98–99.

## Parties' positions and holding

Nunley first argued that he was an invitee as a matter of law. Alternatively, he maintained that his status was a jury question and that the judge's intervention effectively decided it against him. Pettway argued that he was a licensee as a matter of law and that the absence of gross or willful negligence required judgment in its favor. Alternatively, Pettway defended the instruction as permissible federal judicial comment and the resulting answer as a proper basis for judgment notwithstanding the verdict. Id. at 96.

The Sixth Circuit rejected both attempts to make the classification a matter of law. The evidence supported submitting the issue to the jury. Tennessee invitation principles recognized premises use for the owner's benefit, mutual interest, or business dealings; a business visitor could be permitted to enter for purposes directly or indirectly connected to such dealings. Nunley's purchase intention and activities required factual assessment against those standards. The trial judge originally acted correctly by leaving that assessment to the jury. Id. at 96–97.

The appellate court nevertheless held that his later comment crossed the line. It addressed the ultimate disputed fact that the jury was struggling to decide and, under these circumstances, amounted to an instructed verdict for Pettway. The federal authority to comment on evidence remained established, and the court did not seek to restrict useful explanations narrowly. It also acknowledged the judge's legitimate desire to avoid a mistrial. But neither that purpose nor his repeated statements that the comment was nonbinding neutralized its practical intrusion into the jury's role. Id. at 99.

## Reasoning and the limits of the rule

The court relied on *Quercia v. United States* for the proposition that judicial comment is controlled discretion, not an unrestricted power. Judges may help analyze evidence, but they may not become witnesses, distort or supplement testimony, or use judicial authority to undermine independent factfinding. The reproduced *Quercia* passage also explained why a general disclaimer might fail to cure a concrete, persuasive factual assertion that the judge had not withdrawn. Nunley applied that safeguard to the judge's decisive status assessment. Id. at 99.

The holding is consequently narrower than a ban on all judicial opinions about evidence. Nor did the court hold that mentioning retrial costs independently made every supplemental instruction invalid. The operative defect was the ultimate factual classification supplied by the judge in the setting described, despite a genuine dispute for the jury. The appellate court did not itself classify Nunley as an invitee, determine that Pettway was liable, or reinstate the damages judgment as the final remedy. Id. at 97–99.

Judge Edwards wrote for a panel consisting of Judges Miller, Phillips, and Edwards. The report contains no separate opinion, dissent, judicial footnote, or numerical vote notation. The exact disposition was **reversal and remand for a new trial as to Pettway Oil Company**. Id. at 99.
