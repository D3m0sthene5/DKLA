# Hicks v. Bush
10 N.Y.2d 488, 180 N.E.2d 425 (1962) | Decided January 18, 1962 | Contracts

## A proposed merger dependent on new capital
On July 10, 1956, Frederick Hicks, Michael Congero, Jack McGee, and members of Clinton G. Bush Company signed an agreement to combine their corporate interests in a holding company, Bush-Hicks Enterprises. The purpose was more efficient operation and financial strength. Hicks would subscribe for approximately 425,000 holding-company shares, the Bush interests for over a million shares, and the remaining participants for fewer than 50,000. The principal consideration was transfer of stock in their operating companies to the holding company. 10 N.Y.2d at 489–90.

The document required subscriptions within five days and terminated everyone’s obligations if Bush-Hicks failed to accept any subscription within twenty-five days. Subscriptions were promptly made and accepted. Hicks transferred his operating-company stock, but the Bush defendants did not transfer theirs; Hicks received no holding-company shares, and the merger did not occur. Id. at 490.

The defendants maintained that all parties had orally agreed to a further prerequisite: the writing and merger would become effective only if $672,500 in equity expansion funds was raised. They regarded that money as indispensable to the venture. Testimony from the Bush company’s president described signing preliminary documents so the parties would be ready, while the actual understanding remained no money, no deal. The capital, hoped for by December 31, 1956, was never raised. Id. at 490–91. The opinion relies on the trial court’s finding that this understanding existed, rather than assuming every asserted oral condition is true. The assigned editors also quote Hicks’s contrary testimony: he denied that such a condition had been stated at signing, citing Record pages 410 and 434. That factual denial belongs to the editorial introduction; the judicial holding rests on the condition the trial court found proved. Assigned PDF p. 168 (printed p. 826).

## Litigation and the claimed contradiction
Hicks sued for specific performance and an accounting. The defendants pleaded the oral condition as an affirmative defense. Over Hicks’s parol-evidence objection, the trial court admitted testimony, found the condition established, and entered judgment for the defendants. The courts below sustained that conclusion; the Court of Appeals granted leave to consider admissibility. Id. at 489–91.

Hicks argued that the written twenty-five-day subscription provision was irreconcilable with the additional condition withholding effectiveness until capital was raised. Because the writing already identified a circumstance terminating the parties’ obligations, he sought to exclude another orally established limitation. The reported advocacy also characterized the oral understanding as a condition subsequent rather than precedent. The judicial opinion addressed the understanding actually found: effectiveness of the contract and merger was suspended until the capital arrived. Id. at 489, 492–94.

## Parol evidence of a condition to legal effectiveness
The court distinguished proof that a writing never became operative from proof contradicting its express terms. Parol testimony may establish a condition precedent to a written agreement’s legal effectiveness so long as the condition does not genuinely contradict the writing. Every added oral condition produces some disparity from an apparently unconditional written promise, but treating that disparity alone as contradiction would eliminate the recognized rule. The question is whether the asserted condition can coexist with the actual provisions. Id. at 491.

Fadex illustrated a genuine conflict: its writing said goods were ready and called for prompt delivery, while the seller sought to condition the sale on obtaining the goods later. Giving effect to that oral understanding would annul affirmative written assurances. Hicks involved no comparable assurance that expansion funds existed or that the merger must proceed without them. The writing was silent on that matter. Id. at 491–92.

The subscription deadline and capital requirement could stand together. Acceptance within twenty-five days satisfied one prerequisite without removing the other. If both had been written into the agreement, there would be no immediate duty to transfer the operating-company shares until both were fulfilled. The presence of one written condition did not itself exclude another consistent oral condition. Id. at 492–93.

## The footnote analogy and the factual finding
The court supported that conclusion with authorities recognizing that an instrument containing a conditional promise can itself be conditionally delivered. Footnote 1 discusses Golden v. Meier: a written creamery arrangement required all signatures, but an additional oral condition required commitments of milk from approximately 300 cows. That condition was collateral rather than contradictory; satisfying it would leave the writing effective according to its terms, while failure meant the agreement never became binding. Id. at 493 n.1.

Here the evidence sufficiently established that the merger remained a proposal and that formal preliminary steps were permitted without making the contract operative. Obtaining subscriptions and signing the public-facing documents therefore did not prove the capital condition had disappeared. The court regarded such an oral understanding as plausible in a transaction whose writing was intended for public consumption. Because the required capital was never raised, the trial court was justified in concluding that no operative binding contract came into existence. Id. at 493–94.

## Judgment, participation, and limits
The Court of Appeals affirmed with costs. Fuld wrote; Desmond, Dye, Froessel, Van Voorhis, Burke, and Foster concurred. All seven listed judges joined, with no separate opinion. Id. at 494.

The holding requires both a proved condition to effectiveness and consistency with the writing. It does not allow a party to defeat an integrated document by simply relabeling a contradictory promise as a condition. The complete reported opinion and its sole judicial footnote were read together with the assigned edited excerpt. The source record distinguishes the editors’ factual introduction and later merger-clause discussion from the court’s words. Current treatment was not evaluated through a citator.
