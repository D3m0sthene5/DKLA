# Havens Realty Corp. v. Coleman

**455 U.S. 363 (1982) — Supreme Court of the United States — February 24, 1982.** Justice Brennan wrote for a unanimous Court; Justice Powell also concurred separately. Assigned account: November second casebook, physical PDF page 112, printed page 1169.

A Black housing tester who received racially discriminatory false information alleged injury to a statutory right even though she did not intend to rent an apartment. A white tester who received accurate information did not have that claim. Both could attempt to establish a different injury from segregation of their own neighborhoods, and a housing organization adequately alleged injury to its counseling and referral work. These standing conclusions did not mean every claim could proceed: the Black tester's individual misinformation claims were time-barred. (455 U.S. at 373–381.)

## Facts and litigation

Havens owned and operated Camelot Townhouses and Colonial Court Apartments in Henrico County, near Richmond, Virginia. Its employee Rose Jones was also a defendant. The January 1979 complaint alleged racial steering: directing applicants toward or away from housing according to race so as to preserve segregation. Paul Coles, a Black prospective renter, alleged that on July 13, 1978, he was falsely told Camelot had no apartments, while being directed toward integrated Colonial Court. (366–368 & nn.1–4.)

Housing Opportunities Made Equal, or HOME, employed Sylvia Coleman, who was Black, and R. Kent Willis, who was white, as testers. They posed as prospective renters to discover discriminatory practices rather than to obtain housing. On three dates in March 1978, Coleman allegedly received no-vacancy answers while Willis was told vacancies existed. Coleman made another inquiry on July 6 and again was told Camelot had none, while a different white tester received contrary information. HOME provided housing counseling and investigated and referred discrimination complaints. It alleged that the steering impaired those services and drained its resources. The individual plaintiffs also claimed loss of benefits from living in integrated communities. (368–369.)

Before discovery or evidence, the district court dismissed Coleman, Willis, and HOME for lack of standing and expiration of the then-applicable 180-day limitations period. Coles's claims remained, went to trial, and produced a finding of unlawful steering followed by a consent order awarding monetary and injunctive relief to him and his class. The Supreme Court did not review the correctness of that separate merits determination. The Fourth Circuit reversed the other plaintiffs' dismissals, accepting their standing allegations and treating the alleged practice as a continuing violation extending through the timely Coles incident. (369–370 & nn.5–7.)

The dismissed plaintiffs also reached a conditional agreement with the defendants: if approved by the district court, each would receive $400 and no further relief if certiorari was denied or the judgment affirmed, but nothing if the Supreme Court reversed. That arrangement did not moot the controversy. Their damages claims remained adverse; agreeing upon a contingent amount merely liquidated potential damages. They could not recover from the separate Coles fund. The Court therefore proceeded despite the consent order and proposed agreement. (370–372 & nn.8–10.)

## Statutory injury and the two testers

Under *Gladstone*, Congress intended the Fair Housing Act's private enforcement provision to reach Article III's limits, leaving no additional judicial prudential barrier. A plaintiff still had to allege a distinct and palpable injury caused by the defendant's conduct. Congress's creation of a statutory right mattered to identifying that injury; it did not dispense with injury altogether. (372–373.)

Section 804(d) prohibited racially discriminatory representations to any person that an available dwelling was unavailable. The private cause of action made that right enforceable. Coleman alleged precisely the prohibited misinformation. Expecting discrimination and approaching the agent solely to test compliance did not erase injury to her own right to truthful housing information. Unlike the provision concerning discriminatory refusals to rent, this misinformation provision did not require a bona fide offer. The choice of statutory language thus answered the objection that a person who never intended to rent suffered no actionable injury. (373–374 & nn.13–14.)

Willis, however, alleged accurate vacancy information. He neither identified injury to this statutory right nor pleaded a discriminatory-misrepresentation cause of action. The Court rejected tester standing for him, rather than treating all testers as automatically entitled to sue. (374–375 & n.15.)

## Neighborhood and organizational injuries

Coleman and Willis separately alleged that steering deprived them of social, professional, economic, political, and aesthetic benefits of integrated neighborhoods. Those were personal injuries even though the steering directly targeted someone else. A resident injured by conduct violating another person's statutory rights could seek redress under the Act; the essential question was actual injury fairly traceable to that conduct. (375–376 & nn.16–17.)

Their pleading remained strikingly vague. Merely living somewhere in Richmond or Henrico County did not show how discrimination at two adjacent complexes affected their neighborhoods. The Court considered metropolitan-wide effects implausible and emphasized its precedents' relatively compact neighborhoods. Nevertheless, before further factual development it could not rule out an appreciable local effect. The district court was to permit more definite allegations on remand and dismiss if the resulting record still failed to establish standing. Thus the Court preserved the opportunity to substantiate neighborhood injury; it did not hold that the existing general allegations conclusively established it. (377–378.)

HOME's claim rested on injury to the organization itself. Perceptible impairment of counseling and referral services, with a consequent resource drain, was concrete injury beyond frustration of an abstract social mission. Dismissal was inappropriate on those allegations, although HOME would have to prove the impairment before obtaining relief. Its apparent willingness to abandon its separate representative claim for injunctions led the Court to leave associational standing undecided. (378–379 & nn.19–21.)

## Limitations, concurrence, and judgment

The Court applied the Act's then-existing requirement to sue within 180 days of the alleged discriminatory housing practice. An ongoing unlawful practice extending into the period differed from a stale isolated act. Neighborhood injuries and HOME's service impairment were allegedly caused by a continuing pattern that included Coles's July 13 incident. Those claims could therefore proceed without a limitations dismissal. The Attorney General's authority to sue over patterns or practices did not make that category unavailable to private plaintiffs. (380–381 & nn.22–23.)

Coleman's tester injury was different: she personally received misinformation on four isolated occasions, all more than 180 days before filing. The later misinformation to Coles did not violate her right to truthful information. Her tester claim was therefore untimely despite sufficient injury for standing. Willis's tester claim failed for the different reason that he received no misinformation. (381–382.)

The Court **affirmed the Fourth Circuit insofar as it reinstated the neighborhood claims and HOME's own organizational claim, reversed insofar as it permitted the two tester claims to proceed, and directed consistent further proceedings on remand**. It awarded no damages. Brennan's opinion was unanimous. Powell joined but separately criticized the vague neighborhood allegations and the expense of nearly four years of litigation without meaningful jurisdictional facts. The parties and district court should have used amendment or a more definite statement earlier. The proper procedural preconditions to dismissal had not been observed, so he agreed that the Supreme Court could not simply dismiss now. (382–384.)

The assigned discussion highlights statutory information rights and organizational injury. The complete report adds the decisive distinction between standing and timeliness, the contingent settlement, the limited neighborhood pleading ruling, and Powell's concurrence. Its 180-day rule and statutory formulation are reported as part of this 1982 decision, without a claim that they describe every later statutory amendment or standing doctrine.
