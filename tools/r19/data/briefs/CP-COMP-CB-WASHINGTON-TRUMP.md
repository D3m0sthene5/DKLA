# Washington v. Trump

**847 F.3d 1151 (9th Cir. 2017) — February 9, 2017 — Civil Procedure**

Judges William C. Canby, Richard R. Clifton, and Michelle T. Friedland issued the per curiam order. There is no separate writing in this February 9 decision. Assigned: CivPro PDF physical page 603, printed page 1191. Pinpoints use the preserved twenty-nine-page court-issued order, rather than an unverified conversion to reporter pages.

## The order, alleged injuries, and emergency posture

Executive Order 13769, signed January 27, 2017, suspended entry for ninety days by nationals of Iraq, Iran, Libya, Somalia, Sudan, Syria, and Yemen; suspended the refugee admissions program for 120 days; and indefinitely suspended Syrian refugee entry. It directed religious-minority preference when refugee processing resumed and permitted national-interest exceptions. It also directed evaluation of the admission programs during the suspensions. The court reported immediate visa cancellations, travel restrictions, and detentions. (Slip 3–5.)

Washington filed suit on January 30, alleging statutory and constitutional violations, including harm to state universities, separated families, and residents stranded abroad. It asserted that the order implemented a promised Muslim ban rather than its stated security rationale. Minnesota joined the amended complaint, which also added a Tenth Amendment claim. The States sought nationwide interim relief; the Government opposed their motion and participated in the district court hearing. On February 3, Judge James L. Robart issued a TRO restraining enforcement of the entry and refugee suspensions and the religious-priority provisions. He directed briefing on a preliminary injunction and denied a stay. (5–6.)

The Government appealed and requested both an immediate stay and an emergency stay pending appeal. The Ninth Circuit first denied immediate relief and then heard the emergency motion after expedited briefing and telephone argument. The February 9 order decided that motion, not the ultimate constitutionality of the executive order or the full merits appeal. (6 & n.1, 18–19.)

## Why the TRO could be reviewed

The States challenged appellate jurisdiction because TROs are ordinarily not appealable. The panel nevertheless could review an order labeled a TRO if it possessed a preliminary injunction's qualities. Ordinarily that required a substantial adversarial challenge and an order extending beyond Rule 65(b)'s fourteen-day period. Both considerations mattered here. Written briefs and oral argument vigorously contested the TRO's legal foundation. It had no expiration date, no hearing was scheduled, and the district court's schedule showed it would remain effective longer than fourteen days. Given the extraordinary national-security emergency asserted by the Government, that was sufficient for review at this stage. Footnote 2 expressly preserved later reconsideration of appellate jurisdiction at the merits stage. (7–8 & n.2.)

Thus, the ruling was more specific than a rule making every contested TRO appealable. Its label did not control, but neither did adversarial participation alone. The order's actual expected duration and unusual setting were part of the analysis.

## Standing and judicial authority

The panel found a sufficient preliminary showing of standing based on the States' universities. The applicable evidentiary burden depended on the litigation stage, but the States still had to make a clear showing of injury, causation, and redressability. Their declarations described blocked visiting scholars, prospective employees, and interns; paid visa costs at risk; and disruption of teaching, research, travel, and recruitment. The universities were undisputed branches of the States. Entry restrictions therefore caused proprietary institutional injury, and relief against those restrictions could redress it. (8–12 & n.3.)

The university relationships also supported assertion of students' and faculty members' rights: their educational and research interests were intertwined with the universities' activities. Footnote 4 did not decide whether a State possessed its own Establishment Clause rights, because third-party rights sufficed. Footnote 5 left alternative proprietary and parens patriae theories unresolved. The holding should not be restated as general authorization for States to sue on any resident's behalf. (11–13 & nn.4–5.)

The Government further asserted that immigration and national-security decisions were unreviewable, even against constitutional challenge. The panel distinguished substantial deference from complete immunity to judicial review. Constitutional adjudication remained a judicial responsibility even where the political branches had broad immigration powers and superior security expertise. It read Mandel as addressing an individual visa decision applying a congressionally specified standard, rather than exempting sweeping presidential immigration policy from constitutional scrutiny. This was the panel's historical rationale, not a certification that subsequent cases left every formulation intact. (13–18 & n.6.)

## The stay standard and due process analysis

A stay was discretionary, and the Government bore the burden of justifying it. The panel considered likelihood of success, irreparable injury to the applicant, injury to other parties, and the public interest. The first two were most important; the Government failed both, and the remaining factors also did not favor a stay. The analysis was expressly preliminary. (18–19.)

The States' due process theories included inadequate process for certain lawful permanent residents and visa holders denied reentry, interference with protected travel interests, and disregard of statutory asylum procedures. The panel did not determine the complete rights of every person covered by the TRO. It held that the Government had not made the required strong showing against the claims. Due process protected persons within the United States, including noncitizens, and certain returning noncitizens. The Government had not established either that returning permanent residents lacked rights or that the order supplied sufficient process. (19–21.)

White House counsel's later guidance excluding permanent residents did not establish mootness on this record. The Government had shown neither authority to supersede the President's signed order nor binding force throughout the responsible departments. Shifting interpretations also prevented assurance that the alleged conduct could not recur. Even if permanent-resident claims fell away, other persons and institutions could retain viable claims. The opinion carefully identifies potential protected categories instead of announcing an unrestricted right of every prospective entrant abroad. (21–23.)

The panel declined two requested reductions in the TRO. A narrower set of protected persons proposed by the Government omitted some potentially valid claims. A restriction to Washington and Minnesota lacked a workable alternative accounting for the interconnected transit system and multiple ports of entry while protecting the States' interests. The panel referred to the Fifth Circuit's uniform-immigration rationale but expressly declined to adopt that legal conclusion for itself at this stage. It would not rewrite the executive order into a new policy; failure to establish likely success on the due process argument was enough. (23–24.)

## Reserved religion claims, hardship, and exact disposition

The panel discussed the States' evidence of religious purpose, including the President's statements and the order's preference provisions. It explained that purpose evidence could extend beyond a provision's facial wording and regarded the allegations as serious. Because the due process analysis already defeated the requested stay and the emergency record was limited, it reserved the religious-discrimination claims for fuller merits briefing. It expressed no view on the States' other claims. This was not a final holding that the order violated the Establishment or Equal Protection Clause. (19, 24–26.)

On irreparable injury, the Government's urgent interest in combating terrorism did not itself explain the need to reinstate these restrictions immediately. It had not supplied evidence rebutting the States' contention that the TRO temporarily restored the prior position. Footnote 7 distinguished earlier country-of-concern designations from proof of an immediate need for the particular restrictions. Footnote 8 explained that courts could receive classified material under protective procedures; absence of public access did not make review categorically impossible. Institutional separation-of-powers interests could still be vindicated in ordinary litigation. (26–27 & nn.7–8.)

Conversely, the States supplied evidence of disrupted university work, separated families, and stranded residents. The Government did not explain how discretionary national-interest waivers would function or avoid those injuries. The public had substantial interests in security and elected policymaking, but also in travel, family unity, and freedom from discrimination. Those competing interests, considered with the hardships, did not justify relief. The exact order **denied the emergency motion for a stay pending appeal**; it did not finally affirm a merits judgment or permanently invalidate the executive order. (28–29.)
