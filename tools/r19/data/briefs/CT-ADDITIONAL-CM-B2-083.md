# In re Woodcock
45 F.3d 363 (10th Cir. 1995) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and divided scope of agreement

The Tenth Circuit decided this bankruptcy appeal January 6, 1995. Judge Stephen H. Anderson wrote the majority opinion for the panel with Judges Seth and Barrett. Seth wrote separately that he was not prepared to concur in resolving the several questions but concurred in remand. Thus the majority's interpretive and other conclusions should not be attributed unanimously to all three judges. All three did agree to decide without oral argument at the parties' request. The district court had affirmed the bankruptcy court's determination that the student loans were nondischargeable. The Tenth Circuit affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded for findings about applicable suspensions of repayment; it did not itself discharge the loans. (363, 365 & n. 1, 368.)

## Loans, education, and the historical statutory alternatives

Woodcock financed legal education with four guaranteed loans of $5,000 each. NYSHESC guaranteed them and was effectively the only appellee participating in this appeal: the other named appellees had not briefed it and had not been parties in district court. Woodcock graduated from law school in spring 1982 and received his MBA in January 1983. He then took various college courses part time through 1990 without enrolling in another degree program. The creditor conceded he remained at least a half-time student without a nine-month interruption before April 21, 1985. He filed Chapter 7 bankruptcy in 1992 and sought a discharge determination in an adversary proceeding. (365 & n. 2; 367.)

The statute applied in this opinion generally excluded educational loans from discharge but provided two alternatives: loans first due more than seven years before the petition, excluding applicable repayment suspensions, or an undue hardship from nondischarge for the debtor and dependents. The seven-year date relevant here was April 21, 1985. This is the decision's historical statutory framework, not a representation of present bankruptcy discharge law. Legal rulings were reviewed de novo and factual findings for clear error. (365.)

## The competing meanings of matriculation

The notes began repayment at the end of the ninth month after the borrower ceased to be matriculated, withdrew, or became less than a half-time student at an approved school. Other provisions triggered repayment immediately upon failure to enroll for the approved term and institution, verify student status, or make required interest payments. Woodcock argued that matriculation required enrollment in a degree program and ended after his MBA. The creditor equated matriculation with enrollment, claiming the loans matured only in 1991 after his half-time attendance ended. (365.)

The bankruptcy court found both definitions in a dictionary but, without deciding ambiguity, selected simple enrollment. Alternatively, it reasoned that enough courses would eventually qualify Woodcock for some degree. In denying a new trial it also invoked estoppel because he had not reported cessation of matriculation. The district court adopted the primary enrollment reading. These are the lower courts' positions, not alternative rationales endorsed by the appellate majority. The majority's disposition reverses the maturity interpretation without separately developing an estoppel analysis. (366–368.)

## The majority reads the whole instrument

New York law governed interpretation. It required reading terms in the context of the whole contract and resorting to extrinsic evidence only for ambiguity; ambiguity and interpretation of unambiguous terms were legal questions. The majority held that these notes unambiguously used matriculation to require pursuit of a degree. Failure to enroll was already a distinct trigger, requiring immediate repayment rather than repayment after nine months. Another provision required reporting failure to enroll, leaving school, or ceasing matriculation, distinguishing the concepts. A further provision distinguished matriculation from half-time attendance. Treating them as synonyms would disregard those differences. (366.)

The repayment sentence also made practical sense as a degree-progress standard: cessation of matriculation meant no longer pursuing a degree, while withdrawal and attendance below half time described insufficient progress even within such a pursuit. A dictionary's alternative possibilities did not compel ambiguity where the contractual provisions resolved the meaning. (366.)

The majority added that even assuming ambiguity, external evidence supported the same construction. The guaranteed-loan program required satisfactory progress in a course of study. The creditor's own representative said matriculation concerned the student's intent to obtain a degree. He explained that some institutions no longer determined that status, causing the creditor to stop using the measure because it became unworkable. The majority answered that administrative difficulty did not authorize unilateral alteration of the agreed maturity terms. Woodcock's uncontradicted testimony showed no degree-program enrollment after the MBA, and nothing supported speculation that miscellaneous courses would eventually yield a degree. The notes therefore matured in October 1983, nine months after graduation from business school. (366–367.)

## Maturity did not finally resolve discharge

Applicable suspensions had to be excluded when calculating the statutory seven-year period. The bankruptcy court had made no findings on that issue, and the appellate record was insufficient to decide it. The majority cited decisions considering ineligible deferments and forbearances as possible suspensions but did not resolve their application to Woodcock. Remand was specifically required for this remaining question. An October 1983 maturity date alone did not establish discharge. (367–368.)

The debtor separately asserted undue hardship, on which he bore the burden. The lower court had applied several tests and rejected hardship under each. The appellate majority affirmed substantially for the district court's reasons; it did not announce a new controlling test or expressly adopt Brunner as the circuit's exclusive standard here. (367–368.)

## Evidentiary and pleading rulings were affirmed

The bankruptcy court excluded an affidavit and numerous exhibits as offensive or irrelevant but permitted Woodcock's testimony and the exhibits he discussed. His vague objection that the whole story could not be told did not demonstrate denial of a fair opportunity under the evidence rules. The majority found no abuse of discretion. (368.)

The creditor initially conceded early maturity while relying on continuous deferments. At trial it shifted to the enrollment interpretation and a 1991 maturity date. The bankruptcy court permitted amendment to conform to evidence under Bankruptcy Rule 7015 and Civil Rule 15(b). The majority rejected claimed surprise because Woodcock himself raised the matriculation theory only the day before trial; the creditor expressly disputed it, offered evidence without his objection, and he requested no continuance. The maturity issue was tried by consent, so permitting amendment was within discretion even though the majority ultimately rejected the creditor's substantive interpretation. (368.)

## Assigned account and reading scope

The actual notes contain additional triggers and use the same concepts elsewhere; these whole-document details resolve the majority's interpretive question. The answer concerns degree-program enrollment, but the case's ultimate discharge question remained open because of possible suspensions.
