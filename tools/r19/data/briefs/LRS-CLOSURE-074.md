# Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc. v. Gorsuch — the circuit judgment reversed in Chevron

685 F.2d 718 (D.C. Cir. 1982) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Court, posture, and judgment

Ruth Bader Ginsburg wrote for a unanimous panel with Mikva and visiting Senior District Judge William J. Jameson on August 17, 1982. NRDC, Citizens for a Better Environment, and Northwestern Ohio Lung Association petitioned for direct review of EPA’s October 14, 1981 regulations; industry intervenors supported EPA. The court granted review and vacated the challenged regulations. This is the distinct circuit judgment subsequently reversed in Chevron, rather than the Supreme Court’s deference holding itself. (718–720, 724–725, 728; assigned PDF 153, printed 933.)

## Regulatory setting and the change challenged

The Clean Air Act’s national ambient air quality standards required state implementation plans. After many areas failed to achieve timely attainment, Congress’s 1977 amendments added Part D for nonattainment areas. Those provisions required expeditious attainment, reasonably available controls, and annual progress toward the standards. A permit program governed new or modified major stationary sources, and a construction moratorium applied where a state lacked an approved plan satisfying Part D. (721–723.)

Permits depended on emissions offsets or an appropriate growth allowance, the lowest achievable emission rate, compliance by the applicant’s other controlled sources, and the state’s implementation of its plan. What counted as a stationary source therefore controlled when stringent requirements attached. Part D did not expressly define that term, and the legislative history did not directly settle it. (722–723 & nn. 16–21.)

EPA’s August 1980 regulations used a dual definition: a source could be an entire plant or an identifiable individual piece of process equipment within it. EPA reasoned that including individual units would bring more projects under review and promote improvement in polluted areas. It also treated reconstructed facilities as new sources, presumptively where replacement components exceeded fifty percent of comparable new-facility capital cost. (723–724.)

The October 1981 revision substituted a plantwide definition and deleted the reconstruction requirement. Under this bubble approach, an increase at one unit could be offset by a decrease elsewhere in the plant without triggering review if the overall increase stayed below the relevant threshold. EPA emphasized reduced regulatory complexity and greater state flexibility; whole-plant reconstruction would rarely occur, making the separate reconstruction rule of little use. The environmental petitioners challenged that narrowing. (724–725 & nn. 26–28.)

## The circuit’s controlling precedents

The court treated two prior circuit judgments as controlling. Alabama Power had required the bubble approach in the prevention-of-significant-deterioration program for comparatively clean areas. ASARCO had rejected it for national new-source performance standards. The panel reconciled these holdings through the purposes of the particular programs: a plantwide approach suited preservation of existing air quality, while an improvement program could not permit altered equipment to escape requirements through unchanged net plant emissions. (719–720, 725–727.)

This analysis did not rest on a conclusion that Congress expressly specified the exact source definition for Part D. The panel acknowledged absent explicit statutory direction and contradictory legislative history. Instead, it regarded its own circuit precedent as establishing the legal rule governing EPA’s permissible discretion. Footnote 7 expressly reserved what result the court would reach without those controlling precedents. (720 n. 7; 726 n. 39.)

The opinion also recognized that EPA could define the constituent terms building, structure, facility, and installation differently for different statutory programs. The boundary it imposed concerned the program’s objective, rather than a universal rule that every Clean Air Act source always meant an individual unit. (723 n. 22; 725–726.)

## Application to Part D and EPA’s arguments

Because the nonattainment program aimed to improve unhealthy air to meet national standards, the panel placed it on the improvement side of its precedent-derived line. Allowing a bubble to exempt changes from review conflicted with that program’s objective. EPA argued that states needed flexibility and would still have to attain the standards through their overall plans. The court distinguished the goal of improving air quality from a method of achieving it: flexibility was not an independent end overriding mandatory federal permit requirements. (726–728.)

EPA also attempted to distinguish technology-oriented performance standards from emissions-oriented programs. Footnote 38 rejected that reconciliation because Part D itself included technology-forcing requirements and because Alabama Power’s operative distinction concerned preservation versus improvement. The court likewise declined to isolate the construction moratorium as a maintenance-only scheme; whatever its particular role, it formed part of the larger nonattainment program. It did not need to resolve the parties’ competing descriptions of the moratorium’s separate purpose. (726–727 nn. 38–40.)

Footnote 41 supplied an additional criticism of EPA’s revised policy explanation. Previously EPA thought the dual definition promoted improvement; it now suggested that the definition discouraged replacement with cleaner equipment. The panel found no supporting study or survey in the record for that new proposition and considered speculation insufficient for reasoned policy change. This criticism supplements the central precedent-based statutory analysis; it should not be presented as the only ground on which the court vacated the rule. (727 n. 41.)

## Reconstruction requirement and precise disposition

EPA deleted the reconstruction rule because its plantwide definition left that rule little practical work. Once the court rejected the plantwide definition, the ancillary deletion also had to be vacated. Industry intervenors proposed alternative justifications, but a reviewing court could not sustain agency action on a rationale different from the agency’s stated basis. (728 & n. 43.)

The final judgment granted the petition and vacated EPA’s October 14, 1981 regulations. It did not remand for the agency simply to improve an explanation while leaving those rules operative. Nor did it hold that a bubble approach was unlawful throughout the Clean Air Act: its ruling concerned this nonattainment program and the implications of the circuit’s precedents. There were no separate writings. (728.)

## Relation to the assigned Chevron account and source limits

The textbook’s separate narration identifies this lower-stage ruling as the decision the Supreme Court reversed. The Supreme Court’s criticism quoted there—that the circuit adopted a static definition despite absent congressional command—is the later Court’s assessment, not Ginsburg’s stated method. Preserving both stages makes the conflict intelligible: this panel found its precedents legally controlling, whereas Chevron rejected that judicial restriction of EPA’s interpretive choice. (Assigned PDF 153, printed 933.)
