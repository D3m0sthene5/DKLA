# Sinochem International Co. Ltd. v. Malaysia International Shipping Corp.

**549 U.S. 422 (2007) — Supreme Court of the United States, March 5, 2007.** Justice Ginsburg delivered the opinion for a unanimous Court; there were no separate writings. The assigned account is CivPro, physical PDF page 237, printed page 433, Note 9.

## Facts and the parallel litigation

Sinochem, a Chinese state-owned importer, contracted with Triorient, a domestic corporation, to buy steel coils. Payment under a letter of credit required a valid bill of lading certifying loading for shipment to China by April 30, 2003. Triorient subchartered a vessel owned by Malaysia International, a Malaysian company, and hired a stevedoring company to load the coils in Philadelphia. A bill dated April 30 triggered payment. Triorient was not a party to the case before the Supreme Court. (426.)

On June 8, Sinochem sought preservation of a maritime claim and arrest of the vessel in the Guangzhou Admiralty Court. It alleged that Malaysia International had falsely backdated the bill. The Chinese court arrested the vessel that day. Sinochem filed its substantive complaint there on July 2. Malaysia International contested the Chinese court's jurisdiction, but that objection was rejected and the jurisdictional ruling was affirmed on appeal. The Supreme Court did not determine whether Sinochem's backdating allegation was true. (426–427.)

Meanwhile, on June 23, Malaysia International sued Sinochem in the Eastern District of Pennsylvania. It alleged that Sinochem's preservation petition had negligently misrepresented the vessel's fitness and suitability to load cargo, and sought compensation for losses caused by the arrest's delay. The United States action thus concerned alleged misrepresentations to a Chinese court resulting in arrest of a foreign vessel in China, although the cargo had been loaded in Philadelphia. (427–428.)

## Procedure and the sequencing dispute

Sinochem sought dismissal for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction, lack of personal jurisdiction, forum non conveniens, and international comity. The district court found admiralty subject-matter jurisdiction under §1333(1), but concluded that Pennsylvania's long-arm statute did not provide personal jurisdiction. It thought discovery might establish national contacts sufficient under Rule 4(k)(2). Rather than order that discovery, it dismissed for forum non conveniens because the dispute could be heard adequately and more conveniently in China. It subsequently denied reconsideration. (427–428.)

The Third Circuit agreed on subject-matter jurisdiction and the need for discovery to resolve personal jurisdiction. Although it regarded forum non conveniens as a nonmerits ground, the majority held that the district court had to establish both forms of jurisdiction definitively before using it. Judge Stapleton dissented, arguing that unnecessary jurisdictional discovery defeated the doctrine's protection against needless expense. The Supreme Court reviewed the sequencing question and rejected the appellate majority's rule. (428–429.)

## Issue, rule, and holding

Must a federal court conclusively establish its own subject-matter and personal jurisdiction before dismissing in favor of a plainly more suitable foreign forum? No. A court may address a forum non conveniens objection first when convenience, fairness, and judicial economy warrant that course. Such dismissal declines to adjudicate the merits rather than exercising substantive law-declaring power. This discretion does not allow a court to assume jurisdiction and then decide the underlying claim. (425, 430–433.)

## Why a nonmerits dismissal may come first

Steel Co. generally requires jurisdiction before a merits determination. Ruhrgas, however, rejects an inflexible sequence among threshold grounds for declining to hear a case: personal jurisdiction can sometimes be resolved before subject-matter jurisdiction. Sinochem applies the same distinction to forum non conveniens. The court determines that another forum should decide the merits; it does not declare whether the plaintiff's substantive claim succeeds. (430–432.)

A forum non conveniens analysis may require examining claims, evidence, or defenses to identify the appropriate forum. That factual overlap does not transform it into a merits adjudication. Van Cauwenberghe's observation that forum analysis can overlap the dispute concerned immediate appellate review under the collateral-order doctrine. Whether an issue is sufficiently separate for that appellate exception is a different question from whether a threshold dismissal exercises substantive law-declaring power. Personal-jurisdiction analysis itself can also touch facts related to a claim without adjudicating liability. (432–433.)

The Third Circuit relied on Gulf Oil's statements that forum non conveniens presupposes jurisdiction and two available forums. The Supreme Court read those statements in context. Gulf Oil had asked whether an otherwise competent court could nevertheless decline adjudication; it had not addressed whether definitive resolution of the chosen court's jurisdiction was invariably required first. Sinochem did not authorize continued consideration after a court actually determines that it lacks jurisdiction: in that situation it must dismiss on that ground. It permitted avoiding a difficult jurisdictional determination in the circumstances presented. (433–435.)

## Why China made immediate dismissal appropriate

Forum non conveniens ordinarily places a heavy burden on a defendant challenging the plaintiff's forum choice, although the presumption carries less force when the plaintiff chooses a forum outside its home. The doctrine addresses an adequate alternative forum and practical or administrative inconvenience; ordinary transfers between federal districts are governed by statutory provisions instead. (429–430.)

Here, the district court's subject-matter determination raised an issue of first impression, personal-jurisdiction discovery would impose expense and delay, and the dispute was already being litigated in China. Its core allegations concerned representations made to the Chinese admiralty court. The district court's appraisal made dismissal inevitable without reaching the merits, so further jurisdictional litigation would serve little purpose. The Supreme Court nevertheless cautioned that when absence of jurisdiction can readily be determined, that is ordinarily the proper dismissal ground. Taking the forum question first is especially appropriate when jurisdiction is difficult and the forum considerations strongly favor dismissal. (435–436.)

## Reservation, judgment, and assigned correction

The Court expressly left open whether a court must first establish jurisdiction before **conditioning** dismissal on waiver of a limitations defense or objection to the foreign forum's jurisdiction. That concern did not arise here: the Chinese litigation was underway and jurisdiction there had already been determined and affirmed. The decision did not resolve the separate problem posed by a genuinely uncertain alternative forum requiring such conditions. (435.)

The Supreme Court **reversed the Third Circuit's judgment and remanded for proceedings consistent with its opinion**. (436.) The assignment calls this an affirmance of dismissal, which conveys approval of the district court's forum disposition but is not the Supreme Court's precise appellate mandate. Its statement that the district court had not ascertained either form of jurisdiction also needs qualification: the district court had found subject-matter jurisdiction; personal jurisdiction remained unresolved, and the Supreme Court's general rule permits bypassing either issue. (427–428; assigned printed 433.)
