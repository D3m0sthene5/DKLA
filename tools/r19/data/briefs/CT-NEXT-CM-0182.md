# Murray v. ILG Technologies, LLC
378 F. Supp. 3d 1227 (S.D. Ga. March 28, 2019) | Contracts

## Events, agreement, and alleged harm
On September 6, 2016, the Georgia Board of Bar Examiners announced that ninety applicants—forty-five from July 2015 and forty-five from February 2016—had incorrectly received failing results although they had passed. Murray and McGhan were among them. They alleged that errors in ILG's software caused the incorrect scores and notices. Whether ILG's software or another part of the examination, grading, and communication process actually caused the error remained disputed. The court resolved the motions on legal and evidentiary grounds without determining that causation dispute. (1232–1234.)

ILG, owned by Misman, contracted with the Office of Bar Admissions to provide a customized system administering the admission process. OBA supported the character-and-fitness and bar-exam boards. The exam included the MBE and written components; an MBE score below 115 meant automatic failure without essay grading. A combined score of 270 passed, and initial scores from 265 through 269 triggered essay regrading before final release. ILG's system helped organize applications, calculate scores, and communicate results. All communications to the plaintiffs came from OBA and the Board, rather than ILG itself. The plaintiffs were not parties to the OBA–ILG contract. (1234–1235.)

The plaintiffs sought costs of retaking examinations and obtaining study materials, lost income, injury to employment in the legal profession, and reputational injury. They asserted breach of contract as beneficiaries, negligence, negligent misrepresentation, negligent design, strict products liability, and defamation, plus attorney's fees. Their original demand for regrading additional examinations was withdrawn. (1235.)

## Proceedings and decisional standard
The plaintiffs filed a putative class action in Georgia state court; defendants removed it. An initial summary-judgment motion addressed the original causes of action, and a supplemental motion addressed the amended complaint's additional claims. Class certification, plaintiffs' partial-summary-judgment motion, and a request for oral argument were also pending. Judge Baker applied Rule 56, viewing genuinely disputed evidence and reasonable inferences favorably to the nonmovants while requiring affirmative evidence supporting essential elements after defendants discharged their burden. (1232–1233, 1235–1236.)

Georgia law governed the contract under its choice-of-law term and the tort claims under Georgia's place-of-wrong rule; the parties offered no competing substantive law. This brief concerns the district court's March 28 order. The assigned heading cites a later affirmance, but that reference does not make this a brief of a separate appellate opinion. (1236–1237 & n. 3.)

## Beneficiary rule and contract holding
O.C.G.A. § 9-2-20 permits a person to enforce a contract made for that person's benefit despite the ordinary privity requirement. Georgia required the intention to benefit the claimant to appear clearly from the agreement considered as a whole. Beneficiary status attaches to particular contractual provisions: a person may enforce only the provisions intended for that person's benefit. Where the operative writing and its unambiguous language are undisputed, interpretation is a legal question. Parol evidence cannot confer beneficiary status absent the necessary contractual basis. (1237–1238.)

The plaintiffs relied on § 1.1, which promised OBA a complete customized system, including correspondence with applicants and processing of admission data. They argued that accurate communication to applicants was the whole purpose of the undertaking and that the express reference to applicants established intended-beneficiary status. The court held that this provision promised software to OBA, tailored to OBA's administrative duties. Mention of applicants described an aspect of those duties; it did not clearly promise them the specific score-accuracy benefit they sought to enforce. (1238–1239.)

Other provisions promised an applicant communication portal, user support, and confidentiality. The court acknowledged that applicants might be intended beneficiaries of those provisions, but they alleged breach of different provisions concerning scoring for OBA. It therefore did not hold that applicants could never enforce any term of this contract. It rejected their claim to enforce the particular scoring undertaking. Misman's deposition references to beneficiaries would not change the result even if considered: benefit from use was different from the legally required contractual intention. (1239 & n. 4.)

The court analogized to Benator, where water customers were incidental beneficiaries of a public entity's meter-reading/software contract despite suffering overbilling. Natural or practical benefit from correct performance, even accompanied by serious harm from failure, did not establish an intent to confer the specific enforceable benefit. It also reviewed the Olympic-park security and inmate-safety cases. The contract claim failed for lack of privity or qualifying beneficiary status, not because the grading error was harmless. (1240–1241.)

## Economic-loss claims and asserted exceptions
Georgia's economic-loss rule had expanded beyond defective-product and contracting-party cases. Absent an independent legal duty, qualifying personal or property injury, or an applicable exception, a plaintiff could not recover purely economic loss through ordinary negligence. The allegedly breached scoring and reporting duties arose from the OBA contract. A generalized duty not to create unreasonable harm supplied no independent duty on these facts. Strict-products-liability and negligent-design theories did not avoid the same barrier. (1241–1246.)

Reputational damages under the plaintiffs' negligence theories did not establish a qualifying injury where they neither alleged nor supported willful or wanton conduct. They did not seek damages for their headaches, nausea, or weight gain or develop those ailments as an exception to the rule. Those symptoms resulted from emotional distress following economic loss, rather than a directly inflicted physical injury such as toxic exposure. Even a recoverable personal-injury claim would not make retaking costs and lost income recoverable unless those losses resulted from that injury. The claimed right to practice law supplied no protected property injury under the cited Georgia law. (1246–1248 & nn. 11–15.)

The accident exception also failed. It concerned a sudden, calamitous event posing unreasonable danger in the product context. The software undertaking was a service, plaintiffs sought no damages to the alleged product itself, and receipt of an incorrect score was not the physical calamity contemplated by the exception. The misrepresentation exception failed with the separately analyzed misrepresentation claim; note 16 additionally addresses defendants' lack of awareness of the score errors before release. (1249–1250 & n. 16.)

## Misrepresentation, defamation, and remaining claims
Negligent misrepresentation required affirmative false information supplied by defendants, reasonable reliance, and resulting economic injury. Plaintiffs supplied no evidence that they knew of or relied on ILG's contractual representations before receiving the results. They admitted that OBA sent the communications, and offered no legal basis for treating the software platform itself as ILG's affirmative representation. That essential element was absent. (1250–1251 & n. 18.)

For defamation, plaintiffs likewise failed to establish publication by defendants. Assuming software-generated computations could be statements, authorized OBA employees' internal receipt fell within Georgia's intracorporate nonpublication rule. Any actionable communication made in good-faith performance of ILG's private contractual duty to OBA was also privileged under the court's analysis. The subsequent release by OBA did not establish the missing publication element against these defendants. (1251–1253.)

The withdrawn regrading request independently conflicted with Georgia's rule against regrading after general release. Attorney's fees under § 13-6-11 required success on a substantive claim; none survived. (1251, 1253.)

## Exact order and scope
The court granted both defense summary-judgment motions on all claims, denied every other pending motion as moot, directed entry of judgment, and closed the case. It did not certify a class, adjudicate the software-causation dispute, or find no errors had occurred. Baker was the sole district judge; there are no separate writings or panel votes. The entire actual opinion at 1232–1253 and all eighteen judicial notes were read, with the assigned principal on textbook pages 1178–1185. The textbook principally retains the beneficiary analysis and omits the developed tort rulings. Its OCR corrupts the reporter volume; the actual citation is 378 F. Supp. 3d 1227. Historical doctrine and this district-court stage are preserved without certifying current law or the reasoning of the separately cited later appeal.
