# Noll v. eBay, Inc.

**309 F.R.D. 593 (N.D. Cal. 2015) — September 15, 2015 — Civil Procedure**

District Judge Edward J. Davila approved a settlement class and its settlement, awarded attorneys’ fees and expenses, and reduced the requested class-representative payments. This was a single district judge’s order, not an appellate opinion with a numerical vote.

## Transaction and litigation history

Richard Noll and Rhythm Motor Sports, LLC sold items on eBay. They challenged the disclosures concerning recurring fees for Good ’Til Cancelled listings, which renewed every thirty days until ended or sold. eBay charged insertion fees and optional fees for enhancements such as highlighting or bold type. Sellers alleged that the fee schedules did not adequately disclose monthly recurring charges, particularly optional-feature charges. They sought refunds of disputed renewal fees rather than a judicial declaration that every kind of eBay charge was unlawful. (597–600 & n.1.)

Before September 16, 2008, this listing duration was available only in eBay’s Stores marketplace, whose schedule expressly described recurring fees. On that date eBay introduced it in Core and Motors. Core acquired an express recurring-insertion-fee disclosure in December 2008; Motors and Stores received that language in March 2010. In June 2012 eBay added explicit language covering both insertion and optional-feature fees. The opinion’s background paragraphs refer to June 19 and June 20, respectively; the settlement’s defined endpoint was June 19, 2012. These disclosures and differences among marketplaces mattered to the scope and litigation risks of the claims. (598–600.)

The 2011 complaint included contract, consumer-protection, unjust-enrichment, fraud, and declaratory claims. Motions to dismiss narrowed the case, including dismissal of foreign eBay entities and repeated dismissal of fraud-based claims with leave to amend. The court also dismissed the automatic-renewal statutory count with prejudice in 2013. After amended pleadings, discovery, expert work, and a class-certification motion, the parties reached a settlement following two mediations before retired judges. The agreement was executed January 14, 2015; final approval and fee requests were now before the court. Settlement approval did not establish liability on the unresolved underlying claims. (600–601.)

## Agreement and class definition

The class comprised United States residents, including natural persons and entities, who placed qualifying listings and paid disputed fees. The Stores period ran March 30, 2010, through June 19, 2012; Core and Motors ran September 16, 2008, through June 19, 2012. The class excluded the judge and staff and specified eBay insiders and their families. (600.)

The fund was $6.4 million. There was no claims-submission process: active accounts would receive automatic credits, while inactive or closed accounts would receive checks. Net payments were expected to exceed $4.5 million, approximately half of the first renewal month’s relevant fees. Remaining returned or uncashed checks would go equally to two identified nonprofit organizations, rather than revert to eBay. Members released claims asserted or assertable on the action’s allegations. (600–601.)

The report identified 1,192,947 unique user IDs. Footnote 2 expressly cautions that one person may hold multiple accounts and IDs; this number is not a verified count of distinct human class members. The final notice list contained a different number, 1,188,451 records after address analysis. The agreement’s initial size, the processed notice list, and actual receipt must be kept distinct. (600 n.2, 605.)

## Certification and notice

The court applied Rule 23(a)’s numerosity, commonality, typicality, and adequacy requirements, plus Rule 23(b)(3)’s predominance and superiority requirements. Numerosity was undisputed. The common user agreement, schedules, and listing system generated questions capable of resolving central issues across the class. Representatives suffered the same alleged injury and faced defenses typical of the other sellers, including their continued purchases after learning of recurring fees. Uniform allocation, coverage across the relevant periods, experienced counsel, and vigorous investigation supported adequacy. (602–604.)

Common contractual questions predominated for this settlement, and small individual recoveries made separate suits uneconomical. Centralized settlement also avoided duplication and conflicting determinations. Certification was expressly for settlement purposes; the opinion separately acknowledged that proving liability in contested litigation could require individualized evidence of what each seller understood, read, paid, or earned. Settlement certification was not a declaration that those litigation risks had vanished. (604, 606 & n.5.)

Notice combined individual email with a linked settlement website, mailed notices when email information was missing or delivery failed, a press release for people without current addresses, and toll-free support. By early May, the administrator had handled 615 calls and 975 emails. It initially mailed 20,739 notices for missing or invalid email addresses, then mailed notices after 170,746 emails were returned. Further tracing produced 16,635 remailings after returned postal notices. The court reported that 98.57% appeared to have received direct notice; it did not establish actual receipt by every individual. (601 & n.3, 605 & n.4.)

The court approved the implemented plan under the best-practicable-notice standard and Rule 23(e)’s reasonable settlement notice requirement. Reasonable efforts did not guarantee each member’s actual receipt. The opening sentence in the notice discussion prints Rule 23(e)(2)(B), while its accompanying citation correctly identifies Rule 23(c)(2)(B). The ruling concerned the combined email, website, postal, press, and support plan, not approval of an email-only system. (604–605.)

## Fairness and protection against collusion

The court assessed the eight Churchill factors: claim strength, continued-litigation burdens, risks to class status, settlement amount, discovery, counsel’s experience, governmental participation, and class response. Prior claim dismissals and disputed contractual understandings posed real risks. The $6.4 million fund exceeded 71% of first-renewal fees. The court also described the payment as approximately 9% of all renewal fees at issue. Thus describing recovery simply as 71% would select an incomplete denominator. Extensive document and transaction discovery, depositions, experts, and mediated negotiations supported informed compromise. (605–608.)

No state Attorney General responded to the CAFA notice. Ninety-seven exclusions and three objections were filed; one objector was both late and outside the class. The court treated the low response rates and lack of governmental objection as support for approval, not affirmative proof that every absent member had reviewed and endorsed it. (608, 611–612.)

Because settlement preceded formal certification, the court separately examined Bluetooth’s indicators of collusion. Counsel requested the 25% benchmark; there was no fee-based valuation of coupons or other soft relief. Fee and representative awards were not conditions of settlement, and reductions would increase funds for class members. No unused amount would revert to eBay. The court found no collusive arrangement and approved the compromise. (608.)

## Fees, representative awards, and exact order

The court allowed $1.6 million in fees, 25% of the fund, after checking the percentage against a lodestar exceeding $1.035 million for approximately 2,600 hours. Rates and an approximately 1.6 multiplier were reasonable in light of work, risk, and comparable awards. It allowed $72,923.16 in expenses. But the representatives’ requested $15,000 each was not justified as an upward departure; each received $5,000 instead. Their work and risk warranted compensation without compelling the larger sum. (609–611.)

The three objections were overruled. Conditional-payment concerns in Radcliffe did not apply because these awards were not contingent on supporting the settlement. Counsel’s out-of-state location did not invalidate court-approved representation, and the requested fee was exactly 25%. Narkin had no qualifying fee payment in eBay’s records and filed after the deadline. (611–612.)

The final order granted settlement approval and awarded the stated fees, expenses, and $5,000 to each representative, directing closure upon entry of judgment. Despite the conclusion’s general statement that the award motion was granted, the requested $15,000 enhancements were reduced; the opening accurately characterizes that motion as granted in part and denied in part. (597–598, 612.)
