# Cohen v. Cowles Media Co. — contract, First Amendment, and remand decisions

457 N.W.2d 199 (Minn. 1990); 501 U.S. 663 (1991); 479 N.W.2d 387 (Minn. 1992)

## Courts, opinions, and the reason for a combined entry
The casebook assigns two Minnesota Supreme Court opinions from the same litigation: the 1992 promissory-estoppel decision at printed pages 135–136 and the 1990 contract decision at pages 183–185. Between those decisions, the United States Supreme Court decided the First Amendment issue. This entry combines the litigation while keeping the three decisions’ judgments and reasoning separate. Justice Simonett wrote both Minnesota opinions. Justice White wrote the United States Supreme Court's five-Justice majority opinion.

The 1990 state court rejected the contract theory and then treated the First Amendment as an obstacle to promissory estoppel. The 1991 Supreme Court decision removed the federal constitutional obstacle and remanded. The 1992 Minnesota decision sustained the $200,000 compensatory verdict on promissory estoppel. The Supreme Court itself did not award that sum, and it did not hold that a conventional contract had been formed. 457 N.W.2d at 202–05; 501 U.S. at 672; 479 N.W.2d at 388–92.

## Parties and material facts
Dan Cohen worked in advertising and public relations and was associated with the Republican gubernatorial campaign of Wheelock Whitney. On October 27, 1982, near the end of the election campaign, he approached reporters for two Minnesota newspapers with records concerning Marlene Johnson, the opposing candidate for lieutenant governor. The records concerned an earlier unlawful-assembly arrest whose charge had been dismissed and a shoplifting conviction that had been vacated. Cohen wanted the information published but insisted that his identity remain confidential. 457 N.W.2d at 200–01; 501 U.S. at 665–66.

Lori Sturdevant and Bill Salisbury promised anonymity. Neither told Cohen that an editor could revoke the promise. Both intended to honor their undertakings when they made them. Cohen then supplied the material. His concern was retaliation from the press and political actors, rather than a desire to conceal the documents from the newspapers themselves. 457 N.W.2d at 200–01.

The newspapers' editors independently concluded that identifying the source was part of the story. Their editorial staffs debated whether the promise should be honored. The reporters strongly objected to disclosure, and Sturdevant refused to put her name on the resulting story. The newspapers nevertheless identified Cohen when they published the material. His employer then dismissed him. Id. at 201; 501 U.S. at 666.

The record contained unusually strong evidence of a professional practice of honoring confidential-source promises. Reporters, editors, and experts described confidentiality as fundamental to news gathering. Witnesses could not recall comparable occasions when editors overrode such promises. That evidence played different roles in the two Minnesota opinions: in 1990 it supported describing the obligation as ethical, while in 1992 it helped establish the injustice of leaving Cohen without a remedy. 457 N.W.2d at 202–03; 479 N.W.2d at 391–92.

## Procedural history
Cohen sued the newspaper publishers for breach of contract and fraudulent misrepresentation. The jury awarded $200,000 in compensatory damages, jointly and severally, and $250,000 in punitive damages against each publisher. The Minnesota Court of Appeals rejected the fraud theory and punitive awards but sustained contract-based compensation. 457 N.W.2d at 200–02; 501 U.S. at 666.

In 1990, the Minnesota Supreme Court agreed that fraud had not been established and rejected contractual liability. It considered promissory estoppel even though that theory had not been the basis of the trial, but concluded that imposing liability in these circumstances would violate the First Amendment. Cohen consequently lost the compensatory award at that stage. 457 N.W.2d at 202–05.

The United States Supreme Court granted review of the federal question. In 1991 it reversed and remanded, holding that the First Amendment did not prohibit application of the generally applicable promissory-estoppel doctrine to the publishers' confidentiality promises. It left state-law and state-constitutional issues for Minnesota. 501 U.S. at 667–72.

On remand, the Minnesota Supreme Court held on January 24, 1992, that the compensatory verdict could be sustained under promissory estoppel. It declined to order a new trial. A subsequent March 17, 1992 order addressed interest on the reinstated award and denied a request for sanctions and attorney's fees arising from the rehearing petition. The merits opinion is reported at 479 N.W.2d 387; the later order is at 481 N.W.2d 840. The later date should not be mistaken for the original merits decision.

## Issues by decisional stage
The 1990 state case presented whether a promise of confidentiality in this news-gathering setting created a conventional contract; whether a fraudulent promise had been made; and whether promissory-estoppel relief was constitutionally permissible. The 1991 federal case presented the last question: whether the First Amendment prohibited enforcement through a generally applicable state doctrine. The 1992 remand presented whether that doctrine could be considered on this record, whether Minnesota's Constitution or public policy supplied another obstacle, and whether the elements and remedy were established without another trial.

## I. Minnesota Supreme Court, 1990: contract and fraud
### Holding and actual judgment
The court held that the particular confidentiality arrangements were not enforceable as ordinary contracts. It also sustained rejection of fraudulent misrepresentation. Although it recognized a possible promissory-estoppel analysis, it regarded the First Amendment as precluding the proposed liability. The court's mixed affirmance and reversal removed the $200,000 contract-based recovery while leaving the rejection of punitive fraud damages in place. 457 N.W.2d at 202–05.

### No false intent when the promises were made
The reporters intended to honor their promises when they gave them. The decisions to reveal Cohen came later from editors. That record did not establish an initially fraudulent intention not to perform. A later broken promise is not automatically a fraudulent misrepresentation of the promisor's present intent. The punitive awards tied to the failed fraud theory therefore did not survive. Id. at 202.

### A serious ethical promise was not treated as a conventional bargain
Simonett acknowledged that offer, acceptance, consideration, and breach seemingly appeared. The court did not find the promise vague in this record; it expressly recognized a clear undertaking. Its concern was the legal character of a confidential-source arrangement in the professional setting of news gathering. It considered such arrangements ordinarily understood as moral or ethical commitments rather than commercial exchanges creating conventional contractual liability. Id. at 202–03.

The court also reasoned that the reporter receives the information only after promising confidentiality, while the editor must later assess the information within the larger story. Treating the arrangement as an inflexible contract would, in its view, impose legal rigidity on a relationship managed through professional judgment and ethical duties. This was a context-specific justification for rejecting the contract theory, not a holding that all oral promises are unenforceable or that legal intent must always be expressly stated. Id. at 203.

That conclusion is contestable, and the casebook uses it to examine intent to be legally bound. The parties' seriousness and an exchange of information did not, for the majority, establish a conventional contract in this setting. A brief should not resolve the controversy by pretending that the promise was casual, that the reporters reserved an explicit editorial escape clause, or that Cohen never provided anything in exchange. None of those propositions describes the record. Id. at 200–03.

### The constitutional obstacle later removed
The 1990 court considered a promissory-estoppel theory but concluded that enforcing it would burden publication of truthful political information and require inappropriate second-guessing of editorial decisions. That federal constitutional conclusion was subsequently reversed. It remains part of the litigation's history and the explanation for Supreme Court review, not the controlling First Amendment result after 1991. Id. at 203–05; 501 U.S. at 672.

### Separate opinions in 1990
Justice Yetka would have sustained relief under contract or promissory estoppel. He disputed giving the press a special exemption from ordinary responsibility for its promises and emphasized the importance of trustworthy source relationships. Justice Kelley agreed that fraud was absent but objected to the majority's contract analysis, including its displacement of the factual basis for contractual intent; he also joined Yetka's views. These dissents belong to the Minnesota decision. They are not the federal dissents authored a year later. 457 N.W.2d at 205–07.

Chief Justice Popovich took part in oral argument but did not participate in consideration or decision. His appearance in the case's procedural history does not make him a member of the deciding majority. Id. at 205.

## II. United States Supreme Court, 1991: the First Amendment
### Holding and actual judgment
The First Amendment did not prohibit applying state promissory-estoppel law to enforce the publishers' confidentiality promises in these circumstances. The judgment was reversed and remanded for further proceedings. White's opinion was joined by Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justices Stevens, Scalia, and Kennedy. 501 U.S. at 664–65, 672.

The federal judgment neither found every state-law element conclusively satisfied nor restored damages by its own force. Minnesota remained free to consider its own law and Constitution on remand. Equally, the Supreme Court did not revive the already-rejected fraud claim or punitive damages. Id. at 672.

### Jurisdiction and state action
The federal issue was properly reviewable because the Minnesota court had actually decided it and had relied on it in disposing of the claim. The lack of an earlier party presentation of promissory estoppel did not prevent review of the federal question the state court reached. Id. at 667–68.

Judicial enforcement of promissory estoppel also constituted state action. The proposed duty would be imposed by the court through state law even though Minnesota had found no conventional contract. The majority therefore did not dispose of the constitutional issue by calling the dispute wholly private or outside the First Amendment. It addressed the permissible application of the Amendment after recognizing governmental involvement. Id. at 668.

### Generally applicable obligations and the role of the voluntary promise
The press is ordinarily subject to generally applicable laws, including laws imposing obligations that may affect news gathering or publication. Promissory estoppel did not target the press or prescribe a special category of information that newspapers could not publish. The undertaking at issue originated in the confidentiality promises made to obtain Cohen's material. Id. at 669–71.

The majority distinguished decisions restricting punishment for publishing lawfully obtained truthful information. In Cohen, the asserted liability arose from breach of a self-imposed obligation governed by ordinary state law. The Court did not create an unlimited exemption from the First Amendment whenever a plaintiff uses a contract label; it decided that the application of this generally applicable doctrine to these promises imposed no unconstitutional burden. Id.

The majority also rejected an analogy to an attempt to evade the constitutional requirements of a defamation action. Cohen sought compensation for the loss resulting from violation of a confidentiality undertaking, including the loss of his job, rather than damages simply because a truthful publication injured his reputation. The cause of action had an independent promise-based foundation. Id. at 671–72.

### Blackmun's dissent
Justice Blackmun, joined by Justices Marshall and Souter, argued that the general applicability of a law does not eliminate constitutional scrutiny when liability directly burdens protected expression. The act triggering liability was publication of truthful information of public concern. In his view, that was a direct burden on speech, not merely an incidental consequence of ordinary regulation of unrelated conduct. He relied on the principle that tort labels cannot avoid constitutional protection for expression. Id. at 672–76.

### Souter's dissent
Justice Souter, joined by Justices Marshall, Blackmun, and O'Connor, likewise rejected general applicability as sufficient to resolve the case. He emphasized the public's interest in information about the identity and motives of a political source. That public interest is not entirely within the newspaper's private power to waive through a promise. He would balance the state's interest in enforcement against the specific value of the expression, and regarded the political information here as protected. Id. at 676–79.

Souter did not say that no confidentiality promise could ever be enforced. His analysis depended on the public importance of the information and allowed that other circumstances could produce a different result. Keeping that qualification distinguishes his approach from an absolute press immunity. Id.

## III. Minnesota Supreme Court, 1992: liability and remedy on remand
### Holding and actual judgment
With the federal obstacle removed, the state court sustained the $200,000 compensatory verdict under promissory estoppel. It found a clear promise, intended and actual detrimental reliance, and circumstances requiring enforcement to avoid injustice. It rejected the asserted alternative barriers based on Minnesota's Constitution and public policy. The damages verdict could stand without a new trial because the record and instructions supplied the necessary basis. 479 N.W.2d at 388–92.

The result changed the theory supporting the award. It did not declare the 1990 ordinary-contract analysis reversed by the Supreme Court or transform the reporters' initial good faith into fraud. No separate concurrence or dissent is reported with the 1992 merits opinion.

### Considering a theory not tried below
The publishers objected that promissory estoppel had not been pleaded and tried as the basis of liability. The court used its authority under Minnesota's appellate rules to consider an issue in the interests of justice. The case presented an unusual first-impression problem closely related to the contract theory already litigated. The relevant promise, reliance, and injury were in the record, and the parties had opportunities to address the theory. Id. at 389–90.

That reasoning does not establish a routine entitlement to change theories after trial. The court explained why this case justified doing so without unfairness or a new factual proceeding. The procedural ruling is part of the actual route to the award and should not disappear from a brief focused only on section 90. Id.

### State constitutional and public-policy objections
Minnesota could afford protection under its own Constitution beyond the federal minimum. The court nevertheless declined to adopt the publishers' proposed broader protection in this case. It also rejected the contention that a public policy favoring truthful reporting required refusal to enforce the promise. The relevant question was not whether truthful political reporting is valuable in general, but whether it required exposing Cohen in violation of the particular undertaking. Id. at 390–91.

### Why enforcement was necessary to avoid injustice
The court treated the injustice requirement as a legal question involving policy. It found the newspapers' own professional standards especially significant. Their witnesses described honoring confidentiality as central to journalism, and the reporters who made the promises insisted that they should have been honored. Cohen had relied on that established practice when deciding to reveal the records. Id. at 391–92.

The newspapers argued that identifying Cohen completed the story. The court responded that describing the source as someone close to the opposing campaign could have supplied important context without revealing his name. It did not declare Cohen morally blameless or deny the political motives of his disclosure. It concluded that, absent a compelling need to break this promise, the resulting harm required a remedy. Id.

### Why the existing award could stand
The original damages instructions required the jury to connect compensation to harm flowing directly and foreseeably from the broken undertaking. The state court found the existing $200,000 award adequate under the promissory-estoppel theory and unnecessary to retry. Thus the final compensatory recovery was neither the automatic value of any promise nor an unexamined revival of punitive damages. It rested on the record and verdict already produced. Id. at 392.

The later rehearing order remanded questions concerning interest to the trial court and denied the request for sanctions and attorney's fees in response to the rehearing petition. Those collateral directions did not replace the merits holding that the $200,000 compensation was sustainable. 481 N.W.2d at 840.

## Study synthesis and source cautions
The litigation demonstrates three different inquiries: whether ordinary contract law recognizes the arrangement, whether a reliance-based doctrine supplies an alternative, and whether a constitutional rule prevents that doctrine's application. The first Minnesota opinion answered the first inquiry negatively. The federal decision removed a proposed obstacle to the second. The final state decision then applied that doctrine and sustained the remedy. Combining those steps into “the Supreme Court enforced the contract for $200,000” is incorrect.

The case also shows how the same evidence may matter differently under different legal theories. A professional tradition of honoring promises may fail, in a court's view, to prove intended conventional contractual obligation, yet strongly support foreseeable reliance and injustice. The 1990 dissents expose the disputed nature of the first conclusion. The 1991 dissents expose a separate disagreement over constitutional protection. Neither set should be presented as dissenting from the 1992 state application of promissory estoppel.
