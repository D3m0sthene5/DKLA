# Opera Company of Boston, Inc. v. Wolf Trap Foundation for the Performing Arts
817 F.2d 1094 (4th Cir. 1987) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
The Fourth Circuit decided this appeal on May 4, 1987, after argument November 13, 1986. Judge Donald Russell wrote for himself and Judge Hall. District Judge McMillan, sitting by designation, concurred in the account of impossibility doctrine but dissented from the disposition. The majority vacated a judgment requiring Wolf Trap to pay for a canceled opera performance and remanded for findings about the likelihood and allocation of the power-failure risk. Although the opinion also describes the judgment as reversed, its concluding disposition reads vacated and remanded with instructions. It did not finally hold that Wolf Trap owed nothing. (817 F.2d at 1094–1095, 1103–1105.)

## The contract and the storm
The Opera Company promised four fully staged, orchestrally accompanied performances meeting its normal standards on June 12–15, 1980, at the Filene Center in Wolf Trap National Park. Wolf Trap promised $272,000: $20,000 at signing, $40,000 on April 1, and the balance in four equal installments before each curtain rose. It also provided the venue and lighting equipment specified by the company's lighting designer. The federal National Park Service owned and controlled the park, including parking, roads, and paths beyond the performance area. The covered auditorium seated approximately 3,500 and the lawn another 3,000; audience members walked hundreds of yards from parking to the center. (Id. at 1095 & nn.1–2.)

The first three sold-out performances went ahead. On June 15, a hot, humid, rainy day, a severe thunderstorm around 6:00–6:30 p.m. interrupted electricity throughout the park. The utility predicted that power could not resume until after eleven and possibly not until morning. Approximately 3,000 people had arrived and 3,500 more were expected before the eight-o'clock performance. Park Service and Wolf Trap representatives considered alternatives. The Park Service recommended immediate cancellation to permit safe departure and stop others arriving, disclaiming responsibility for public and performer safety if the performance proceeded. Wolf Trap agreed. An Opera Company representative attended some discussions, did not participate in the cancellation decision, and voiced no objection; the majority later emphasized that this was not affirmative agreement. (Id. at 1095–1096, 1103.)

Wolf Trap withheld the final payment. Five years later the Opera Company sued for the balance, maintaining that it had been ready, willing, and able to perform but was prevented by cancellation. Wolf Trap asserted impossibility. It did not pursue an Act of God defense on appeal; the court expressly distinguished that unasserted theory. (Id. at 1095–1096 & n.3.)

## Trial reasoning and the appellate issue
The district court considered the stipulated contract, the amount in dispute, and a contemporaneous memorandum by Wolf Trap official Craig Hankenson. It found a complete power failure, public-safety concerns, questionable ability to provide adequate lighting for both the theater and backstage movement, and the Opera Company's readiness to perform. It understood Wolf Trap's obligation as furnishing enough lighting for the performance. Prior outages made an interruption affecting a performance foreseeable, even though none had previously done so. On that basis the court rejected impossibility and entered judgment for the company. The sole appellate issue was whether that rejection applied the proper legal standard. (Id. at 1096.)

## Foreseeability, basic assumptions, and allocated risk
Russell traced the movement from strict enforcement despite unexpected impediments to relief when a necessary person or thing becomes unavailable. The older explanation invoked an implied condition; the opinion discussed commentators' criticism that this merely restated rather than explained the judicial choice. It treated modern impossibility or impracticability as an equitable doctrine concerned with a change so severe that enforcing the promise as written would be unreasonable. The court used Virginia decisions, federal precedents, the Restatement, and the UCC as doctrinal support, rather than applying sales law directly to the opera agreement. (Id. at 1097–1100 & nn.4–10.)

A promisor could not rely merely on its personal inability. Note 8 distinguished objective impracticability from subjective inability and required attention to available alternative performance and whether its cost was genuinely excessive. The majority's summary required an unexpected intervening event, whose nonoccurrence was a basic assumption of the agreement, making performance impracticable. Substantial interference and the risks assumed under the contract remained part of the inquiry. (Id. at 1099 & n.8, 1100, 1102.)

The important distinction was between a contingency being conceivable and its risk having been assigned to the promisor. Even a reasonably foreseeable or foreseen event did not necessarily defeat discharge. Parties cannot always negotiate terms for every possible contingency; an event's degree of likelihood and commercial significance mattered. The court rejected an absolute foreseeability bar, while preserving foreseeability as evidence bearing on reasonable risk allocation. Note 14 addressed apparently contrary language in other decisions and explained why the majority did not read those authorities as requiring the absolute rule. (Id. at 1100–1102 & nn.11–14.)

## Why further findings were required
The majority agreed that electricity was necessary to satisfactory performance and that the findings supported impracticability, subject to the unresolved risk issue. The district judge had stopped at foreseeability instead of deciding whether this failure was sufficiently likely that Wolf Trap should have guarded against it or negotiated protection. The court therefore required findings with reasons concerning that degree of likelihood and allowed the parties to offer further evidence on remand. (Id. at 1102–1103.)

Responding to McMillan, Russell stressed that the danger extended beyond a dark stage. Auxiliary service available through the park might have supported the stage and perhaps dressing rooms, but arranging it would have delayed the start until ten or eleven, without illuminating park roads and paths. Thousands of people could remain stranded in a wooded park during a lightning storm. Wolf Trap leased the pavilion area, and its possible duty to supply stage lighting did not itself establish a duty to furnish emergency lighting throughout the park. Its failure to supply that wider system did not, in the majority's view, automatically foreclose the defense. These observations did not replace the ordered findings about the actual risk. (Id. at 1103.)

## McMillan's disagreement
McMillan accepted the majority's doctrinal discussion but thought the trial judge had reached the correct result. Lighting was an essential, priced component of Wolf Trap's undertaking. Hankenson's memorandum described a relatively inexpensive switch-over arrangement for minimal theater lighting and sound, potential access to a civil-defense portable generator, and a preferable permanent backup system. It also referred to earlier outages and Hankenson's warning to the theater board chairman the previous summer that a theater without emergency capacity was incomplete. McMillan regarded this as supporting a conclusion that the interruption was a known, practical risk rather than an excusing impossibility. (Id. at 1103–1105.)

An express power-failure exemption could have been written into the contract, but was not. McMillan would not supply the omitted defense and would affirm the trial judgment. His disagreement concerned applying the agreed doctrine to this record and the weight of the contingency and precautions, rather than denying the doctrine altogether. (Id. at 1104–1105.)
