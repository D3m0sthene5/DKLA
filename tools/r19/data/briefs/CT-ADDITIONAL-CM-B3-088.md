# Kehm Corp. v. United States
93 F. Supp. 620 (Ct. Cl. 1950), November 7, 1950 | Contracts

## Court and limited proceeding
Judge Howell wrote for the United States Court of Claims; Chief Judge Jones and Judges Madden, Whitaker, and Littleton concurred. The five judges agreed, and the full published report contains no judicial footnote or separate writing. Rule 39(b) limited this phase to whether the Government was liable for any part of Kehm’s claimed additional expenses. The contract price had already been paid. The requested $21,737.94 was a claim, not the amount awarded in this ruling. (620–622, 625.)

## Contract, production sequence, and Government delays
On October 8, 1943, Kehm agreed to manufacture 2,800 concrete practice bombs for the Navy: 2,500 weighing 100 pounds and 300 weighing 1,000 pounds. Its Fort Lauderdale plant made the smaller bombs and its Miami plant the larger ones. The Government was to supply tail assemblies, and completed bombs were due within 45 days, by November 22. The written agreement did not specify the tail type or supply dates. Practice tails had to be cast into the concrete, whereas service tails could be attached after casting. Although early negotiations contemplated practice tails, before execution the Navy told Kehm it wanted and would supply service tails. The Navy had ordered 500 practice tails in September but had not yet ordered service tails for the smaller bombs. (621–622.)

On October 16 it delivered the practice tails. Kehm had begun making molds for service-tail bombs; a week was lost clarifying whether the delivery was a mistake or a change of plan. The Navy again promised service tails for most production but directed use of the practice tails already delivered. Kehm made 500 smaller bombs by the deadline. On November 19 it received another 500 smaller-bomb practice tails and 500 larger-bomb service tails; all 300 larger bombs were ready by the end of December. No further tails arrived until February 23, 1944, when 2,050 smaller-bomb service tails were delivered. They had not been ordered until November 12, with an additional shipping order issued January 25. Casting finished at Fort Lauderdale by March 10, after which remaining smaller bombs were moved to Miami, some with tails still to be attached there. (622.)

Repeated assurances that service tails were imminent, combined with deliveries of the other type, created uncertainty affecting casting. Kehm completed the bombs within 45 days after receiving the relevant tails. A second delay concerned acceptance. Trucks could not safely be dispatched without the receiving Navy supply officers’ approval, because they might return loaded. By the end of January only 739 smaller and 96 larger bombs had been delivered. The Navy refused further deliveries until a March 21 amendment redirected the balance from Fort Lauderdale to Miami; later shipments followed the Miami officer’s schedule, ending April 12. The Navy had lost interest in the program and had difficulty finding storage for unwanted bombs. The opinion ties that loss of interest particularly to delayed acceptance, while separately detailing the earlier supply confusion and tardy procurement. (622–623.)

Kehm filed its additional-expense claim April 8, for a period ending April 7. The contracting officer sustained its contentions on July 10. The Comptroller General disallowed the claim in January 1945 and reaffirmed that decision in September because unliquidated damages required judicial determination. The court now addressed breach rather than computing recovery. April 7, rather than the last delivery date, remained the damages-period endpoint for this claim. (622–623.)

## Implied cooperation and the Foley distinction
A promise to fabricate goods by a deadline using materials the customer will furnish ordinarily implies complementary duties to provide those materials sufficiently early and to accept completed goods without undue delay. Cooperation can require affirmative action necessary to make performance possible, not merely avoidance of active obstruction. Such an implication binds as effectively as an express term. (623.)

The Government invoked United States v. Howard P. Foley Co., involving delayed availability of an airport site. Kehm explained that the existence of an implied timely-cooperation duty depended on the contract’s circumstances. Foley involved governmental diligence and a setting in which both parties knew dredging, settling, and grading could cause delay. Here Kehm expected to begin immediately, was prepared to meet the 45-day schedule, and had no contextual warning that the Navy would delay ordering tails or refuse completed deliveries. The bulk of the tails was not even ordered until ten days before the required completion date. The usual implication therefore applied, and the Government offered no adequate explanation excusing its failures. (624.)

Service-tail bombs could have been cast before their tails arrived, but the changing tail deliveries understandably made Kehm reluctant to cast until it knew which type would be supplied. Whether that reluctance was justified affected mitigation and the extent of loss, not whether the Government technically breached its supply obligation. The court reserved that question for the damages phase. (624.)

## Extension and changes provisions; exact result
A contract provision extending performance time for Government-imposed delay did not necessarily make an extension the exclusive remedy. Together with particular surrounding facts it could indicate that delay was anticipated and allocated without a damages promise, as in Foley. But absent those circumstances, a Government drafter intending immunity from delay damages ordinarily would say so expressly. The mere presence of an extension provision did not erase the implied duty here. (624–625.)

Likewise, the right to make changes did not justify waiting until February to furnish materials required in October or November. The Government could change the destination but could not force Kehm to store completed bombs for weeks while choosing the least inconvenient receiving location. The breach-damages claim could not be finally settled under the cited administrative contract articles simply because the contracting officer had considered it. (625.)

The court held that the Government’s delays breached the contract, prevented timely performance, and caused some damage. Kehm was entitled to recover loss actually attributable to those delays, but still bore the burden of proving its nature and amount. Further proceedings would determine contractor-caused loss and actual or reasonably available mitigation, including the significance of other contract work performed during the delay. No final dollar recovery was fixed. (625.)
