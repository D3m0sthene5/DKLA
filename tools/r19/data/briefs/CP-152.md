# Allen v. McCurry

## Citation

Allen v. McCurry, 449 U.S. 90 (1980). Supreme Court of the United States. Argued October 8, 1980; decided December 9, 1980. Justice Stewart delivered the Court's opinion, joined by Chief Justice Burger and Justices White, Powell, Rehnquist, and Stevens. Justice Blackmun dissented, joined by Justices Brennan and Marshall. Full judicial opinion: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-449/pdf/USREPORTS-449-90.pdf.

## Parties

Willie McCurry, previously a defendant in a Missouri criminal prosecution, was the plaintiff in the later federal civil-rights action and respondent in the Supreme Court. He sought $1 million in damages from officers Allen and Jacobsmeyer, other unidentified officers, the City of St. Louis, and its police department. The petitioning officers asserted that an issue resolved against McCurry in the criminal proceeding could not be relitigated in the damages suit. The state prosecutor and the individual officer defendants were distinct actors; the doctrine under examination was issue preclusion, not a simple identity of the two lawsuits. 449 U.S. at 91–94.

## Material Facts

Undercover officers went to McCurry's house after an informant reported heroin dealing. Allen and Jacobsmeyer asked to purchase heroin. McCurry returned from inside the house firing a pistol, seriously wounding them. Following a gun battle and his surrender, officers entered without a warrant, purportedly to search for other people. They seized contraband in plain view and additional evidence from dresser drawers and tires on the porch. The Supreme Court took this factual account from the court of appeals; it was not conducting a new evidentiary hearing. Id. at 92 & n.1. 

In the criminal proceeding, McCurry moved to suppress the seized evidence under the Fourth and Fourteenth Amendments. The state judge excluded the material found in the drawers and tires, but admitted the evidence in plain view. The state courts treated entry into the house as justified by emergency circumstances while rejecting part of the subsequent seizure. A jury convicted McCurry of heroin possession and assault with intent to kill, and the conviction was affirmed. Id. at 91–93 & n.2. 

His later federal complaint alleged a conspiracy to violate his Fourth Amendment rights, an unconstitutional seizure of his house, and an assault after arrest and handcuffing. Those allegations were not all necessarily resolved by the suppression ruling. McCurry argued that even if the adverse portion of that ruling had preclusive effect, he could seek damages for conduct the state court had found unlawful. The officers disputed the complaint's scope. The Supreme Court expressly left those issue-specific questions unresolved. Id. at 92–93 nn.2–3.

## Procedural History

The federal district court understood the complaint principally as challenging the search and seizure and granted summary judgment to the officers based on collateral estoppel. The Eighth Circuit reversed. It acknowledged the ordinary applicability of preclusion in civil-rights cases but reasoned that McCurry needed access to a federal forum because Stone v. Powell foreclosed federal habeas review of a Fourth Amendment claim where a full and fair state opportunity had existed. It directed proceedings unencumbered by collateral estoppel, subject to its separate abstention instruction concerning remaining state review. Id. at 93–94 & n.4. 

The Supreme Court reversed the Eighth Circuit and remanded. It rejected the proposed exception based on the unavailability of habeas relief. It did not decide how Missouri preclusion law, § 1738, or ordinary qualifications to collateral estoppel applied to each allegation in this complaint. The remand therefore was not a merits determination that every officer acted lawfully or an unconditional reinstatement of summary judgment on every claim. Id. at 105 & n.25.

## Issue

Does 42 U.S.C. § 1983 prevent application of ordinary issue-preclusion principles to a constitutional issue previously litigated in state criminal proceedings? In particular, does the absence of a federal habeas remedy for McCurry's Fourth Amendment claim entitle him to relitigate that issue without preclusion in a federal damages action? Id. at 91–92, 96–97, 102–105.

## Rule and Holding

Section 1983 creates a federal cause of action but does not categorically displace conventional preclusion principles or the full-faith-and-credit obligations of 28 U.S.C. § 1738. Federal courts generally must give state judgments the preclusive effect the rendering state's courts would give them. A party must have had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the issue, and the other applicable conditions and exceptions remain relevant. Id. at 94–101 & n.7. 

Stone v. Powell's limitation on federal habeas relief does not create an exception allowing unrestricted relitigation through § 1983. Neither the Constitution nor § 1983 guarantees a fresh district-court hearing of every federal issue already decided after an adequate state proceeding. The Court held that the Eighth Circuit's categorical reason for withholding estoppel was erroneous; it did not hold that a full and fair opportunity is the only element needed to establish estoppel. Id. at 102–105, 95 n.7.

## Judgment

The judgment of the Eighth Circuit was reversed, and the case was remanded for proceedings consistent with the opinion. The Supreme Court left the scope and application of preclusion to particular issues for further consideration. It did not decide McCurry's entitlement to damages for the suppressed evidence, conspiracy, or alleged post-arrest assault. Id. at 93 nn.2–3, 105 n.25.

## Reasoning

The Court first described the different reach of claim and issue preclusion. A final merits judgment can bar another action on the same claim, including grounds that could have been presented. Issue preclusion concerns a factual or legal matter actually decided and necessary to the earlier judgment when it arises in a later claim. McCurry's case concerned the latter use of the state suppression determination. The Court expressly reserved questions about federal issues that could have been raised but were not raised in earlier litigation. Id. at 94–95 & n.5, 97 n.10. 

Finality serves more than docket economy. It protects reliance on judgments, avoids inconsistent determinations, and limits the burden of repeated litigation. When the first judgment comes from a state court, respecting it also supports the relationship between state and federal tribunals. Congress reinforced those principles through § 1738, which requires recognition of state judicial proceedings according to their effect in the rendering state. A general preference for a federal forum cannot simply override that instruction. Id. at 94–96. 

The text of § 1983 did not address preclusion or purport to repeal the predecessor of § 1738. It supplied a remedy against persons acting under color of state law who caused deprivations of federal rights. Creating that remedy did not answer what effect an existing judgment should have in a later action invoking it. Because repeal by implication is disfavored, the majority required stronger evidence of displacement than the statute's silence and broad remedial purpose. Id. at 97–99. 

The majority acknowledged that Congress enacted the 1871 civil-rights legislation against a history of serious failures by state institutions to protect federal rights. It nevertheless read the history as establishing supplementary federal protection rather than stripping competent state adjudications of all effect. Congress retained state jurisdiction over federal questions while enlarging federal jurisdiction. The majority found no sufficient indication that an issue fairly heard and decided in state court could always be retried merely because the claimant preferred a federal tribunal or believed the first ruling erroneous. Id. at 98–101. 

The existing full-and-fair-opportunity limitation addressed important concerns about state procedures. A proceeding incapable of adequately hearing a constitutional claim, or one that refused to recognize the applicable constitutional principle, could present grounds for denying preclusion. But the Court distinguished those circumstances from ordinary alleged error within an otherwise adequate adjudication. It did not create a rule immunizing defective procedures from review, nor did it make disagreement with a state decision an automatic right to relitigate. Id. at 100–101. 

The Court rejected the Eighth Circuit's reliance on Stone. That case addressed the costs and benefits of the exclusionary rule in federal habeas proceedings. It did not amend § 1983 or determine the preclusive effect of state judgments. Habeas and damages actions serve different functions and operate under different statutory conditions. The inability to obtain one remedy therefore did not justify transforming another into a substitute free of its otherwise applicable defenses. Id. at 102–105 & n.24. 

The majority also rejected a constitutional premise that every federal-right claimant must receive one unrestricted district-court adjudication. Article III leaves Congress substantial authority over lower federal jurisdiction, and state courts are obligated to enforce federal law. A criminal defendant's involuntary presence in state court did not alone remove the normal effect of issues actually litigated there. Id. at 103–105. 

The opinion carefully limited its result. It rejected Blackmun's suggestion that it had fashioned a new, unusually strict preclusion rule based on a single factor. Ordinary identity-of-issue requirements, exceptions, and state-law questions were still open. The Court's conclusion was about the compatibility of § 1983 with conventional estoppel, not a conclusive application of all those rules to McCurry's entire complaint. Id. at 95 n.7, 105 n.25.

## Separate Opinions

Justice Blackmun, joined by Brennan and Marshall, would have affirmed. He did not reject preclusion in every § 1983 action. He argued that its use must be compatible with the federal remedial purpose of the 1871 legislation and that the majority gave insufficient weight to Congress's decision to interpose federal courts against failures of state justice. In his view, the remedial history supported more than a right to federal court only when state procedures were facially deficient. Id. at 105–112 (Blackmun, J., dissenting). 

Blackmun stressed that modern nonmutual issue preclusion differed from the doctrines familiar when Congress enacted § 1983. At that time, mutuality and narrower definitions of claims and issues would generally have prevented officers who were not parties to the prosecution from using the criminal ruling in this way. He therefore questioned treating congressional silence as approval of the expanded modern defense. Id. at 113–115. 

He also distinguished the institutional setting of suppression from a civil damages action. Excluding reliable evidence may affect a criminal prosecution's truth-seeking function, whereas damages do not directly set aside the conviction. A criminal defendant faces strong pressure to assert every available defense and cannot realistically be described as freely choosing to submit federal rights to the state forum. Forcing a choice between a suppression defense and a later federal damages hearing was, in his assessment, inconsistent with the remedy Congress created. Id. at 115–116. 

The majority responded that ordinary preclusion remained qualified, that England's reservation rule concerned a different federal-abstention setting, and that involuntary participation in a criminal proceeding supplied no categorical statutory exception. The disagreement thus concerned how the remedial statute should interact with modern estoppel and the significance of the first proceeding's setting. Id. at 101 n.17, 104 & n.23.

## Notes

The state court only partly rejected McCurry's Fourth Amendment position. Describing the suppression motion as wholly denied would erase the reason some damages issues might survive. Likewise, the federal complaint included alleged conduct that the district court may have overlooked. The Supreme Court explicitly preserved those matters for the ordinary preclusion analysis on remand. Id. at 92–93 nn.2–3, 105 n.25. 

The rule is not that every state conviction defeats a later § 1983 claim. Identify the rendering state's preclusion law, the precise issue actually and necessarily decided, the opportunity to litigate, and any relevant exception. Allen rejects one broad statutory escape from that analysis; it does not supply every answer within it. Id. at 94–96 & n.7. 

The casebook centers on § 1738, § 1983, and the competing views of the federal forum. The full opinion's footnotes supply essential limits on the disposition. This historical brief does not attempt to incorporate every later doctrine governing civil-rights claims related to convictions or current habeas practice. Id. at 93 n.2, 94 n.5, 97 n.10, 105 n.25.
