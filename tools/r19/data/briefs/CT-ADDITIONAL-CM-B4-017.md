# Saporoso v. Aetna Life and Casualty Company
221 Conn. 356, 603 A.2d 1160 (1992) | Contracts

## Court and result
The Connecticut Supreme Court released its decision on March 3, 1992. Justice Shea wrote for himself and Justices Callahan and Glass. Justice Berdon, joined by Justice Borden, concurred in the result while criticizing the procedural rule limiting appellate review. All five agreed to affirm the directed verdict for the defendants. The majority found no plain error in the pleading ruling, the treatment of the emotional-distress tort claim, or rejection of contract damages for continuing psychiatric disability. The contract analysis depended on the evidence of foreseeability when employment resumed in 1982, rather than establishing that an employer’s wrongful discharge could never cause a psychiatric injury. (356–358, 363, 370–374.)

## Facts and trial posture
Eugenia Saporoso worked for Aetna from 1966 until 1974, left to give birth, and returned in September 1982 to its employee-benefits claim department. In 1984 she moved into the NSF collection department supervised by Laura Larese. Viewing the trial evidence most favorably to Saporoso, her satisfactory evaluations deteriorated after January 1985, when Larese became unduly critical, required work through lunch, and blamed her for tasks assigned to others. A written warning in February led her to seek help from human resources and the company infirmary for nervousness, headaches, shaking, and stomach problems. (358–359.)

Her physician advised rest. During March and April, medical examinations documented difficulty speaking, nosebleeds, a choking sensation, headaches, insomnia, and stomach trouble, with certifications that she could not return to work. Larese continued calling and writing about her absence, sometimes accusing her of malingering. At Larese’s insistence, an Aetna-selected orthopedist examined her in May, found no orthopedic problem, and referred her to a specialist who recommended remaining out until June 18. Nevertheless, relying on the orthopedist’s report, Larese demanded that she return June 5 and warned of termination. Aetna terminated her when she did not appear. These are the evidentiary circumstances the court assumed favorably to her when reviewing the directed verdict, not jury findings against Aetna. (359–360.)

Her condition worsened after discharge. A psychiatrist attributed anxiety, depression, and physical symptoms to her employment and termination and found her totally disabled. Following a contested formal hearing, a workers’ compensation commissioner awarded total-disability benefits from March 6, 1985, until the disability was cured or diminished. The commissioner found no evidence contradicting the disability’s relationship to work and found that Aetna had unreasonably contested liability and delayed payment. (360–361, 367 & n.9.)

The civil complaint asserted intentional infliction of emotional distress and breach of the employment contract; her husband’s consortium counts were derivative. She withdrew statutory workers’ compensation-discrimination and unfair-trade-practices counts before trial. After several weeks of trial, the court directed a defendants’ verdict on the remaining claims. Saporoso appealed without first moving to set the verdict aside. (358 & nn.1–3, 361.)

## Why review was limited to plain error
Under the majority’s reading of General Statutes §52-228b and the applicable practice rules, a motion to set aside was necessary for full appellate review of a monetary verdict, including a directed verdict and challenges to rulings made during trial. The majority rejected a distinction between evidentiary insufficiency and already-decided legal rulings: the statutory text did not supply that exception. Posttrial reconsideration could allow correction without appeal, explain rulings more clearly, improve counsel’s presentation, and encourage settlement. (361–363 & nn.4–5.)

Because Saporoso conceded plain error was the governing standard, the majority asked whether an obvious error so affected fairness, integrity, and public confidence that this extraordinary review was warranted. None of her claims met that standard. This procedural setting limits how broadly the affirmance should be read. (363.)

## Amendment and the tort claim
Saporoso contended on appeal that a proposed July 1990 amendment had automatically superseded the earlier operative complaint under Practice Book §176 because no timely objection was filed. The filing chronology was unclear: the transcript showed the trial judge knew of the request before trial, while the docket reflected a later filing. The amendment would substantially expand the allegations to include events connected to termination. At trial she submitted the request for a ruling, acknowledged its denial, and never argued automatic adoption. The judge could have refused a midtrial amendment likely to delay proceedings while defendants prepared for new allegations. Those circumstances prevented her from obtaining plain-error reversal on the newly advanced theory. (363–365 & nn.6–7.)

The operative emotional-distress count alleged wrongful acts during January through March 1985, before the June discharge. She conceded that the distress caused by those employment-period acts was the injury for which she received compensation. The contested award conclusively determined its employment relationship, and the majority applied collateral estoppel together with workers’ compensation exclusivity to that pleaded claim. It declined to reconsider established precedent about intentional workplace torts through plain-error review. It did not treat an unaccepted amendment alleging later termination-related conduct as part of the claim actually tried. (365–368 & nn.8–9.)

The majority also expressly distinguished Larese’s position. The fellow-employee provision contained exceptions for willful or malicious wrongs and certain motor-vehicle negligence. Moreover, Larese was not a party to the compensation proceeding and could not invoke that award’s collateral-estoppel effect. Saporoso, however, advanced no appellate claim seeking liability against Larese apart from Aetna. The decision therefore does not establish that the supervisor independently obtained preclusion merely because the employer did. (368 n.10.)

## Contract damages: causation did not establish foreseeability
For the contract count, the trial court assumed termination might constitute breach but found no recoverable loss. If she had remained on sick leave for the plan’s maximum twenty-six months and received the other benefits available upon disability termination, benefits would total $23,107.73. She had instead received $63,132.75 in compensation after discharge, plus employer-paid medical expenses. The sick-pay plan undisputedly required compensation payments to be deducted from sick pay. On that calculation, the promised benefits did not yield an unpaid net contractual entitlement. (369.)

Saporoso proposed a different measure: her termination prolonged the disabling condition, prevented return to work, and deprived her of earnings and benefits for her remaining career, exceeding compensation benefits. The Supreme Court accepted that there was enough evidence for a jury to find both the causal connection and that resumed employment would have produced greater earnings. It rejected those losses on a different ground. Under Restatement (Second) of Contracts §351, probable consequences had to be reasonably foreseeable when the agreement was made. Nothing in this record reasonably supported a finding that, when she returned in 1982, Aetna should have foreseen a continuing disabling psychiatric disorder if her employment were later terminated, with or without cause. Thus, proof of post-discharge causation did not itself establish the contractual foreseeability necessary to recover those future losses. No plain error appeared in directing the contract verdict. (369–370.)

## Berdon and Borden’s concurrence
Berdon agreed to affirm only because the plaintiff conceded the restrictive standard. He accepted requiring a posttrial motion where the trial judge had not yet ruled on an alleged error, such as verdict insufficiency or improper amount. He opposed extending it to legal rulings already carefully considered, especially a verdict the judge had directed after extensive briefing and oral argument. In that setting, a redundant motion repeated the same issues after the jury was discharged and could not save the existing trial. He also criticized the rule’s extension to nonjury cases. The concurrence disputed the procedural restriction, not the disposition under the standard the plaintiff accepted. (370–374 & concurrence nn.1–3.)
