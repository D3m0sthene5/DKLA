# National Labor Relations Board v. Bell Aerospace Co., Division of Textron, Inc.
416 U.S. 267 (1974) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Judgment, votes, and questions left open

The Supreme Court affirmed the Second Circuit insofar as it held that the NLRA excludes all employees properly classified as managerial, rather than only managerial personnel with a labor-relations conflict of interest. It reversed the requirement that the Board use rulemaking to determine whether Bell's particular buyers actually belonged in that excluded category. The case went back to the Second Circuit with directions to remand to the Board for reconsideration under the correct standard. The Court expressly declined to decide whether these buyers were managerial. Thus the judgment neither validated the Board's existing bargaining order nor permanently denied these buyers bargaining rights. (289–291, 295.)

Powell wrote for Chief Justice Burger, Douglas, Blackmun, and Rehnquist. White, joined by Brennan, Stewart, and Marshall, dissented from the categorical managerial exclusion but agreed that the Board was not required to resort to rulemaking in deciding the case. The exclusion was a five-to-four holding; rejection of a mandatory rulemaking requirement had agreement from all nine. That agreement did not decide whether the Board's earlier, legally erroneous general coverage standard had itself been improperly promulgated as a rule. The Court regarded that separate procedural question as unnecessary after rejecting the standard on its merits. (268–269, 291, 295–296.)

## Buyers' responsibilities and administrative proceedings

Bell operated an aerospace research, development, design, and fabrication plant in Wheatfield, New York. On July 30, 1970, a UAW local petitioned to represent twenty-five buyers in its purchasing and procurement department. Bell argued that they were managerial employees outside the NLRA. Their work involved obtaining standardized supplies and specialized items made to technical specifications. Requisitions sometimes specified a vendor; changing that vendor could require approval. Otherwise buyers selected vendors, drafted invitations, evaluated bids, negotiated price and terms, and prepared orders. (269–270.)

Absent contrary instructions, the buyers exercised that negotiating discretion without a dollar limit, but their authority to commit Bell was qualified. They executed orders up to $50,000; orders below $5,000 could be placed or cancelled on their own signatures, while commitments above $5,000 required superior approval at progressively higher levels. For the Minute Man project, accounting for seventy percent of sales, purchases were team decisions involving engineering, quality assurance, finance, and manufacturing. The buyer chaired the team and signed the order, with pricing personnel participating in negotiations. These details mattered because the proper inquiry concerned actual responsibility and authority, rather than a job title or an assumption that every purchasing employee was managerial. (270, 290 n.19.)

The Board directed an election on May 20, 1971. Relying on its North Arkansas decision, it reasoned that even managerial buyers were covered unless union membership created a relevant labor-relations conflict. It rejected Bell's concerns that unionized buyers would favor union contractors or choices to manufacture internally, benefiting other plant unions. The Board considered that conjecture controllable through Bell's established directions. Board member Jenkins independently viewed the buyers as nonmanagerial; the majority apparently accepted that they might be managerial but nevertheless entitled to statutory protection. (270–271 & n.2.)

Fifteen buyers voted for the union and nine against on June 16, 1971. The Board certified it on August 12. That same day, the Eighth Circuit rejected the Board's general managerial-coverage approach in North Arkansas. Bell sought reconsideration, but the Board adhered to a narrower exclusion for personnel involved in labor-relations policy or a related conflict. Bell refused to bargain, prompting an unfair-labor-practice finding under §§ 8(a)(5) and (1) and an order to bargain. Bell petitioned for review; the Board sought enforcement. (271–272 & n.3.)

The Second Circuit denied enforcement. Although it saw substantial evidence that these buyers might not be sufficiently high in the hierarchy to be truly managerial, it could not tell whether the Board had found that fact or instead applied its erroneous coverage theory. It remanded and required rulemaking for any reversal of the Board's prior treatment of buyers. The Supreme Court granted the Board's petition and decided the case on April 23, 1974. (267, 272–274.)

## Statutory construction: managerial status is broader than supervision

The NLRA expressly excluded supervisors and several other categories from employee coverage, but did not name managerial employees. Powell nevertheless held that the text's broad reference to employees did not settle their inclusion. He consulted the Board's early practice, the Taft-Hartley amendments, later agency and judicial construction, and Congress's further amendment of the statute in 1959. Longstanding administrative interpretations, especially those left intact during reenactment, could strongly indicate intended statutory meaning; later legislation bearing on the earlier law also mattered. (274–275 & nn.4–8.)

Before 1947, the Board had excluded personnel closely identified with management from rank-and-file units: expediters, buyers, and executives able to formulate and effectuate management policies. Powell acknowledged uncertainty whether every early decision excluded such employees from the Act entirely rather than only particular units. Dravo, for example, expressly reserved buyers' and expediters' ability to organize. He nonetheless emphasized the absence of any certification of a managerial unit and the Board's recognition of managerial functions. A related supervisory policy had changed repeatedly, ending in the Packard decision permitting foremen to organize, which the Supreme Court upheld in 1947. (275–278.)

Congress's Taft-Hartley response expressly excluded supervisors. The House proposed a broad definition reaching labor-relations, confidential, police, and time-study personnel; the Senate proposed a narrower definition centered on genuine authority over other employees. The conference adopted the Senate approach. Its explanation stated that some omitted groups were already outside the Act under prevailing Board practice, so an express exclusion was unnecessary, while professional and guard categories received distinct treatment. Powell read those statements and the broader concern about blurring labor and management as reflecting an implicit exclusion extending beyond supervisors to genuine managerial employees. Statements treating executives as already excluded reinforced that inference. The Court distinguished professionals, defined through the nature of specialized work, from managerial employees defined through policy authority. (279–285 & nn.9–13.)

After 1947, the Board repeatedly treated managerial personnel as outside bargaining protection. The opinion reviewed assistant buyers, procurement personnel, and employees authorized to bind employers, citing Palace Laundry, American Locomotive, Curtiss-Wright, and Swift. It regarded the pre-1970 pattern and appellate decisions as consistent support for the broader exclusion, left standing when Congress amended the Act in 1959. The Board therefore could not replace that construction with a rule excluding only managers exposed to a labor-relations conflict. Under the proper inquiry, actual duties, authority, and relationship to management determined whether an employee formulated and effectuated employer policies. (285–290 & nn.14–19.)

A conflict of interest could still independently justify excluding employees from a unit; it simply was not the sole basis for excluding true managerial employees from statutory coverage. Note 20 also preserved a record issue. Bell asserted that it lacked fair notice and an opportunity to present conflict-specific evidence at the representation hearing. If new relevant information was offered on remand, the Board should consider reopening the record. The Court did not decide that additional evidence was necessarily required or that the existing record was inadequate. (290 n.20.)

## Choice of rulemaking or adjudication on remand

The precise procedural issue was how the Board could decide, on remand, whether these buyers were genuinely managerial under the correct substantive standard. The Court did not reach the legality of the process used to announce the now-rejected narrower standard. Although prior Board decisions had often excluded buyers from other units, only American Locomotive and Swift appeared to deny organization even in separate units. That history did not compel a legislative rule governing every buyer classification. (290–292 & nn.21–23.)

Chenery II recognized both a useful role for prospective rulemaking and a necessary role for case-by-case development when problems were unforeseen, experience incomplete, or factual variations too specialized for a general formula. Wyman-Gordon's plurality and concurrence, despite their differences, also accepted policymaking through adjudication and agency discretion in selecting a proper procedural mode. The Board could announce new principles while deciding a case; rulemaking was not automatically required merely because a decision might differ from earlier classifications. (292–294.)

The Court reserved circumstances where adjudication could constitute an abuse of discretion or violate the statute. Here, however, buyers worked in many industries and possessed widely varying responsibilities. A general standard might have little practical usefulness. Careful individual determinations allowed the Board to examine authority and duties at particular firms, and its choice of that method deserved substantial weight. Although rulemaking could solicit broad industry and labor perspectives, the company and buyers immediately concerned could supply pertinent information through a full opportunity to be heard in adjudication. (294–295.)

Industry reliance did not establish an obstacle on this record. No substantial adverse reliance consequences had been demonstrated, no fines or damages were at stake, and the proceeding did not impose new liability for past acts taken in good-faith reliance on Board pronouncements. Concerns were also speculative because managerial status remained undecided. Those observations explain why adjudication was allowed here; they do not authorize every retroactive agency change regardless of consequences. (295.)

## White's partial dissent: text, unit boundaries, and agency reconsideration

White agreed that rulemaking was unnecessary, but rejected a statutory command to exclude all managerial employees. The statute covered any employee subject to precise enumerated exclusions. Congress carefully defined supervisors but omitted the broader managerial class. It also expressly contemplated organizational rights for professional employees exercising judgment, some of whom could perform functions characterized as managerial. The dissent saw the Board's labor-relations-conflict exclusion as more faithful to that statutory structure than the majority's categorical addition. (295–299.)

The historical disagreement turned on the distinction between excluding an employee from a particular unit and denying all statutory protection. White read early managerial decisions as exercises of unit-designation authority, not a determination that managers lacked employee status. Dravo explicitly preserved organizational rights. Even the Board's periods of refusing supervisory bargaining units had not necessarily removed supervisors from protection against unfair labor practices. The Taft-Hartley enactment went further and expressly excluded supervisors; the dissent did not infer an unstated extension to a separate group Congress had not similarly defined. (299–303 & n.1.)

The House managers' explanation could establish exclusion for expressly discussed labor-relations and confidential personnel, but did not justify extending that exclusion to every omitted category. Guards and time-study professionals received different, affirmative treatment. References to executives did not establish a general exclusion for hundreds of thousands of comparatively low-level buyers; most senior executives already fell within the specified supervisor, confidential, professional, or conflict categories. The burden, for White, lay on those seeking to exclude employees despite the inclusive text. (303–308 & nn.2–3.)

He considered later Board practice neither cohesive nor decisive. American Locomotive and Swift expressly denied separate-unit rights, but most other decisions merely excluded managers from particular units. Courts often described a total exclusion without needing it to decide the case. The Board could reconsider an erroneous interpretation, and the 1959 amendments concerned other matters without evidence that Congress focused on managerial coverage. Congressional silence alone did not ratify every earlier agency statement. White would accept the Board's newer construction if it had support in the record and a reasonable legal basis, rather than freeze a disputed historical interpretation into mandatory exclusion. (308–311.)
