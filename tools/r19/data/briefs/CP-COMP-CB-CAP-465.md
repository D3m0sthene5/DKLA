# Peacock Records, Inc. v. Checker Records, Inc.

**Citation:** 365 F.2d 145 (7th Cir. 1966). **Court/date:** Seventh Circuit, July 26, 1966. **Opinion:** Judge Schnackenberg, on a panel with Judges Castle and Kiley. **Course:** Civil Procedure—relief from a judgment procured through perjury. **Assigned location:** CivPro.pdf, physical pages 598–599, printed pages 1181–1182 (Peacock note and following Philos discussion).

## Facts

Peacock Records and Don Robey sued Checker Records, Chess Record Corporation, and Phil and Leonard Chess in diversity. The complaint alleged interference with recording contracts involving the Blind Boys gospel group and Reverend Ballinger, a conspiracy to interfere, a request for injunctive relief, and fraudulent interference with Robey’s contracts with composers Loyd Woodard and Rosco Robinson. Evidence included record sales, royalty advances, promotional expenditures, and an expert’s valuation of exclusive recording rights for one year at $20,000–$25,000. Those figures describe evidence; they are not the eventual award. Woodard and Robinson were also members of the Blind Boys. (146 & n.2.)

The defendants’ amended counterclaim was tried first. Its two counts alleged interference with their recording rights and a conspiracy to manufacture false evidence and maliciously prosecute them, including this suit. The court ruled against defendants on both counts. Following a bench trial of plaintiffs’ claims, the court entered a $250,000 judgment on January 17, 1964, reducing it to $150,000 on September 21, 1964. Defendants appealed that amended judgment in No. 14944. (146 & n.1.)

## Procedural posture

While that appeal was pending, defendants moved under Rule 60(b) on June 22, 1965 to vacate the judgment. They asserted, among other grounds, that plaintiffs had procured and suborned material perjury. Affidavits came from Leonard Chess and witnesses Woodard, J. T. Clinkscales, and Lawrence Abram. Plaintiffs responded on July 9. A hearing began August 10. On August 17, three Blind Boys and one member’s wife appeared; the court appointed counsel to advise them about refusing to testify and the consequences of testifying. After consultation, they were willing to testify, but the court stopped taking further testimony. (146.)

The judge considered voluntary admissions of perjury inherently incredible, questioned jurisdiction, and feared reopening would encourage endless litigation. On August 19 he denied relief and stated that he would not grant it if the appellate court remanded. Defendants appealed that denial in No. 15382. The appellate court therefore addressed both the damages judgment and the postjudgment order. (146–147 & n.3.)

## Issue and holding

Did the district court exercise sound legal discretion by denying relief despite this evidentiary showing of perjury? **No.** The record affirmatively supported the motion. The denial was an abuse of discretion; defendants were entitled to vacatur and a new trial. (147–148.)

## Rule and reasoning

Where perjured testimony may have played a part in inducing a judgment, the court must confront whether the judgment was obtained partly through perjury. Once that is established, the judge cannot save the judgment by merely weighing how much the perjury mattered. The taint reaches the judicial proceeding itself. Here, refusing to take willing witnesses’ further testimony avoided the necessary direct inquiry. The appellate court expressly found no indication that any counsel participated in wrongdoing. (147.)

The court distinguished Parker v. Checker Taxi Company rather than treating every allegation of perjury as sufficient. Parker involved a bare petition verified by attorneys on information and belief, with no additional proof. Peacock instead involved affidavits occupying 110 pages of the printed appellate appendix, witnesses willing to testify after receiving advice about their constitutional rights, and unimpeached sworn testimony already given in open court. That record so clearly supported the motion that denial fell outside sound discretion. Rule 60(b) reinforced the court’s power to relieve a party from a judgment obtained through fraud, misrepresentation, or an adverse party’s misconduct. (147–148.)

## Exact disposition

**No. 15382:** reverse the order denying Rule 60(b) relief and remand with instructions to grant the motion. **No. 14944:** vacate the damages judgment and remand for a new trial. This is more than an instruction merely to conduct another hearing; it does not finally adjudicate defendants’ liability at the new trial. (148.)

## Textbook connection and limits

The assigned note accurately quotes the refusal to weigh perjury’s influence once it helped procure the judgment. The next paragraph attributes a clear-and-convincing-evidence discussion to Philos Technologies, a later decision; that is not additional language from Peacock. The surrounding Hazel-Atlas material and Rule 60(d) independent-action discussion also concern distinct decisions and procedures. Peacock should not be reduced to an automatic-vacatur rule triggered by an unsupported accusation.
