# Monroe v. City of Charlottesville

**579 F.3d 380 (4th Cir. 2009) · Fourth Circuit · August 31, 2009 · No. 08–1334**

**Civil Procedure — adequacy of a class representative, certification evidence, and pleading constitutional claims.** Assigned: CivPro.pdf, physical page 427, printed page 826, the Monroe example in Note 7. Pinpoints below use the numbered pages of the preserved seventeen-page court-issued opinion; reporter-page conversions are not inferred.

## Investigation, claims, and procedural chronology

Charlottesville police investigated a serial rapist beginning in spring 2002. Several victims consistently described a youthful-looking Black male, and police had composite images and samples of the assailant’s DNA. Officers asked individuals matching the general description, without a DNA sample already on file, to provide a specimen. Over several years they contacted 190 Black men. Officer Mooney approached Larry Monroe at his home. The opinion describes contacts based on composite descriptions, tips, police records, and other investigatory information; its first footnote preserves possible minimal exceptions rather than asserting that every contact followed an identical screening process. (2–3 and n.1.)

Monroe brought a § 1983 action against the City and officers. He alleged an equal-protection violation because police approached him because he was Black and because they did not investigate similarly when an assailant was described as white. He also alleged an unreasonable seizure through the encounter at his home and the taking of bodily fluids for DNA analysis. He sought class treatment of the equal-protection claim. (3.)

The district court dismissed the racial-classification theory but allowed the different-investigatory-methods theory to continue. It dismissed the encounter-as-seizure theory, allowed the bodily-fluids claim to proceed, and permitted an amended complaint. The amendment added the officer’s uniform, the absence of advice that Monroe could decline, the home setting, and Monroe’s beliefs concerning police relations with minority communities. The court again dismissed the same encounter theory while leaving the other claims pending. The opinion notes that uncontested material outside the pleadings was considered on equal protection and discusses permissible appellate conversion to summary judgment when necessary. It does not treat that entire analysis as depending solely on the complaint. (3–5 and n.2.)

Certification then exposed a different problem. An officer’s affidavit reported that Monroe said his lawyers did not return calls, they had approached him about suing, he learned the action was filed in his name from a newspaper, the suit made him look bad, and he was not interested in damages against Mooney. The court set July 13, 2007 as the memorandum deadline and held a July 20 certification hearing. Monroe did not attend or provide a responsive affidavit by then. His lawyer testified that she returned calls and Monroe had approached her about suit, submitted his deposition, and told the judge no further evidence would be offered. On July 23 Monroe filed a responsive affidavit. The court refused it as untimely and denied certification for lack of adequacy. His deposition showed that he could not recognize the complaint and knew little about the litigation. (5–6.)

Rather than pursue the remaining individual claims, Monroe requested voluntary dismissal without prejudice. To obtain the City’s agreement and establish appellate jurisdiction, he agreed not to bring those claims again, making the dismissal effectively with prejudice. He appealed the certification denial and the previously dismissed constitutional theories. This is the sequence reported in the 2009 opinion, not a claim that later appellate-finality doctrine approved this mechanism. (6.)

## Holdings and judgment

The Fourth Circuit **affirmed**. The district court acted within its discretion in rejecting the late affidavit and finding Monroe inadequate to represent the class. His home-encounter allegations did not plausibly establish a Fourth Amendment seizure. On the record described, police use of several victims’ suspect descriptions did not constitute the express governmental racial classification asserted; the panel additionally explained why the investigation would satisfy strict scrutiny on the undisputed facts. It did not adjudicate the voluntarily dismissed remaining theories on their merits. (7–17.)

## Adequacy required demonstrated participation

Rule 23(a)(4) protects absent members through a representative who can fairly and adequately pursue the class’s interests. The plaintiff bears the burden of establishing the certification requirements, and the district court’s certification assessment receives considerable deference. Certification is an evidentiary inquiry; it does not simply accept the complaint’s allegations as true. The court may set and enforce filing deadlines. Here the prehearing deadline, the completed hearing, and counsel’s express statement that no further evidence remained justified refusing the later affidavit. (6–7.)

The panel also found no material prejudice from that refusal. Footnote 3 explains that the late affidavit’s response to the phone-call issue was weak and that its statement of a desire to proceed addressed neither a claim made by the officer nor a basis on which the district court had relied. Thus the footnote does not establish an absolute rule against considering late evidence; it supplies an additional record-specific reason that this affidavit would not change the result. (7 n.3.)

Adequacy does not require mastery of every legal intricacy. It does require enough interest, credibility, and understanding to avoid a person merely lending a name to litigation controlled by lawyers. The officer’s evidence, Monroe’s inability to recognize his own complaint, and his lack of knowledge concerning filing raised serious questions. His lawyer’s limited rebuttal did not resolve them. Questions about when Monroe considered or authorized suit had also drawn privilege objections. The panel relied on the absence of sufficient evidence meeting Monroe’s burden; it did not announce that every privilege objection itself establishes inadequacy. (7–8.)

## Encounter and Fourth Amendment pleading

The court separately applied de novo review and the Twombly plausibility standard to the alleged encounter seizure. It distinguished pleaded facts from conclusions such as coercion, nonconsent, and objective reasonableness. A police approach and questions do not alone amount to a seizure; the inquiry asks whether, in all the circumstances, a reasonable person would feel unable to disregard the officer and terminate the encounter. (8–10.)

The panel rejected Monroe’s attempt to make his subjective beliefs controlling by pointing to shared community experiences. It reasoned that the proposed approach risked treating all police encounters with minorities as seizures. The officer’s failure to announce a right to leave or refuse was not by itself sufficient; a uniform and a visit at home also described many consensual encounters. The actual allegations did not plausibly establish a seizure or suggest additional discoverable facts raising the claim beyond speculation. That reasoning concerned the encounter claim dismissed under Rule 12(b)(6), not an adjudication that every DNA collection is consensual or that the separately pending bodily-fluids claim lacked merit. (10–11.)

## Equal protection: actual descriptions versus a police-created profile

Monroe argued that police had expressly classified him by race and therefore had to satisfy strict scrutiny. He invoked Shelley v. Kraemer and Evans v. Newton to argue that governmental reliance on privately created racial distinctions could still be state action; he also cited Grutter against treating race as merely one factor among others. (12.)

The panel distinguished the investigated facts. Several victims consistently described the same characteristic of their actual assailant. Police did not invent a racial profile, choose among conflicting racial descriptions, or decide to investigate Black people as a group. They used a description containing several pertinent characteristics and further investigatory leads. The court distinguished that process from state enforcement of covenants deliberately designed to exclude people from property based on race. It also drew on Iqbal’s distinction between an investigation targeting suspected links to crimes and an incidental disparate impact. These were the panel’s reasons for rejecting the express-classification theory; the opinion does not authorize every race-conscious investigatory practice. (12–16.)

Footnote 4 acknowledged criticism of Brown v. City of Oneonta but considered important factual differences and concluded that criticism did not undermine this record’s result. It is not a separate concurrence or dissent in Monroe. (12 n.4.)

The court alternatively discussed strict scrutiny, while acknowledging that the factual record might not be completely developed. Protecting the public from crime was compelling, and the investigation was narrowly tailored on the undisputed facts: multiple consistent victim descriptions, race as one pertinent characteristic, further suspect-identifying information, and 190 contacts rather than a sweep of every Black male resident. The panel gave this additional rationale after agreeing that no express governmental racial classification occurred. (16–17.)
