# Beaver v. Brumlow
2010-NMCA-033, 148 N.M. 172, 231 P.3d 628 (N.M. Ct. App., Mar. 4, 2010) | Contracts | Part performance, omitted land-sale terms, and specific performance

## A home established in reliance on an oral sale

Warren and Betty Beaver owned twenty-four acres in Ruidoso Downs and employed Michael Brumlow in their racehorse transportation business. In June or July 2001, Michael asked Warren to sell a portion as a home site. Warren agreed, and they walked its boundaries. No price or closing date was fixed. Michael expected to pay the property's market value. The Brumlows took possession with the Beavers' consent and established their home there. Michael's employment and the proposed land purchase were related circumstances, but the agreement was a sale of land, rather than a promise to convey land as compensation for employment. Opinion ¶¶ 3–6, 32.

Karen Brumlow cashed out retirement accounts, incurring substantial penalties, to purchase a double-wide home and finance improvements. The buyers installed a foundation, footers, a deck, stairs, water and electricity, septic and propane systems, storage, and landscaping. The home and improvements cost approximately $85,000. Warren signed municipal applications for the home's placement and the septic installation. The Beavers observed the work, permitted uninterrupted possession, and sought advice about sale documents. Their mortgage's due-on-sale clause apparently impeded formalization. When the Brumlows repeatedly requested written documents, the response was that the parties would work it out. ¶¶ 4–7.

In March 2004, Michael gave notice that he would leave the Beavers' employment for a competitor. The relationship deteriorated. The Beavers decided against the sale, attempted to characterize the arrangement as a lease, and required an agreement calling for $400 monthly payments. The Brumlows understood those payments as payments for the land. After they labelled their checks accordingly, the Beavers stopped cashing them and asserted that the payments were rent. The document did not use rent or lease terminology. The sellers refused the buyers' offer to pay the land's fair market value in cash and obtain a survey. ¶ 8.

## Litigation and the actual judgment

The Beavers sued for ejectment on the theory that the Brumlows had violated a rental agreement. The buyers denied a lease, asserted an agreement to purchase, and counterclaimed for breach of contract, fraud, and prima facie tort. The sellers invoked the statute of frauds. After finding the oral sale proven by clear, cogent, and convincing evidence, the district court concluded that both sides' part performance removed the agreement from the statute and that the sellers' later conduct constituted prima facie tort. ¶¶ 9–10.

The buyers were offered damages for the tort or specific performance and chose specific performance. An appraiser valued the identified tract at $10,000, and a survey was prepared. The final judgment required the buyers to deposit $10,000 in their lawyer's trust account within thirty days of judgment and required the sellers to execute a warranty deed for the surveyed tract. The sellers would receive payment upon delivery of the deed. All other claims and counterclaims were dismissed with prejudice. Thus $85,000 was the buyers' reliance expenditure, while $10,000 was the land's appraised purchase price; neither was a damages award. ¶ 11.

On appeal the sellers did not challenge the factual findings or the sufficiency of proof that an oral agreement existed. They challenged the character of the buyers' part performance, the absence of agreed price and timing, and the adequacy of damages. The court reviewed applicability of the statute of frauds de novo and the equitable remedy for abuse of discretion. ¶¶ 2, 12–13, 19.

## Part performance: the circumstances must naturally indicate a sale

New Mexico treats the land-contract statute of frauds as part of its adopted common law. Its writing requirement serves evidentiary, cautionary, and boundary-setting functions. Equity nevertheless recognizes part performance where the oral agreement has been acted upon so substantially that denying effect to it would be inequitable. The exception prevents a mechanical use of the statute from producing the injustice the statute was intended to prevent. ¶¶ 14–18.

The sellers argued that the buyers' conduct could be explained by an informal opportunity to live on someone else's property. On that view, any plausible alternative explanation prevented the conduct from being unequivocally referable to a sale. The court rejected that interpretation. Applying Nashan, it asked whether an outsider who knew the circumstances, except for the claimed oral agreement, would naturally and reasonably infer a land contract of the alleged general character. The factors were to be considered together rather than applied mechanically. The court expressly declined to require that the performance have no conceivable explanation apart from the contract. ¶¶ 19–21.

Possession and valuable, permanent, substantial improvements were especially important. Here the buyers took possession of the agreed tract, liquidated retirement savings at a penalty, established a home, and improved the land with the sellers' knowledge and consent over several years. Those acts adequately demonstrated reliance on the sale and warranted taking the agreement outside the statute of frauds. The holding concerns this combined factual setting; it does not establish that every monthly payment or any improvement alone proves a land sale. ¶¶ 21–22.

## Equity could supply fair value and reasonable timing

The sellers argued that setting a price and payment deadline made a contract the parties had never made. The court distinguished Bellamah, which refused to rewrite a written bargain through a price abatement the parties had not provided. Colcott suggested that otherwise sufficient, partly performed land agreements could permit implication of a reasonable price, although Colcott had not actually decided the enforceability of the repurchase option at issue in its discussion. The court then adopted the more directly analogous approach of O'Keefe. ¶¶ 23–27.

The agreement identified the land; its existence was conceded; extensive reliance occurred with the sellers' continuing consent; the buyers expected to pay its worth; and the failure to formalize was not the buyers' fault. In those circumstances equity could fix fair market value through an objective appraiser. The sellers did not dispute the fairness of the $10,000 figure. The missing time term likewise allowed performance within a reasonable time, a factual question. These were context-dependent implications in enforcing the proven transaction, not a general authority to manufacture essential terms in every incomplete negotiation. ¶¶ 28–30.

The report quotes an earlier conclusion providing sixty days from the court's decision, but describes the final judgment as requiring tender within thirty days of its entry. The brief preserves that distinction rather than substituting one deadline throughout. ¶¶ 11, 23, 30.

## Damages did not displace specific performance

The sellers relied on dismissal of the fraud claim and argued that the buyers' expenditures could readily be repaid. The court distinguished Hubbard's claim to land in exchange for services: such services must have an extraordinary character beyond ordinary pecuniary compensation. This case instead involved possession and improvements in reliance on a sale. Hubbard did not make proof of actual fraud a universal prerequisite to specific performance. ¶¶ 31–32.

Nor were the buyers merely seeking reimbursement of their work. They sought the land promised to them. Land is ordinarily presumed to have special value and each parcel is unique, so an available monetary measure of expenditures did not show that damages adequately replaced the contractual subject matter. The court rejected the sellers' arguments and affirmed the district court's judgment. It did not award both tort damages and conveyance. ¶¶ 33–36.
