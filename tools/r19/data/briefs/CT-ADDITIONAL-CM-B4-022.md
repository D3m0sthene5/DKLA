# Ward v. McGarry
2022 UT App 62, 511 P.3d 1213 (2022) | Contracts

## Court and disposition
The Utah Court of Appeals affirmed dismissal of Sara Marie Ward’s complaint on May 12, 2022. Judge Michele M. Christiansen Forster wrote for a unanimous panel with Judges Gregory K. Orme and Jill M. Pohlman. The court reviewed the dismissal for correctness. The result upheld rejection of emotional damages under an enforceable agreement, promissory estoppel concerning that agreement, the preserved tort theories, and dependent punitive damages. It did not hold that no independent tort duty concerning intimate photographs could ever exist. (¶¶1, 7, 24–27 & n.5.)

## Allegations and litigation
Ward and Meredith Dwight McGarry had been romantically involved for approximately two-and-a-half years and had a child together. During their relationship, Ward sent him nude photographs of herself. After their separation and the start of a custody dispute, she requested by text that he delete the photographs, promising in exchange not to use them in that dispute. He agreed. The actual messages were not in the record, but both parties accepted that the complaint accurately reflected their contents. (¶¶2–3, 12 & n.3.)

Several years after the separation, McGarry’s former wife told Ward that she and her son had seen the photographs. According to the former wife, he had given his phone to the child to play with; the child brought it home and showed the photographs to his mother. She sent one to Ward as proof. This account supplied the complaint’s allegations, not trial findings about precisely how the photographs were accessed. (¶4.)

Ward pleaded intentional and negligent infliction of emotional distress, breach of contract, equitable estoppel, punitive damages, negligent misrepresentation, and fraud. McGarry moved to dismiss based on the economic loss rule, the absence of recoverable contract damages, and the inability to plead equitable estoppel or punitive damages as independent causes of action. The District Court dismissed the complaint. The fraud dismissal rested on limitations and was not challenged on appeal; the appellate estoppel analysis addressed the promissory-estoppel theory Ward advanced. (¶¶5–7 & n.1, 14–21.)

## Foreseeability did not establish explicit contemplation
The usual Utah rule denied mental-anguish damages for breach of contract. The exception required both foreseeable emotional injury and explicit contemplation of emotional damages at formation, demonstrated through the contract’s specific language and obligations. Ward’s agreement was personal, and the District Court accepted foreseeability. The missing element was contractual language showing that the parties contemplated that category of damages. These are separate requirements: the foreseeability of emotional distress from breach does not itself establish an agreed expectation of compensation for it. (¶¶8–10.)

Cabaness involved language prohibiting harassment, disruption, interference with work, and an intimidating, offensive, or hostile environment. Because those promises specifically concerned mental well-being, their language created a factual question about contemplation of emotional damages. The court stressed that Cabaness had not conclusively established contemplation. Kranendonk had subsequently clarified and disavowed portions of that decision while retaining some of its analytical utility. A lawyer’s promise to use best efforts to obtain a recovery in Kranendonk concerned pecuniary interests and did not imply a further promise to secure peace of mind. (¶11 & n.2.)

Here, the accepted language promised deletion in exchange for nonuse in custody litigation. It contained no terms about mental or emotional harm. The panel distinguished the understandable emotional response to nondeletion from promises innately directed toward harassment, intimidation, or offensive conduct. Even the absence of a financial component did not change what the language promised. Ward’s argument that any breach would naturally cause emotional harm established the nature of the interest and foreseeability, but not explicit contemplation. The language therefore created no factual issue on the missing requirement, and emotional damages were unavailable. The ruling did not require the court to find that the photographs were emotionally insignificant. (¶¶12–13 & n.4.)

## Estoppel could not supply damages under the same enforceable agreement
Ward argued that rejection of contract damages left her without a legal remedy and permitted promissory estoppel. The court distinguished a valid contract under which a particular loss is not compensable from an absent or unenforceable contract. The existence and enforceability of this agreement were undisputed; indeed, the District Court acknowledged that failure to delete the photographs breached it. That did not establish the recoverability of every consequential injury. (¶¶14–17.)

Alternative equitable claims may remain when contractual enforceability is genuinely disputed, as the court explained through AGTC’s unresolved defenses. But neither party here suggested a defect in formation or enforceability. A failure to prove recoverable contract damages could not convert an express agreement into a quasi-contract or authorize the court to make a better agreement for the parties. Promissory estoppel consequently supplied no alternative route to the same excluded emotional damages. (¶¶18–21.)

## Tort preservation and punitive damages
The economic loss rule generally confines a duty supplied by contract to contract remedies while permitting an independently imposed tort duty. The deletion duty came from the agreement. On appeal Ward asserted an independent duty not to show her intimate photographs to others, but she had not presented that specific argument below. The only asserted independent duty in the District Court was to keep harmful material from children—a duty allegedly owed to the child, rather than to Ward. The appellate court therefore could not use the newly asserted duty to disturb the dismissal. (¶¶22–24.)

Importantly, footnote 5 assumed without deciding that such an independent duty might exist and support a tort claim. The holding was about preservation of the argument, not a categorical rejection of privacy-related duties. Without a preserved basis to revive the tort claims, the punitive-damages request also failed. Ward did not challenge the determination that punitive damages were not an independent cause; she requested reinstatement only if the tort claims returned. They did not, and the entire appealed dismissal was affirmed. (¶¶24–27 & n.5.)
