# Fera v. Village Plaza, Inc.
396 Mich. 639, 242 N.W.2d 372 (1976) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
Chief Justice Kavanagh wrote the Michigan Supreme Court's opinion reinstating a $200,000 jury award for anticipated profits of a proposed new business. Justices Williams, Fitzgerald, and Lindemer joined. Justice Coleman concurred in part and dissented in part; Justices Levin and Ryan took no part. The participating court thus divided four to one over the disposition. It reversed the Court of Appeals and restored the trial court's judgment on the verdict, with costs to plaintiffs. The report also records denial of rehearing at 397 Mich. 956. 396 Mich. at 641, 648–649.

## Facts and proceedings
On August 20, 1965, Anthony and Frank Fera contracted for a ten-year lease of a particular location in a proposed shopping center for a book-and-bottle shop. Minimum rent was $1,000 monthly, with an additional five percent of annual receipts exceeding $240,000. They paid a $1,000 deposit. Later they surrendered approximately 600 square feet so another tenant could use it; in exchange, liquor sales were excluded from the percentage-rent calculation. Work stoppages and other complications delayed the project. After the developers defaulted, Bank of the Commonwealth took a deed in lieu of foreclosure and Schostak Brothers managed the property. Id. at 641–642.

When the premises became ready, plaintiffs were refused their contracted location because the lease had been misplaced and the space let to others. They rejected alternative space as unsuitable for their planned venture. They sued in Wayne Circuit Court, including for anticipated profits. The jury returned a $200,000 verdict against the defendants. The Court of Appeals ordered a new damages trial on the premise that lost profits were an improper measure for this new business and that plaintiffs' proof was speculative. The Supreme Court addressed both propositions rather than simply rejecting a categorical rule and leaving proof unresolved. Id. at 642, 645.

## Rule and issue
A new business is not automatically barred from recovering expected profits. The plaintiff must provide a reasonable basis to estimate the monetary loss; a jury may not guess. Historical decisions distinguishing new from interrupted businesses reflected evidentiary differences, because an established operation often has a record supporting prediction. A new operation's lack of that history makes proof more difficult but does not necessarily make it inadequate. Uncertainty describes the evidence, not an inherently unrecoverable category of profits. Id. at 642–645.

The Supreme Court read Jarrait's restrictive lease-damages language alongside Isbell's recognition that sufficiently proven prospective profits can be recovered. The governing inquiry was whether the evidence afforded reasonable inference, not whether the business had begun operating. Absolute or mathematical certainty was unnecessary. The plaintiff still needed more than sympathy, conjecture, or a numerical estimate unsupported by the circumstances. Id. at 643–645, 648.

## Proof, arguments, and majority reasoning
The parties extensively litigated both profitability and the prospect of a liquor license. Defendants presented witnesses associated with the Liquor Control Commission, a nearby Cunningham Drug store, and other retail operations. Plaintiffs offered experienced witnesses, and had backgrounds in liquor sales, books, and liquor distribution. Their specially prepared budget was challenged on cross-examination. Defendants' evidence predicted that the venture would lose money, while Fera projected approximately $270,000 in profits over ten years. The $200,000 verdict fell within the testimonial range, but the majority's reasoning was not simply that any award below a requested amount is permissible. It relied on substantive testimony from both sides, full cross-examination, and the jury's informed credibility assessment. Id. at 645–647.

The trial judge explained that this was among the most thoroughly tried issues in the case. He found enough evidence to move the claim beyond conjecture and denied a new trial. One defense witness had an ownership or officer relationship with Schostak Brothers and a potential financial interest in the outcome, which the jury could consider in weighing credibility. Contradictions or impeachment did not deprive plaintiffs' evidence of all probative value. Id. at 645–648.

The jury received instructions about speculative damages, including the need to reject a speculative claim. The majority treated its choice between competing expert accounts, including competing license evidence, as a factfinding function. The Supreme Court acknowledged that its members might have found plaintiffs' proof unpersuasive as jurors. That was different from deciding that reasonable minds could not disagree or that the trial court abused its discretion in allowing the issue to reach the jury and refusing a new trial. Neither ground for appellate displacement was shown. Id. at 647–648.

The court also relied on the principle that once some injury is established, inability to achieve precise measurement need not defeat recovery, especially where the defendant's conduct contributes to the imprecision. That principle operated with the extensive proof here; it did not dispense with the requirement of a reasonable evidentiary basis. Id. at 648.

## Coleman's separate writing
Justice Coleman accepted that a new business can prove expected profits with reasonable certainty and agreed that bookstore losses could go to the jury. She disagreed about liquor profits. Plaintiffs had no license, and the regional supervisor and former commissioner had testified that the proposed operation could not obtain one. For her, speculation about obtaining a license compounded speculation about future sales. Liquor profits should therefore have been excluded. Id. at 649.

Coleman approved the remedy proposed in Judge O'Hara's earlier dissent: affirm only if plaintiffs agreed within thirty days to remit the judgment above $60,000; otherwise reverse for a new trial. She separately recognized plaintiffs' entitlement to the $1,000 deposit. That was the dissent's proposed conditional outcome, not the majority's judgment. The majority reinstated the full $200,000 verdict. Id.

## Significance and source scope
Fera rejects a per se new-business bar while illustrating the difference between insufficient evidence as a matter of law and a factual dispute a properly instructed jury may resolve. It also shows that judges who agree on the general standard may disagree sharply about whether a specific licensing contingency satisfies it.
