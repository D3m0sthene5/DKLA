# Luttinger v. Rosen
164 Conn. 45, 316 A.2d 757 (1972) | Contracts

## Transaction and the financing condition
The Luttingers agreed to buy the Rosens’ Stamford property for $85,000 and paid an $8,500 deposit. Their agreement conditioned the purchase on obtaining a first mortgage from a bank or other lending institution for $45,000, for at least twenty years, at no more than 8.5 percent annual interest. The buyers promised due diligence in seeking that financing. If their effort failed and they notified the sellers within the specified time, the deposit would be returned and the contract would end without further obligation. The clause therefore allocated both the risk of unavailable financing and the consequences of that failure; it did not simply state a hoped-for source of funds. 164 Conn. at 46.

The buyers relied on an attorney familiar with mortgage terms at institutions inside and outside the area. He applied to a New Haven lender for a $45,000 mortgage at 8.25 percent over twenty-five years. He knew it was the only institution then willing to lend as much as $45,000 on a single-family dwelling. The resulting commitment called for the prevailing rate at closing, with a minimum of 8.75 percent. The amount and duration met the contract’s requirements, but the interest term did not. The buyers timely notified the sellers and demanded their deposit. Sellers’ counsel then offered an arrangement to fund the difference between the lender’s rate and 8.5 percent for all twenty-five years, without defining its exact terms. The buyers rejected the proposal; the sellers refused the refund. Id. at 46–47. ## Litigation and the sellers’ arguments
The buyers sued for the deposit. The trial court entered judgment for them, and the sellers appealed. The appeal presented two briefed assignments: whether the buyers had exercised due diligence despite applying to only one lender, and whether the financing condition remained unsatisfied despite the sellers’ proposed subsidy. The Supreme Court addressed the record’s unattacked findings rather than retrying the attorney’s knowledge or substituting its own view of available credit. Id. at 47.

The sellers argued that applications should have gone to additional lenders even if the buyers had relied on their lawyer’s familiarity with lending practices. They also challenged the conclusion that nonoccurrence of the mortgage condition entitled the buyers to a refund and sought consideration of their supplemental funding proposal. These were distinct questions. A buyer could fail a promised diligence obligation even though no qualifying mortgage ultimately materialized, but this record established the requisite effort.

## Rule, application, and evidentiary ruling
A condition precedent is an event the parties intend to occur before a right to performance arises. Here the unambiguous language made a mortgage on the specified institutional terms a prerequisite to the purchase obligation. The available commitment exceeded the interest ceiling, so it did not satisfy the event the parties had chosen. The buyers consequently obtained the contractual right to recover the deposit. Id. at 47–48.

The diligence findings supported a single application because the attorney was fully informed about institutions’ terms and had applied to the only lender that might meet the amount requirement. The law did not require additional futile applications. That conclusion rests on the established lending circumstances; it does not make one application sufficient under every mortgage contingency. Id. at 47.

The sellers’ subsidy would have introduced a new funding arrangement alongside the institutional mortgage. It did not turn the lender’s commitment into the mortgage described in the bargain. The buyers were free to reject the additional offer, and evidence about it was irrelevant to whether the agreed condition had occurred. The court therefore upheld the exclusion of testimony concerning that offer. Id. at 48. The reasoning enforces the specified condition and refund mechanism rather than asking whether the substitute would have produced approximately the same economic cost.

## Judgment and participation
The Supreme Court found no error, leaving the buyers’ favorable judgment intact. Loiselle wrote the opinion. House, Ryan, Shapiro, and MacDonald joined; the report states that the other judges concurred and contains no separate writing or judicial footnote. Id. at 48.

## Scope and use
This historical decision connects a promised effort with an express condition: the buyers satisfied their diligence promise, the specified financing event failed, and the refund provision then controlled. It does not excuse an obstructive buyer or resolve a case in which a lender actually offers compliant financing. The casebook’s lottery and mortgage-preapproval discussions after the opinion are editorial comparisons, not additional Luttinger holdings.
