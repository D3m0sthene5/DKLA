# Coulas v. Smith

**Civil Procedure · CP-108**  
96 Ariz. 325, 395 P.2d 527 (1964) · Supreme Court of Arizona, en banc · October 1, 1964

## Claims and the missed trial

Paul Smith sued Nicholas Coulas and William Bray on an open account for $669.32 and a promissory note with an alleged balance of $3,666.67. Bray denied liability and crossclaimed against Coulas for any amount Smith recovered against him, an alleged $4,000 debt, and $500 in attorney fees. Coulas answered Smith's complaint and Bray's crossclaim and asserted an $18,000 counterclaim against Smith. Smith replied. The pleadings therefore placed the claims in dispute. 96 Ariz. at 326.

The court initially set trial for October 10, 1958. On October 6, counsel for Smith and Bray stipulated to a December 10 date, and the court reset trial. Coulas's counsel neither attended nor joined that stipulation. Although the court records showed notification to all counsel, Coulas and his lawyers denied receiving the new-date notice. Neither appeared on December 10. The court nevertheless heard evidence, including Bray's testimony and the promissory note. Its minute entry used the word “default,” but also recorded a trial on the issues and evidence. Smith obtained judgment against Coulas on both counts and against Bray on the note; Bray obtained judgment against Coulas on the note. Judgment was entered December 11, 1958. 96 Ariz. at 327–28.

Nearly two years later, on October 29, 1960, Coulas moved to vacate. The superior court denied the motion, and he appealed. He argued principally that the judgment was void because he had not received the three-day notice of an application for default judgment required by Arizona Rule 55(b) for a party who had appeared.

## Issue and the controlling distinction

Did the absence of default-application notice invalidate a judgment entered after a defendant answered but failed to attend trial? Chief Justice Udall's unanimous opinion answered no on this record because the judgment was **a merits judgment following proof**, not a Rule 55 default judgment.

Default under Rule 55 concerned failure to plead or otherwise defend. Once Coulas answered on the merits, his denial required proof of the opposing claims. Failure to attend the scheduled trial did not turn that denial into an admission. The plaintiffs could proceed in his absence, but they still had to prove the claims. The judgment resulting from that hearing was therefore outside Rule 55(b)'s default-application notice requirement. Merely calling the event a “default” in a minute entry did not change its legal character. 96 Ariz. at 328–29.

## Authorities, notice, and the time bar

The court relied on *Bass v. Hoagland* and *Klein v. Rappaport*, both distinguishing pleading default from nonattendance after the case was at issue. The phrase “otherwise defend” covered preliminary defensive steps, such as jurisdictional attacks or motions, that could prevent default without yet answering. It did not require a party who had already answered to remain physically present at every trial call to avoid confessing the complaint. 96 Ariz. at 329.

The full opinion also clarifies language from *Phoenix Metals Corp. v. Roth*. Coulas relied on that case's statement requiring notice before a default judgment against someone who had answered. But *Phoenix Metals* involved an answer mistakenly attached to the wrong file, producing the false appearance that no answer existed. To the extent its language suggested that default was substantively appropriate after a merits answer, the court called that implication incorrect. The proper route was proof and a merits judgment, rather than notice followed by an inappropriate default. 96 Ariz. at 329–30.

Coulas's separate objection to the resetting stipulation failed because setting trial was the court's function under Rule 40. He had an opportunity to object to the court's notified order; his lack of participation in the lawyers' stipulation did not by itself invalidate it. His assertion of missing notice also lacked support in the minutes. The court applied the presumption that the clerk performed the notification duty under Rule 77(h), absent a contrary showing. It added that attendance on the originally scheduled date would have revealed the change. The opinion did not treat actual failure of notice, adequately established on another record, as categorically immaterial. 96 Ariz. at 330.

At most, the existing judgment was voidable rather than void. Arizona Rule 60(c)'s six-month limit therefore barred this attempted attack nearly two years after entry. The court did not apply that deadline to a judgment it regarded as void; it rejected the premise of voidness. 96 Ariz. at 330–31.

## Exact disposition and significance

The court **affirmed the denial of the motion to set aside and vacate the judgment**. Udall wrote; Lockwood, Struckmeyer, Bernstein, and Scruggs concurred. No separate opinion or judicial footnotes are reported.

The case distinguishes two consequences of inactivity. Failing to answer can admit allegations through default procedure. Failing to attend after answering leaves an opposing party able to proceed with evidence, while retaining that party's burden to prove the case. Coulas also demonstrates why the judgment's actual procedural basis, rather than an informal label, determines the applicable notice requirement and available relief. These conclusions arise under the Arizona rules and record addressed in 1964; the brief does not purport to supply a current deadline for every postjudgment motion.
