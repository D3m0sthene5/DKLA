# Redgrave v. Boston Symphony Orchestra, Inc. — En Banc Appeal
855 F.2d 888 (1st Cir. 1988) | Contracts

## Court, votes, and procedural sequence
The First Circuit decided the en banc appeal on August 31, 1988. Judge Coffin wrote the court’s opinion. Judges Bownes and Selya concurred in its contract analysis but dissented from its treatment of the Massachusetts Civil Rights Act claim; Judge Bownes wrote their separate opinion. The five participating judges were Coffin, Bownes, Breyer, Torruella, and Selya. Thus the contract ruling was unanimous, while the civil-rights result divided three to two. The court affirmed judgment for BSO on the civil-rights claim but vacated and remanded the contract judgment for entry of consequential damages supported by the evidence: $12,000 less performance expenses. (888, 890, 900, 912.)

This was not the initial trial ruling. The First Circuit had first certified state-law questions to the Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court, then issued a divided panel decision after receiving the response. It withdrew the panel opinion and dissent to reconsider the civil-rights issue en banc. The present opinion incorporated and reaffirmed the panel’s contract position while changing its civil-rights disposition. The separate state certification decision and withdrawn federal panel writings are procedural history, rather than additional full opinions independently briefed in this record. (890.)

## Facts and trial disposition
BSO engaged Vanessa Redgrave in March 1982 to narrate a series of Oedipus Rex concerts in Boston and New York. Subscribers and others protested her support for the Palestine Liberation Organization and her positions concerning Israel. Around April 1, BSO cancelled both her engagement and the performances. It asserted concerns about audience and performer safety, disruption, and artistic integrity, rather than an intention to punish her political speech. (890–891.)

After a sixteen-day trial, the jury found that BSO’s cancellation breached the contract and caused foreseeable career harm. The district court awarded the stipulated $27,500 net performance fee. Although it considered the evidence sufficient for the jury’s additional $100,000 consequential award, it set that award aside under expression protections drawn by analogy to defamation law. The jury, acting in an advisory capacity on the civil-rights claim, found that BSO’s own political disagreement did not motivate cancellation. The court also rejected liability based merely on acquiescing in outsiders’ pressure. Redgrave appealed the damages and civil-rights rulings; BSO cross-appealed the evidentiary sufficiency of consequential damages. Her company joined only the contract claim. (890–891 & nn. 1–2.)

## Identifiable lost work versus general reputation damages
BSO invoked decisions generally denying contract damages for reputational injury. The court explained that those decisions usually concerned remoteness, lack of foreseeability, and speculative valuation. Redgrave’s theory was narrower: identifiable movie or theater engagements that would otherwise have been offered were lost because of the breach. Such pecuniary opportunities could satisfy Massachusetts requirements of natural consequence, reasonable foreseeability, and rational calculation on a firm factual basis. The trial judge’s instructions on but-for causation, natural and probable consequence, and contemplation at contracting were appropriate. The law did not categorically exclude this type of claim simply because reputation might help explain an employer’s decision. (892–894.)

## Why the district court’s expression barrier failed
The district judge reasoned that people had to learn of cancellation and derive a message from it before it could affect Redgrave’s employment. He then treated that communicative link as protected expression requiring the plaintiff to isolate a materially false factual message communicated with knowledge or reckless disregard of falsity. The en banc court rejected that reasoning on these facts. BSO had not intended cancellation as symbolic speech or a statement about Redgrave. Its press release was drafted to avoid such implications and did not name her, and the jury rejected the particular implied-opinion message presented to it. (894–895 & nn. 4–5.)

Information being communicated about an act was different from the actor intentionally communicating a protected message through that act. Another employer could draw an inference from BSO’s decision even if BSO had not intended to express an idea. The cancellation could therefore cause loss without becoming protected symbolic speech. The court reserved the broader question whether an intentionally expressive cancellation might ever receive First Amendment protection. It imposed ordinary strict contract-damage requirements here, rather than the added defamation-derived hurdle. (894–896 & n. 4.)

## Why most of the $100,000 lacked support
The court viewed the evidence favorably to Redgrave and gave her reasonable inferences, but also considered uncontradicted opposing evidence. Favorable review did not permit conjecture about causation or amount. (896.)

Redgrave claimed a sharp loss of secure film offers during fourteen months after cancellation. Yet she accepted three offers that together would have produced $850,000 if the films had been made. Annie’s Coming Out offered $250,000; she expected it to proceed and declined other apparently secure work, including Yellowbeard and Who Will Love My Children? The accepted film later failed financially. No Alternatives offered $350,000, likewise failed financially, and produced a $25,000 contractual forfeiture payment. Track 39 offered $250,000 but also fell through. There was no sufficient evidence linking these production failures to BSO’s cancellation. (897–898 & nn. 7–10.)

The comparison with earlier offers was also weak: testimony often identified the work she actually performed rather than all offers received or their financial security. Even assuming the quality of later offers declined, she had to separate the cancellation’s effect from producers’ independently existing concerns about her politics. She had acknowledged lost work from political controversy before the BSO incident. A change of agents supplied a further possible explanation. No film producer directly explained a lost opportunity as a result of the cancellation. This record did not support the claimed film losses. (898–899 & n. 11.)

Most American theater evidence had the same problems. Three expected productions or opportunities did not materialize, but producers did not explain why; some never reached Broadway, and the proposed compensation was not shown. She had received no Broadway offers in 1981 and had historically accepted only one among many American theater offers, weakening assumptions about either a sudden post-cancellation decline or what she would necessarily have accepted. (897 n. 6; 899–900 & n. 12.)

Theodore Mann’s Heartbreak House testimony was different. He described deciding against hiring her partly because a premier organization with similar financial support had cancelled its engagement. One inference was that he independently feared the same controversy; another was that BSO’s example actually influenced the decision. The latter inference was reasonably available and had to be respected under the verdict-review standard. Mann also supplied an ascertainable contemplated fee of $12,000. Only that opportunity supported consequential recovery, reduced by expenses Redgrave would personally have incurred in performing it. The court did not sustain $12,000 for general reputational injury or order payment of the full fee without that expense adjustment. (899–900.)

## Civil-rights certification and the majority’s different ground
The Massachusetts Civil Rights Act allowed suit against private persons interfering with secured rights through threats, intimidation, or coercion; private status alone was not a defense. The Supreme Judicial Court answered that acquiescing in third-party pressure could create liability without a personal desire to interfere. It also rejected, as abstract justifications, fear of economic loss, business disruption, or physical harm, while reserving the possible significance of explicit and imminent physical danger not presented in the certified premise. Those answers defeated the district court’s acquiescence defense. (901–903 & nn. 13–15.)

The en banc majority nevertheless found that additional observations in the state court’s plurality, concurrence, and dissent showed no liability under Massachusetts law in this artistic setting. It distinguished the state judges’ positions rather than treating every observation as a formal holding. The concurrence expressly relied on the state constitutional right to determine what artistic works to perform; the state dissent construed the statute more narrowly and also questioned whether cancellation conveyed a threat of future BSO action. The federal majority predicted that a majority of the state court would deny liability on state statutory or state constitutional grounds, given constitutional-avoidance principles. (903, 907–910.)

The opinion discussed the difficult conflict between the performer’s statutory speech protection and the orchestra’s freedom to control artistic expression, including a right not to perform. It did so to explain the state court’s concerns. Its ultimate civil-rights ruling rested on its understanding of Massachusetts law, and it expressly declined to decide the existence or content of a federal First Amendment defense. Its denial of a federal expression barrier to ordinary contract damages therefore should not be confused with its different state-law treatment of the statutory civil-rights claim. (904–911 & nn. 16–23; 912.)

## Bownes and Selya’s partial dissent
Bownes and Selya agreed wholeheartedly with the contract analysis. They objected to assembling a state-law bar from separate minority positions and speculative readings of remarks on issues the state plurality expressly declined to decide. Certification supplied answers to questions, they argued, rather than a prediction of a case’s aggregate result from incompatible voting positions. They believed the federal court had to confront the unresolved constitutional issues instead of declaring an independent state ground. Their discussion of Michigan v. Long disputed the majority’s characterization of state and federal expression protections. (912–920 & dissent nn. 1–6.)

On the constitutional merits, they recognized artistic-expression rights but rejected the proposed breadth of an artistic-integrity defense. In their view, applying the statute to an orchestra that yielded to hostile third-party pressure served Massachusetts’s compelling interest in protecting political expression without dictating the production’s artistic content. They compared antidiscrimination precedents, distinguished cancellation as political symbolic speech from the actual decision here, and warned that broad immunity would allow a heckler’s veto and insulate discrimination whenever an institution characterized it as artistic judgment. They acknowledged that different applications of the statute, such as control of newspaper editorial decisions, could require different results. These were dissenting positions, not the court’s holding. (920–925 & dissent nn. 7–9.)

## Exact disposition and source scope
The court affirmed the civil-rights judgment, vacated the contract judgment, and remanded for entry of consequential damages to the approved extent, with no appellate costs. Its discussion retained the stipulated $27,500 performance-fee recovery while authorizing the additional $12,000 less expenses identified above. It did not order another trial to support the discarded balance of the $100,000 award. (890–891, 900, 912.)
