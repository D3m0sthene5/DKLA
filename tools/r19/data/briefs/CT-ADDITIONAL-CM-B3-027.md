# Bank One, N.A. v. Coates
125 F. Supp. 2d 819 (S.D. Miss. 2001) | Contracts

## Court, decision, and assigned setting
Chief Judge Tom S. Lee of the United States District Court for the Southern District of Mississippi, Jackson Division, decided this separate action to compel arbitration on January 2, 2001. He denied Coates’s requests for joinder and dismissal, abstention, and discovery and granted Bank One’s motion to compel arbitration. There is one district-court opinion, with thirteen judicial footnotes and no separate writing. The decision determines forum and enforceability, rather than whether the bank actually committed the financing wrongs alleged in the underlying action. (125 F. Supp. 2d at 819–821, 836.)

The assigned account on printed page 719 is an adapted problem involving depositors, signature cards, an account booklet, a monthly statement announcing arbitration, and a proposed declaratory class action. Those are not the actual satellite-financing facts of Coates. The reported opinion supplies a reasoned comparison for the problem: the content of the original amendment authority, the notice, the opportunity to reject, and the arbitration rules matter. Its outcome should not be transferred to an altered account agreement without examining those differences.

## Facts and procedural chronology
Coates financed a home satellite system through a Bank One revolving credit-card plan. On October 20, 1999, he and thirty-seven others sued Bank One and additional defendants in the Circuit Court of the Second Judicial District of Bolivar County, Mississippi, asserting claims arising from the purchases and alleged improper financing conduct. On November 22, none of the defendants had been served, but Sky Scanner Satellite voluntarily appeared and removed the case to the Northern District of Mississippi, invoking federal statutes including the Truth in Lending Act. Bank One did not remove that case. Judge Neal Biggers remanded it on August 23, 2000. Bank One had meanwhile been served on May 1 and answered on June 2. (Id. at 821 & nn. 1–3.)

On September 7, Bank One commenced this separate federal action against Coates under § 4 of the Federal Arbitration Act and filed identical actions against the other thirty-seven state plaintiffs. The state plaintiffs subsequently sought a trial setting, and the state judge also directed mediation. Coates responded to the motion to compel by asking the district court to abstain in favor of the state case. He alternatively sought to join additional parties, including the local agent or employee S. Broadwater, whose joinder would defeat diversity, and requested discovery before any ruling on arbitration. The procedural history matters to his charge that Bank One had already tried and failed to secure a federal forum: the prior removal belonged to another defendant and presented a different jurisdictional question. (Id. at 821–822, 824–825 & n. 6.)

The credit application that Coates signed stated immediately above the signature that he agreed to abide by the Account Agreement and Disclosure Statement issued from time to time. The agreement selected Ohio law. Its amendment provision authorized the bank to change or amend the agreement’s terms upon fifteen days’ prior written notice if required by law. A following sentence allowed amended fees, interest rates, finance charges, annual percentage rates, and minimum payments to apply to both an outstanding balance and future transactions. The court read that second sentence as addressing the operation of those particular changes, rather than restricting the broader amendment power in the first sentence. (Id. at 825–826, 831.)

On March 8, 1998, Bank One sent cardholders a notice adding arbitration. The provision covered claims by either party against the other, its employees, agents, or assigns, arising from or relating to the agreement or account, including the arbitration provision’s applicability or the agreement’s validity. It designated the National Arbitration Forum and its procedural code, provided for a hearing in the federal judicial district containing the customer’s billing address, invoked the FAA, and expressly warned that claims otherwise litigable in court or through representative litigation would instead have to be arbitrated. (Id. at 826.)

The amendment would take effect on April 15, 1998. A cardholder could reject it by mailing a written notice containing identifying account information by that date; rejection would not change the prior obligation to pay the outstanding balance under the prior terms. Coates did not reject. He did not dispute these contract and notice facts or contend that his claims fell outside the clause’s language. His position was that he never agreed to arbitration and that the purported agreement was unenforceable. (Id. at 826–827.)

## Issues and governing framework
The court first considered whether it possessed an independent basis of federal jurisdiction, whether the absent participants in the underlying transaction had to be joined, and whether parallel state litigation justified abstention. It then considered agreement formation and validity, the need for discovery, fraud, procedural and substantive unconscionability, and the asserted need for a separate clear waiver of jury trial. (Id. at 821–828.)

The FAA authorizes enforcement but does not itself supply federal subject-matter jurisdiction. On the contract issues, the court used the familiar inquiry into a valid agreement, the dispute’s coverage, and any external legal constraint on arbitration. Section 2 permits generally applicable contract defenses. The Ohio choice-of-law provision supplied the relevant state contract rules, including the court’s requirement that an unconscionability challenge show both procedural and substantive unconscionability. A genuine dispute over the making of an arbitration agreement may require a trial under § 4, but an unsupported declaration that the party never agreed does not itself establish such a dispute. (Id. at 822, 827–828, 830.)

## Jurisdiction, joinder, and abstention
The parties acknowledged that the claims did not establish federal-question jurisdiction in light of the earlier remand ruling. Diversity nevertheless existed between Bank One, identified as an Ohio corporation, and Coates, a Mississippi resident, with an amount in controversy above $75,000. The court refused to join the local participant under Rule 19. This action sought only to decide whether Bank One and Coates had to arbitrate their claims against each other. Other alleged joint tortfeasors could remain parties to the state litigation without becoming indispensable to that narrower federal controversy. Accordingly, Coates’s joinder and consequential dismissal request failed. The original report’s isolated reference to “Bank One’s motion for joinder” at 822 conflicts with the surrounding description and final order; the motion was Coates’s. (Id. at 821–822, 836.)

Abstention under Colorado River and Moses H. Cone requires exceptional circumstances, with the balance favoring exercise of jurisdiction. There was no property under either court’s control; both forums were in Mississippi, and the federal forum’s convenience did not favor surrendering jurisdiction. The prospect of piecemeal proceedings did not overcome the FAA’s policy of enforcing agreements even where arbitrable and nonarbitrable disputes must proceed separately. The earlier state filing and later trial setting likewise were not decisive. Federal arbitration law supplied important rules, and the adequacy of a state forum did not by itself furnish a reason to abandon the federal one. Lee also considered the relative progress and asserted concurrent enforcement authority, recording the then-existing uncertainty over a state court’s power under § 4 rather than announcing a timeless jurisdictional conclusion. The collection of factors did not supply a clear justification for abstention. (Id. at 822–825 & nn. 4–6.)

## Discovery, fraud, and assent to the amendment
Coates identified two discovery subjects in his memorandum: whether he had clearly agreed to arbitrate and what arbitration would entail in cost and procedure. Lee found the relevant agreement and notice available in the documents, Coates’s own knowledge ascertainable from him, and the incorporated Forum rules available without discovery. The FAA contemplated an expeditious, restricted inquiry into making the arbitration agreement. Footnote 7 also addressed a consideration and mutuality topic mentioned in the motion but not developed in the memorandum or as a substantive defense. The court found no demonstrated mutuality defect and cited Ohio authority concerning contract modification without separate consideration unless otherwise required. It did not deny discovery merely because every arbitration request is immune from factual inquiry. (Id. at 828–829 & n. 7.)

Coates’s fraud affidavit said that the salesperson had not informed him he was signing for a Bank One credit card. The court distinguished an attack on procurement of the overall credit agreement from fraud specifically concerning the agreement to arbitrate. Under Prima Paint and the Fifth Circuit authorities it applied, the former belonged to arbitration. Lee also rejected Coates’s attempt to escape that distinction by labeling the allegation fraud in the factum, relying on the circuit’s then-existing decision in R. M. Perez. The opinion’s footnote 9 explained a similar clause-specific distinction for coercion and duress. This is the historical reasoning employed in this 2001 decision; it is not a certification of the present treatment of every formation defense. Nor did the court find that the salesperson actually committed fraud. (Id. at 829–830 & nn. 8–9.)

On assent, the court treated the signed application’s incorporation and the original agreement’s broad amendment authority as the starting point. Ohio banking law allowed the parties to agree to amendments. The subsequent notice unmistakably addressed arbitration, gave instructions for rejection, and supplied a meaningful opportunity to remain under the prior terms. Coates did not specifically deny receipt, although he did not recall the notice. The court rejected his characterization that arbitration had been hidden among multiple unrelated changes: the notice concerned that single subject, prominently warned of its effect, and explained the opt-out. Small, consistent, legible print did not alone establish procedural unconscionability. (Id. at 830–834.)

The court compared decisions concerning amendments to banking agreements rather than treating silence as a freestanding acceptance rule. It distinguished Badie on the agreement and notice features it understood that case to present, and discussed Long in footnote 12, including that case’s successive amendment notices and separate retroactivity problem. It did not adopt those other courts’ facts as Coates’s facts. Its holding rested on the actual amendment language, notice, rejection mechanism, and failure to reject in this record. (Id. at 831–833 & nn. 10–12.)

## Jury trial, costs, remedies, and impartiality
Lee rejected the demand for a distinct clear and unmistakable waiver of the Seventh Amendment jury right. A valid agreement to place the dispute in arbitration necessarily removes the corresponding judicial jury trial. That reasoning presupposed a valid arbitration agreement; it did not substitute a jury-waiver rule for the antecedent formation analysis. (Id. at 834.)

Coates also said arbitration would be prohibitively costly, prevent available remedies, and place the dispute before a biased forum. The incorporated Forum code allowed arbitrators to grant remedies available under applicable substantive law, including attorney’s fees where permitted. Its fee schedule provided relatively modest filing and administrative fees for small claims, allowed possible relief for an indigent consumer, and permitted an arbitrator to shift fees paid by the prevailing party. Lee applied Green Tree Financial v. Randolph’s requirement that a resisting party establish the likelihood of prohibitive expense, rather than assume such expense from an unsupported assertion. Footnote 13 discussed Justice Ginsburg’s separate writing in Randolph and its treatment of consumer cost protocols; that is a cited separate opinion, not a dissent in Coates. The listed fees and Forum procedures are historical features of the record, not a claim about current availability. (Id. at 834–835 & n. 13.)

The Forum appointed independent third-party arbitrators rather than adjudicating claims itself. Its rules barred those arbitrators from serving as Forum officers or directors, required neutrality and conflict disclosure, and allowed each party one peremptory strike and unlimited challenges for cause. Legal remedies and injunctive relief remained available, and a party could request a written opinion. Against those protections, Coates supplied speculation about institutional bias rather than a concrete demonstration that this agreement was unenforceable. The court therefore rejected the substantive-unconscionability challenge as well. (Id. at 835–836.)

## Holding, disposition, and significance
The court denied Coates’s motions for joinder and dismissal, discovery, and abstention and granted Bank One’s motion to compel arbitration. It did not decide the truth of the financing allegations, award damages, or expressly issue an injunction against the pending state litigation. (Id. at 836.)

For the assigned amendment problem, the useful principle is a sequence of contract inquiries: what amendment power the customer originally accepted, what the later notice actually communicated, whether rejection was available, whether a valid clause covers the particular dispute, and whether the procedure imposes a legally established defect. Coates enforced this amendment on its concrete record. The adapted depositor problem must be evaluated on its own terms rather than silently substituted for the reported credit-financing dispute.
