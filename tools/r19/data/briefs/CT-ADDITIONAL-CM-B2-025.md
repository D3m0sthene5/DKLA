# Zelnick v. Adams
263 Va. 601, 561 S.E.2d 711 (2002) | Contracts

## Decision and stage
Virginia’s Supreme Court held that legal services fall within the general class of necessaries that can overcome an infancy defense, but whether these particular services were necessary required factfinding. It reversed the judgment and remanded. The court neither enforced the agreed one-third contingent fee nor preserved an unconditional $60,000 quantum-meruit award despite a successful infancy defense. Justice Lemons delivered the April 19, 2002 opinion in Record No. 011390; no separate writing appears in the complete report, which does not specify a numerical vote. The companion appeal by Adams, Record No. 011391, was resolved by a separate order; this opinion explains its related pleading issue in footnote 1. (601, 604, 607, 610 n.1, 612.)

## Trust interest and retainer
Jonathan Adams was born April 5, 1980, to unmarried parents, Mildred Adams and Cecil Hylton Jr. A contested Florida proceeding ended with an agreed September 1995 order establishing paternity. Jonathan’s grandfather had died in 1989, leaving trusts under a will that gave executors or trustees authority to determine whether persons claiming descent outside marriage were his issue. Two grandchildren’s charitable trusts contemplated potential distributions in 2014 and 2021, but their future amounts remained uncertain because assets and beneficiary numbers could change. (604–605.)

In July 1996 Jonathan’s mother consulted attorney Robert Zelnick after hearing that grandchildren might already be receiving distributions. She said her inquiries to Jonathan’s father, the will’s drafting firm, and the trustees had produced no useful information about whether Jonathan would be recognized. She supplied the Florida paternity order and, unable to afford an hourly fee, requested contingent representation. After investigating and reviewing the will, Zelnick agreed to seek Jonathan’s recognition as a beneficiary. He discussed the estate’s substantial size and the impossibility of knowing the ultimate contingent fee. On July 19 she signed a retainer for one-third of recovery in Jonathan’s claim against the estate. (605.)

Zelnick wrote the executors in November and December demanding recognition. Estate counsel responded that the trustees knew of the paternity order but should evaluate the claim when distributions became payable; counsel considered the issue not yet ripe. Zelnick filed a declaratory and accounting suit in May 1997, later joining potential beneficiaries. A January 23, 1998 consent decree declared Jonathan the grandfather’s issue and entitled to benefits payable to grandchildren and issue under the will and trusts. It did not establish the amount of future distributions. (605–606.)

## Fee litigation below
In March 1998 Jonathan’s father sued on his behalf to invalidate the fee contract. After majority Jonathan intervened, replaced his father, and expressly disaffirmed. In April 2000 he sought summary judgment, arguing that the Florida order made the later litigation unnecessary, distributions would not occur until 2014 and 2021, and the fee arrangement was unreasonable. The trial court declared the contract void and held the necessaries doctrine inapplicable, reasoning both that Jonathan had been a minor and that the matter could have awaited majority. (606–607.)

Despite that ruling, the trial court awarded Zelnick $60,000 in quantum meruit with prejudgment interest from the January 1998 consent decree. Zelnick testified to approximately 150–200 hours and a $200 hourly rate during 1996–1997; the court emphasized the benefit of obtaining recognition in the trust. Jonathan objected that quantum meruit had not been pleaded. Both sides appealed; the present opinion addressed Zelnick’s appeal, while noting Jonathan’s separate companion disposition. (607.)

## Necessaries requires classification and actual need
A minor’s contract under Virginia law is ordinarily voidable, not void from inception. Following Bear, the court distinguished the judge’s threshold decisions from the factfinder’s ultimate determination. The judge must first decide whether the supplied goods or services can belong to a general class of necessaries and whether sufficient evidence supports submission of actual necessity. If either inquiry fails, the supplier cannot recover on the disaffirmed agreement. If both pass, the factfinder assesses the minor’s position and condition, all circumstances, and whether the minor was already sufficiently supplied. (608–610.)

Legal services can belong to the class, including protection of liberty, reputation, personal injury claims, or property interests. The court relied on decisions recognizing that minors’ ability to protect their interests can depend on counsel being reasonably compensated. But general classification is not proof that every legal service benefits a minor enough to warrant recovery. The factual inquiry remained essential for these trust-related services. (608–609.)

Even if actual necessity defeats the infancy plea, the minor is bound on an implied obligation to pay reasonable value, not automatically on the express agreement. Conversely, if the services were not necessary and the infancy defense succeeds, a court may not circumvent that result with an independent quantum-meruit award. Quantum meruit here describes the measure of recovery under the necessaries-based implied contract, rather than a free-standing exception to minority protection. Footnote 1 explains that reasonable-value recovery was inherent in this dispute over supplied necessaries and thus properly before the trial court despite the absence of an affirmative quantum-meruit pleading. (608, 610 & n.1.)

## Errors requiring remand
Minority at signing could not itself exclude the necessaries doctrine; it was the very condition that made the exception relevant. Legal services belonged to a potentially qualifying class. The trial court’s second ground—that the suit could have awaited majority—was a factual determination unsupported by this summary-judgment record. Necessity had to be assessed in the circumstances when services were rendered, without imposing a hindsight burden on the supplier. The court stated that the specific timing issue had not been presented or supported with evidence as a summary-judgment ground. It did not thereby find, on the merits, that waiting necessarily would have harmed Jonathan or that the Florida order was inadequate. (611–612.)

The recorded summary-judgment discussion earlier recounts Jonathan’s reliance on delayed distributions and lack of ripeness; the later analysis distinguishes that argument from evidence that services should have been delayed until majority. The draft preserves that distinction rather than erasing the arguments from the procedural history. (606–607, 611–612.)

## Exact result and possible recovery
The court reversed and remanded for evidence on actual necessity under all the circumstances. If the doctrine did not apply, the contract would be avoided and no award made. If the evidence defeated the infancy plea, the court was to hear evidence of reasonable value. A contingent award at an appropriate percentage was not categorically excluded, but any award required full evidentiary support; the original one-third fee was not automatically enforceable. Footnote 2 identified the special difficulty of an immediately executable judgment when possible trust receipts had been anticipated only in 2014 and 2021. Thus neither the prior $60,000 fee nor an alternative amount was finally adjudicated as due by this disposition. (612 & n.2.)
