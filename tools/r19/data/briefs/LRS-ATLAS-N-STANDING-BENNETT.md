# Bennett v. Spear

**520 U.S. 154 (1997) — Supreme Court of the United States — March 19, 1997.** Justice Scalia wrote for a unanimous Court. There were no separate opinions. The supplied November continuation retains the ending of a commentary about Bennett at physical page 134, printed page 1217; the account's opening is absent from that file.

Irrigation districts and ranch operators alleged that an inadequately supported endangered-species determination would reduce their irrigation water. The Court allowed their challenge to proceed. Economic interests did not categorically disqualify them from enforcing the Endangered Species Act, their allegations satisfied Article III at the pleading stage, and the biological opinion had sufficiently concrete legal effects to constitute final agency action. The Court distinguished which claims could proceed under the ESA's citizen-suit provision and which required APA review; it did not decide whether the biological opinion was scientifically or legally correct.

## Facts and claims

The Bureau of Reclamation administered the Klamath Irrigation Project, a network of lakes, rivers, dams, and canals in northern California and southern Oregon. In 1992, the Bureau advised the Fish and Wildlife Service that project operations might affect two fish listed as endangered: the Lost River sucker and the shortnose sucker. Under ESA §7, an agency proposing action that might affect listed species had to consult with the Service. The resulting biological opinion assessed jeopardy and, when necessary, identified reasonable and prudent alternatives. An accompanying incidental-take statement specified the conditions under which otherwise prohibited taking could occur. **157–159.**

The Service concluded that the project's long-term operation was likely to jeopardize the fish. Its alternative included maintaining minimum water levels in Clear Lake and Gerber reservoirs. The Bureau announced that it would comply. Two Oregon irrigation districts and operators of two ranches alleged that the restrictions would substantially reduce the water available to them. They sued the Service's director and regional director and the Interior Secretary, rather than the Bureau or its officials. **159–160.**

Their first two relevant claims attacked the jeopardy determination and water restrictions under §1536. They alleged that the available scientific and commercial evidence did not show that existing operations harmed the fish or that the new restrictions would benefit them. Their third claim alleged that the minimum-water requirements effectively designated critical habitat without considering economic impacts as §1533(b)(2) required. Each also alleged arbitrary or unlawful agency action under the APA. Those were allegations to be tested later, not factual conclusions the Supreme Court accepted on the merits. A fourth, NEPA-based claim was outside Supreme Court review because petitioners did not challenge its appellate dismissal. **159–160 & n.1.**

## Procedure and opposing arguments

The District Court dismissed for lack of jurisdiction, reasoning that the plaintiffs' recreational, aesthetic, and commercial interests were outside the ESA's protected zone. The Ninth Circuit affirmed. It applied the zone-of-interests requirement to both APA review and the ESA citizen-suit provision and regarded an interest in preserving endangered species as necessary. **160–161.**

The government did not defend that reasoning in the Supreme Court. It instead argued that the plaintiffs lacked Article III standing, that their claims fell outside the ESA citizen-suit causes of action, and that the biological opinion was not final agency action reviewable under the APA. Although the lower courts had not decided these alternatives, the arguments had been raised below and fully presented in the Supreme Court. The Court chose to resolve them rather than leave them all for remand. **161, 165–166.**

## Constitutional standing at the pleading stage

The Court applied injury, causation, and redressability with attention to the litigation's procedural stage. General allegations could suffice on a motion to dismiss even though evidence would be necessary at summary judgment or trial. Plaintiffs alleged that available irrigation water would decrease and that they would be harmed. The Court could presume supporting specifics, such as a proportional distribution of the reduction among the Bureau's customers; it did not find that this was the Bureau's proven allocation method. **166–168.**

The government argued that the Bureau's intervening choice, rather than the Service's opinion, would determine any shortage. The Court rejected a requirement that the defendant's action be the final step in the causal chain. A defendant can cause injury by exerting a determinative or coercive influence on another actor. The biological opinion was nominally advisory, but departing from it required justification and exposed the Bureau and its employees to substantial risk if they took protected species unlawfully. Compliance with the incidental-take conditions, by contrast, provided protection against the statutory taking prohibition. **168–170.**

The opinion therefore altered the Bureau's legal position. Plaintiffs also alleged that the Bureau had followed essentially the same operating practices before the opinion and would now comply with its restrictions. At this stage, that was enough to allege both a causal connection and the likelihood that setting aside the opinion would remove the challenged water-level restrictions. The ruling established pleading sufficiency, not an evidentiary guarantee of additional water. **169–171.**

## Statutory standing and available causes of action

The ESA citizen-suit provision allowed any person to commence specified civil actions. The Court read that unusually broad authorization, together with the environmental subject and private-enforcement design, as expanding the permissible zone to Article III's limits. The provision supplied litigation costs, removed ordinary citizenship and amount requirements, and preserved government enforcement and intervention opportunities. Its breadth applied to permitted claims of overenforcement as well as underenforcement; the text did not reserve access to environmental advocates. Congress could relax the then-described prudential zone requirement, but not eliminate Article III. **161–166 & n.2.**

Broad eligibility to sue nevertheless did not create a cause of action for every administrative complaint. Section 1540(g)(1)(C) authorized suits against the Secretary for failing to perform nondiscretionary duties under §1533. The economic-impact claim fit that provision: discretion over the ultimate habitat designation did not permit ignoring required scientific and economic considerations. **171–172.**

The §1536 claims did not fit subsection (C), which expressly referred to §1533. Nor did they fit subsection (A)'s authorization to enjoin ESA violations. In statutory context, that provision concerned regulated persons' substantive violations, not every error by the Secretary in administering the Act. Otherwise, subsection (C)'s carefully limited administrative-review authorization would become superfluous, and the citizen-suit route would circumvent the APA's finality requirement. **172–174.**

Those claims could instead proceed under the APA. Nothing in the ESA displaced that review. For APA standing, the Court examined the particular allegedly violated provisions, rather than the ESA's general species-preservation purpose. Section 1536's requirement to use the best available scientific and commercial data served not only conservation but also avoidance of unnecessary economic disruption from erroneous jeopardy findings. Plaintiffs claiming injury from precisely that error were within its protected zone. **175–177.**

## Finality and judgment

The Court identified two ordinary conditions for final agency action: completion of the agency's decisionmaking process and determination of rights or obligations, or other legal consequences. The first was uncontested. The second followed from the biological opinion and incidental-take statement's alteration of the Bureau's legal regime. Unlike a purely advisory recommendation that left another official's legal position unchanged, this opinion carried direct and appreciable consequences even though the Bureau retained choices. Finality was distinct from identifying the last causal step producing plaintiffs' harm. **177–178.**

The Court **reversed and remanded**. The complaint adequately alleged Article III standing; the §1533 claim could proceed under the ESA citizen-suit provision and the other relevant ESA claims under the APA. No damages were awarded, no reservoir levels were ordered, and no finding that the Service had actually violated the ESA was made. **179.**

## Assignment and significance

The surviving Bennett commentary starts mid-sentence and includes its concluding discussion and editorial notes 2–4; all were read against the original scan. Its reference to maintaining high reservoir levels should not replace the actual holding: the opinion says relief would likely remove the challenged minimum-level restrictions, making more irrigation water available. The Court did not promise continued high reservoir levels. The commentary correctly emphasizes the difference between dismissal on the complaint and the more demanding proof required at later stages.

This historical decision separates constitutional standing, statutory eligibility, a particular cause of action, and agency finality. Success at one step does not automatically establish the others or win the underlying merits. Its description of the zone test as prudential reports the 1997 opinion; it is not an independently verified assertion that every aspect of that terminology remains current law.
