# Darner Motor Sales, Inc. v. Universal Underwriters Insurance Co.
140 Ariz. 383, 682 P.2d 388 (1984) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
The Arizona Supreme Court decided Darner on March 29, 1984; reconsideration was denied May 22. Justice Feldman wrote the majority opinion, joined by Vice Chief Justice Gordon and Justice Hays. Justice Cameron expressly concurred in the decision and opinion and wrote separately; Chief Justice Holohan dissented. The result was 4–1. The court vacated the Court of Appeals' memorandum decision, reversed summary judgment on equitable estoppel, reformation, negligent misrepresentation, and fraud, and vacated the appellate attorney-fee award. It allowed the disputed claims to proceed to trial; it did not finally reform the policy, award damages, or establish additional coverage. (140 Ariz. at 383, 385, 400–402.)

## Insurance transaction and disputed assurances
Darner operated an automobile sales, service, and leasing business. In 1973, Doxsee, a full-time employee and agent of Universal, solicited its insurance business. The November 1973 U-Drive policy provided limits of $100,000 per person and $300,000 per accident for Darner but $15,000/$30,000 for lessees. Other dealership insurance remained with Travelers. In April 1975, Darner acquired Universal's Unicover umbrella package and renewed the U-Drive coverage. (Id. at 385–386.)

Joel Darner wanted lessees covered at $100,000/$300,000. Unlike the lengthy umbrella policy, he read the U-Drive limits and contacted Doxsee about the discrepancy. Darner's rental form promised the higher limits, creating a particular concern about liability to renters. Darner and sales manager Hadley testified that Doxsee assured them the umbrella would supply the higher coverage. Doxsee did not recall that conversation but denied he would have made such an assurance. These were disputed accounts on summary judgment, not judicial findings that the assurance occurred. Footnote 3 also describes the subsequent reduction of the rental form's limits and Darner's explanation that Doxsee had suggested it to discourage plaintiffs' lawyers. (Id. at 385–386 & n. 3.)

The umbrella later arrived as a lengthy loose-leaf book, maintained by periodic replacement pages. Darner did not read it. The actual umbrella policy was absent from the appellate record. The court therefore assumed against Darner that it clearly and unambiguously excluded lessees from the relevant definition of insured; it did not find an ambiguity in a policy it had examined. Missing-record notes are important because the case turns on the relationship between standardized terms and the alleged bargain, rather than a newly discovered textual ambiguity. (Id. at 385 n. 1, 386–387 & n. 5.)

About twenty months after the transaction, renter Crawford, using an older rental form promising the higher limits, negligently injured a pedestrian. The pedestrian recovered $60,000 against him. Universal paid $15,000; Darner had paid or was liable for the remaining $45,000. The opinion preserves that alternative and does not unequivocally say Darner had already paid the entire difference. Darner brought third-party claims against Universal and Doxsee under four related theories. Both lower courts rejected them, principally through the policy terms and a duty to read. (Id. at 386–387.)

## Standardized terms and the induced bargain
The majority criticized treating an insurance form as though every provision reflected an individually negotiated intention. Although standardized agreements serve necessary commercial functions, most boilerplate is neither negotiated nor read. Creating fictitious ambiguity to escape an unjust result was also unsatisfactory. The better starting point was to ask what expectations had actually been induced by a promise or conduct. A policyholder's hope, especially after a loss, that all losses are covered does not itself qualify. (Id. at 387–390 & n. 6.)

The court adopted Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 211. Its first two subsections ordinarily bind a person who assents to a writing known to be a standardized agreement and interpret the form alike for similarly situated customers, regardless of their individual understanding of every standard term. Subsection (3) supplies the limit: a particular term is not part of the agreement if the drafter has reason to believe the adhering party would not assent if aware of it. Prior negotiations and surrounding circumstances may show that reason. A bizarre or oppressive term, one that defeats expressly agreed terms, or one that eliminates the transaction's dominant purpose can support the inference; lack of opportunity to read, illegibility, or concealment reinforces it. (Id. at 391–392 & nn. 7–8.)

This approach enforces ordinary standardized terms while denying boilerplate the power to defeat a bargain the seller knows it contradicts. It also treats integration as a question informed by the circumstances, including negotiations, prior understandings, and subsequent conduct. The majority linked its rule to Arizona's existing contextual approach to negotiated agreements. It did not abolish writings, integration, or the duty to read. Negotiated transactions remain subject to ordinary reading obligations; the new formulation addresses standardized provisions that customers ordinarily are not expected to read and cannot meaningfully negotiate. Footnotes 8 and 9 explain comparable mass transactions and reproduce an airline-ticket example. (Id. at 392–394 & nn. 8–9.)

## Equitable estoppel and reformation
Estoppel requires conduct inducing a belief, justifiable action in reliance, and resulting injury. The court rejected a categorical rule that estoppel can never establish insurance coverage beyond printed limitations. If the factfinder determined that Darner and Doxsee agreed to $100,000/$300,000 lessee coverage and Darner justifiably relied on the assurance without knowing the umbrella exclusion, Universal could be estopped from asserting the boilerplate definition that excluded lessees. Actual delivery of the policy before the loss did not automatically defeat the claim. (Id. at 394–396 & nn. 10–11.)

Reasonableness remained a factual question. Footnote 12 contrasts failure to read a standard definition, particularly after an assurance, with ignoring a clearly disclosed limit in a declaration page containing bargained terms. The latter could defeat estoppel. The holding thus permits proof of reliance and the induced bargain rather than presuming all unread terms unenforceable. (Id. at 396 & n. 12.)

Reformation based on one party's mistake requires fraud or inequitable conduct by the other, including knowledge of the mistake. Darner undisputedly expressed concern about the lower lessee limits; what Doxsee understood and said remained disputed. That evidence could establish that the writing differed from the actual agreement and justify reformation. A party whose inequitable conduct caused acceptance of the erroneous instrument could not simply invoke the other's failure to read. Summary judgment on this alternative theory was therefore also improper. (Id. at 396.)

## Professional negligence and fraud
Doxsee represented Universal, not Darner. He owed no special fiduciary duty as Darner's agent. But an insurance professional still must exercise reasonable care, skill, and diligence in procuring insurance and providing the professional advice undertaken. The court distinguished that tort duty from agency status and rejected a requirement of separate consideration for the advice. Proof of the professional standard might require expert evidence; footnote 14 leaves open whether an insurer's employee has a different standard from an independent agent. (Id. at 396–398 & n. 14.)

Failure to read did not conclusively bar the negligence claim. The reasonableness of reliance and any contributory negligence were factual matters, subject to Arizona's constitutional allocation of contributory-negligence questions to the factfinder. Footnote 13 permits the alternative theories even if their underlying factual accounts prove inconsistent: a jury could reject the contract theory yet find negligent failure to advise about proper coverage. Footnote 15 also identifies negligent misrepresentation under Restatement (Second) of Torts § 552. (Id. at 397–400 & nn. 13–15.)

Fraud still required proof of all nine established elements. A licensed insurance seller's alleged misstatement of actual coverage differed from a layperson's legal opinion about an agreement, and earlier duty-to-read cases themselves recognized exceptions. The deposition evidence on fraud appeared especially weak, but the allegations and supporting testimony were not wholly without merit and overlapped the other disputed claims. The court allowed fraud to proceed as well; it did not hold that Doxsee committed it. (Id. at 398–400.)

## Separate writings and limits
Cameron's concurrence emphasized that standardized forms continue to bind customers and that sophisticated businesspeople receive no absolute privilege to disregard them. Whether Darner was reasonably not expected to read these particular provisions was for trial. Only expectations induced by words or conduct, rather than impression or imagination, mattered. He understood § 211 as applying ordinary contractual limits to standardized forms. (Id. at 400–401.)

Holohan would have approved the appellate decision. He characterized the majority as abandoning precedent and allowing oral accounts to undermine written insurance limits. He feared uncertain risk calculation, effects on other financing documents, and incentives not to read. Those were dissenting criticisms of the rule, not an additional majority holding that written agreements had become irrelevant. (Id. at 401–402.)
