# Rodi v. Southern New England School of Law
532 F.3d 11 (1st Cir. 2008) | Contracts

## Holding and specific stage
The First Circuit affirmed summary judgment for an unaccredited law school and its former deans on fraudulent-misrepresentation and consumer-protection claims. Even assuming material false statements intended to induce continued attendance, no reasonable jury could find the student's actual reliance or reasonable reliance on this record. Howard wrote the June 30, 2008 opinion for Torruella, Lipez, and Howard; no separate writing is reported. The court also rejected a preserved reassignment challenge and treated the distinct recusal argument as waived. (11, 13, 15–20.)

This was the post-discovery 2008 appeal. An earlier 2004 appeal had partly reversed dismissal at the pleading stage and allowed further proceedings. Recognition of a colorable pleaded fraud claim did not determine whether the developed evidence could sustain that claim. The earlier decision is procedural background here, not a second judgment combined into the present brief. (14–15.)

## Accreditation representations and transfer attempts
Joseph Rodi applied to Southern New England School of Law in March 1997, knowing it lacked ABA accreditation. The ABA Accreditation Committee then recommended provisional accreditation, which still required approval by two other ABA bodies. Dean Francis Larkin told prospective students he was highly confident of favorable approvals in August. Rodi enrolled intending eventually to take the New Jersey bar examination, which required a degree from an ABA-accredited school. Provisional approval would have provided graduates the relevant recognition, although full approval required at least two years of provisional status. (13 & nn. 1–2.)

In August the ABA denied provisional accreditation, citing substantial noncompliance and other concerns. At a September student meeting Larkin said the school had corrected its deficiencies and promised approval on the next application before the students graduated. The school's catalogue, however, disclaimed representing that approval would occur before any student's graduation. (13–14.)

After his first year, in summer 1998, Rodi applied to transfer to Rutgers and Seton Hall. New acting dean David Prentiss asked him to reconsider transferring and expressed optimism about ultimate ABA approval. Rodi did not withdraw either application; both schools denied admission. Discovery showed that both deans privately doubted the prospects they described: Larkin lacked high confidence after the first rejection, and Prentiss recognized the school's limited resources and did not know how the ABA would rule. The record is presented in the light favorable to Rodi, not as a trial finding that every alleged fraudulent statement was proved. (14.)

The Accreditation Committee rejected the renewed application in November 1999, citing continuing noncompliance and the absence of a reliable three-year compliance plan. The school did not appeal. Rodi graduated in September 2000 without the accreditation needed for New Jersey bar eligibility. (14.)

## Litigation and fraud requirements
A July 2002 New Jersey federal suit was dismissed for lack of personal jurisdiction. Rodi filed in Massachusetts in June 2003 against the school, Larkin, and Prentiss, alleging fraud and violations of Massachusetts Chapter 93A. After the initial dismissal and partial appellate reversal, the original district judge retained the case over a reassignment motion. Discovery followed, then summary judgment on both substantive claims. The district court assumed possible false material statements but found reliance unreasonable as a matter of law. (14–15 & n. 3.)

The appellate court reviewed summary judgment de novo and applied Massachusetts law in this diversity case. Fraud required a knowing false representation of material fact intended to induce action, detrimental actual reliance, and reasonable reliance under the circumstances. Although reasonableness ordinarily belonged to a jury, summary judgment was proper when no reasonable jury could find it. Statements by other faculty and administrators were not examined because the requisite knowing inducement and reasonable reliance arguments had not been developed. (15 & n. 4.)

Rodi alleged that the deans feared an exodus of students, spoke knowingly to retain him, and induced him to forgo alternatives such as a Ph.D. He claimed lost tuition, employment income during attendance, and possible financial benefits from a doctorate. These were alleged losses, not awarded damages. The court assumed the disputed false-material-statement and inducement premises and resolved the claim on its reliance deficiencies. (16.)

## Actual reliance was inadequately supported
The continued transfer applications contradicted the claim that confidence in the deans' accreditation assurances kept Rodi at the school. He offered no explanation reconciling his persistence in transferring with asserted belief in those assurances. The court did not make transfer applications inherently incompatible with reliance: it expressly suggested possible explanations, such as wanting to study closer to home or finding withdrawal inconvenient, but those explanations had not been supplied as evidence. (16 & n. 5.)

A conclusory assertion of reliance did not overcome that record. The Ph.D. alternative likewise lacked persuasive evidentiary support when the assurances had not even persuaded him to abandon his existing transfer applications. The court concluded that no reasonable jury could find actual reliance, while separately explaining why any such reliance would be unreasonable. (16–17.)

## Reasonableness depended on content and context
The only representations pleaded with the required particularity were Larkin's September 1997 statement and Prentiss's July 1998 letter. General student affidavits about numerous assurances did not supply the missing who, what, where, and when. Larkin's additional promise to reapply was not analyzed as deception because the school had actually reapplied. (17 & nn. 6–7.)

Larkin's assurance that significant deficiencies had been corrected in four weeks was implausible in context. His promise of accreditation before graduation also encountered three independent warnings: the catalogue disclaimer, the ABA's independent control over approval, and his earlier failed forecast of August approval. The disclaimer was relevant to reasonableness, but the court expressly rejected treating disclaimers as automatic defenses to fraud. Students had already seen the ABA exercise its independent authority against the school's optimism. A later failure to obtain approval before the then-third-year students graduated supplied another warning under the assurance allegedly given to the entire student body. (17–18 & n. 8.)

Prentiss's letter was weaker still. It predicted ultimate approval, rather than accreditation before Rodi's graduation, while the time available to finish at an accredited institution was shrinking. Its restrained optimism offered insufficient assurance for his time-sensitive objective. Trust in the deans did not overcome the content and context of their words, nor did the ordinary jury role make reasonableness invariably a jury question. Both reliance grounds therefore supported summary judgment. (18.)

## Consumer protection, recusal, and reassignment
Chapter 93A prohibited unfair or deceptive practices in trade or commerce. For this particular claim based on fraudulent misrepresentation, Rodi had to establish reasonable reliance; its failure defeated the statutory claim as presented. The court did not state that every possible Chapter 93A theory required proof of common-law fraud. It reached the merits rather than resolving the school's separate objection about the thirty-day demand letter. (18–19 & n. 9.)

On appeal Rodi asserted that the district judge's graduation speech, honorary degree, and favorable comments about accreditation warranted recusal. He had not moved for recusal below, and his reassignment motion had raised only time savings. The recusal argument was therefore waived, rather than substantively rejected on a finding that those connections could never matter. (19.)

The reassignment question was preserved. Under the local rule governing remands, retention was permitted if required by the remand or if it substantially saved time and the interests of justice did not require a different judge. Applying abuse-of-discretion review, the court upheld retention because the judge knew the background and procedural history and had already conducted a status conference. Lack of an answer or completed discovery at that earlier point did not negate the savings finding. (19.)

## Exact judgment and complete-source scope
The court affirmed the district court's judgment in full. It awarded no damages and ordered no new trial or reassignment. Its dispositive fraud analysis assumed rather than conclusively resolved the deans' alleged knowing falsehoods, and turned on the evidentiary and contextual problems with reliance. (16–20.)
