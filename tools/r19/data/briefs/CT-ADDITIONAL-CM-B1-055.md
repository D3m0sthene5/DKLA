# Bishop v. Eaton
161 Mass. 496, 37 N.E. 665 (June 19, 1894) | Contracts

## Decision and posture

The Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court sustained the defendant’s exceptions and required a new trial. Bishop had adequately notified Eaton that he performed the act invited by an offer of guaranty, even though Eaton denied receiving the properly mailed letter. But an extension of the underlying note’s maturity without the guarantor’s consent discharged the guaranty unless Eaton later ratified that extension; the trial judge had not resolved ratification under the correct rule. Knowlton J. wrote the sole reported opinion. Field C.J. and Holmes, Knowlton, Morton, and Barker JJ. were present; the report supplies no numerical vote or separate writing. (496, 499–501.)

## Facts and procedural history

Bishop lived in Sycamore, Illinois and had business connections with Eaton’s brother Harry. In December 1886, Eaton wrote from Nova Scotia inviting Bishop either to supply Harry with additional money or help him obtain it, and promising to see that the debt was paid. On January 7, 1887, Harry issued a $200 note to Stark, payable in one year; Bishop signed as surety in reliance on Eaton’s letter and looked to Eaton for reimbursement if he had to pay. Shortly afterward Bishop mailed a letter describing the note and amount, properly addressed to Eaton in Nova Scotia with postage prepaid. Eaton testified that he never received it; there was no finding that it reached him. (496–497, 500.)

At maturity, payment was extended for another year. The narrative of the trial findings says Eaton’s knowledge or consent was disputed; the appellate court’s dispositive account states that the bill of exceptions showed an extension without his consent. During an August 1889 interview, Bishop asked Eaton to take up the outstanding note. Eaton answered that Bishop should try to get Harry to pay and that, if Harry did not, Eaton would, assuring Bishop that the matter would not cost him anything. Bishop paid the note on October 1, 1891 and did not attempt collection from Harry afterward. Eaton said he first learned of Bishop’s payment on December 22. (497, 501.)

Bishop brought an action on the guaranty, with a writ dated February 2, 1892. Braley J. tried it without a jury, declined Eaton’s requested rulings, and concluded that Bishop was entitled to judgment as a matter of law on the reported findings. Eaton excepted. His requests disputed the guaranty’s acceptance and notice, argued that Bishop should have pursued Harry and promptly reported default and payment, and asserted that the unconsented extension discharged him. He also maintained that the 1889 conversation was insufficient ratification. (496–498.)

## Nature of the promise and acceptance

The court first classified Eaton’s promise as a guaranty rather than an independent primary undertaking. His letter gave Bishop two routes: lend to Harry directly, or assist Harry to obtain money from someone else. Either way, Harry remained primarily liable for the debt and Eaton guaranteed payment. Bishop’s personal reliance solely on Eaton for eventual reimbursement did not alter that objectively stated allocation. (498–499.)

The letter offered a promise in exchange for an act, not a promise that required a return promise before performance. Bishop’s signing as surety accomplished the invited assistance, supplied consideration, and accepted the offer. Ordinarily an offeror who will promptly learn of the performed act needs no separate notice. Where the act’s nature or the parties’ circumstances make such knowledge unlikely, however, the offeree must give notice with reasonable diligence after performance. Until such seasonable notice, the guarantor would not be bound under the rule the court applied; performance nevertheless protected the promisee against an intervening withdrawal if the required notice followed within a reasonable time. (499–500.)

## Why mailing satisfied the notice requirement

The required notice had to be reasonable in light of the transaction, the parties’ locations, and any prior dealings. Eaton made the offer by letter across the distance between Nova Scotia and Illinois, so he should have contemplated that information about Bishop’s response would also travel by letter. Seasonably depositing a properly addressed, prepaid letter in the mail did everything the transaction reasonably required. Bishop did not assume the risk that the notification failed to arrive. The court expressly reserved how far this reasoning would apply to ordinary contracts formed by correspondence; this decision should not be presented as an unrestricted ruling on every postal acceptance. (500–501.)

Notice that the offered act had been performed was also distinct from notice of Harry’s later default. The guaranty did not require Bishop to exhaust collection efforts against Harry after paying the note. Delayed notice of default was not a defense, at least without evidence that it injured Eaton. The court grounded that conclusion in its established guaranty precedent, rather than treating the formation-notice requirement as a continuing duty to report every event. (501.)

## The independent error and exact result

Although Bishop prevailed on the notice issue featured in the casebook, the court found a separate error requiring a new trial. Extending the principal debtor’s payment time without the guarantor’s consent discharged Eaton, unless he later assented and ratified the extension. The judge should have applied the substance of Eaton’s eighth requested ruling rather than entering judgment for Bishop simply on the reported facts. The appellate court could not determine whether the trial judge would have found ratification had he considered it under the proper rule. It therefore sustained the exceptions; it neither entered final judgment for Eaton nor affirmed Bishop’s judgment. (498, 501.)
