# McFarlane v. Esquire Magazine
74 F.3d 1296 (D.C. Cir. 1996) | Civil Procedure

## Decision and assigned account

Judge Stephen F. Williams wrote the January 30, 1996 opinion for a panel also comprising Judges Ginsburg and Randolph. No separate writing is reported. The court affirmed dismissal of the claim against freelance writer Craig Unger for lack of personal jurisdiction, affirmed refusal to transfer that claim, and affirmed summary judgment for the publisher because the evidence could not support the required finding of actual malice. The assigned account is the transfer parenthetical in Civil Procedure PDF page 230, printed page 417, Note 8. The complete actual opinion includes one footnote. Pinpoints below identify its numbered parts and subparts; the assigned transfer discussion cites reporter page 1301. [Complete judicial reproduction](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-dc-circuit/1344153.html).

## The article, claimed defamation, and distinct defendants

An October 1991 Esquire article described an alleged conspiracy by members of the Reagan–Bush campaign to delay release of American hostages in Iran and undermine President Carter's reelection. Unger wrote the article; Esquire Magazine and Hearst Corporation published it. The article relied heavily on Ari Ben-Menashe, a self-described former Israeli spy, and attributed to him assertions connecting Robert McFarlane with Israeli intelligence and the unnamed American allegedly involved in the Jonathan Pollard espionage affair. The article also disclosed substantial doubts about Ben-Menashe's credibility. Opinion, opening factual discussion.

McFarlane sued both the writer and publisher in federal district court in Washington, D.C., alleging that the passage falsely portrayed him as an Israeli spy and a traitor. As an admitted public figure who had served in senior Reagan Administration roles, he had to establish constitutional actual malice. After discovery, the district court found no personal jurisdiction over Unger and no basis for a reasonable jury to find the necessary malice by Esquire with clear and convincing evidence. Opening discussion; Part II.

The different grounds matter. Lack of jurisdiction over the writer was not an adjudication that his reporting was truthful or constitutionally protected on this record. The publisher's summary judgment concerned its own legally relevant knowledge and conduct, rather than an automatic attribution of everything the freelancer knew. Parts I–II.

## The D.C. statute did not reach Unger's contacts

McFarlane relied on D.C. Code § 13-423(a)(3) and (4), not the separate business-transaction subsection that D.C. precedent interpreted to extend to due process limits. Subsection (3) required both an injury and a predicate act within the District. Unger wrote the article and delivered it to Esquire in New York. Treating the alleged D.C. reputational injury itself as an in-forum act would erase the statute's distinction between an act and its injury. Circuit precedent foreclosed that argument, and the panel could not overrule a prior panel. Part I.

Subsection (4) required an in-District injury plus specified additional contacts: regular business, a persistent course of conduct, or substantial revenue from relevant in-District goods or services. McFarlane did not press the substantial-revenue branch. Unger's writing for national publications circulated in D.C. did not establish regular business or persistent conduct there; the writer's contacts had to be assessed separately from the publisher's distribution. Part I.

Two articles for Washington-based publications also were insufficient. One, for the Washington Post, was written after the complaint and supplied no jurisdictional basis. Even treating both career sales together, the contacts did not establish the statutory regularity or persistence. The court therefore affirmed without relying on the circuit's separate newsgathering exception or deciding the reach of that exception. Part I.

## Expiring limitations did not compel a transfer

McFarlane requested transfer under § 1406(a), which permitted transfer in the interest of justice when venue was wrong. Venue here was at least unchallenged, while personal jurisdiction was absent. Courts disagreed whether that situation called for § 1406(a) or § 1404(a). The panel left the issue unresolved because both routes involved abuse-of-discretion review, both included the interest of justice, and neither party supplied convenience arguments distinguishing the two. Part I, transfer discussion.

New York's defamation limitations period apparently had expired, making dismissal a serious penalty. But Unger's answer had alerted McFarlane to the jurisdictional defense, and counsel had inexplicably failed to file a protective New York action. The panel held that the district court remained within its discretion to deny transfer on those circumstances. Id.; assigned printed 417.

The district court also reasoned that Esquire's insurance left McFarlane unprejudiced. The panel criticized that explanation as oversimplified: a claim against Unger might have supplied an easier route to vindication because he knew substantially more about reasons to question the article. It nevertheless sustained the denial based on notice and the failure to protect the claim. The holding therefore does not establish either that adequate insurance eliminates prejudice or that an expired limitations period automatically requires transfer. Part I.

## The freelancer's mental state was not automatically the publisher's

McFarlane acknowledged that Unger was an independent contractor. The court assumed a possible agency relationship because Unger presented himself to sources as working for Esquire, but distinguished agency from employment. It examined whether constitutional actual malice could be imputed outside respondeat superior. Earlier decisions involving employee writers or state-law agency principles did not squarely settle that constitutional issue. Part II.A.

The panel doubted extending imputation beyond employees because actual malice protected speech through a subjective state-of-mind requirement. In any event, McFarlane showed no evidence that Esquire supervised the process by which Unger converted raw information into the article, as distinct from exercising editorial control over the final product. Accordingly, he had to establish Esquire's malice through its employees' information and conduct. The opinion preserves this independent evidentiary basis rather than treating every conceivable nonemployee arrangement as resolved. Part II.A.

For review, the court assumed in McFarlane's favor that information held by different editors could be aggregated, but did not decide whether aggregation was legally proper. It considered all four asserted grounds together, construing the evidence favorably to him. Part II.B.

## Why the combined evidence did not establish actual malice

First, the editors knew of serious attacks on Ben-Menashe's credibility and published many of them. Such disclosure tended to rebut malice, although it was not a universal shield for defamatory allegations. The editors also relied on his supposed role in Iran–Contra and Unger's favorable prior work. Congressional investigation itself was not a credibility endorsement, and the claimed contradiction in a Frontline report lacked evidence tying the pertinent information to the editors. Part II.B.1.

Second, the court criticized passages suggesting that Elliot Richardson endorsed more of Ben-Menashe's espionage assertions than his affidavit supported. But the record could reflect linguistic confusion rather than reckless disregard. Deleting an ambiguous qualification from a Richardson quotation plausibly reflected space and clarity concerns. A much sharper warning in Unger's interview notes troubled the court, but counsel had not asked editor Mark Warren whether he actually encountered it. Footnote 1 reproduces the original transcript's errors underlying the court's corrected passage. The deletion of another skeptical Richardson quotation also received limited weight in light of the article's substantial disclosures undermining the source. Part II.B.2 & n.1.

Third, sensational headings, highlighting, photographs, and cover language demonstrated a slanted presentation but did not directly establish subjective belief in falsity about McFarlane. Fourth, the change from a reported denial to a refusal to comment was careless: Unger's request for an interview had not disclosed the espionage charge. Still, the evidence did not sufficiently connect Esquire with purposeful misleading, and even a severe departure from professional standards was not necessarily constitutional malice. Parts II.B.3–4.

## Exact judgment and significance

The court affirmed. That disposition sustained the jurisdictional dismissal and transfer denial concerning Unger and the actual-malice summary judgment concerning Esquire. It did not find the accusation true or give McFarlane a merits determination vindicating his reputation. Closing discussion and mandate.

The assigned parenthetical accurately uses the transfer ruling as a qualification to the usual preference for preserving claims through transfer. The complete decision shows why that procedural discretion turned on the plaintiff's notice and protective options, while also explaining the independent statutory-jurisdiction and constitutional grounds that produced the final result. Assigned printed 417; Parts I–II.
