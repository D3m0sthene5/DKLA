# National Nutritional Foods Association v. Kennedy
572 F.2d 377 (2d Cir. 1978) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Court, posture, and result

Friendly wrote a unanimous opinion for the Second Circuit panel of Friendly, Smith, and Meskill on February 16, 1978, in Nos. 77-4097 and 77-4104. Manufacturers and trade associations petitioned for review, as did Dr. Miles Robinson pro se. They challenged FDA's reissued vitamin-and-mineral regulations after this court's 1974 remand and Congress's 1976 amendment of the governing statute. FDA could not bypass notice and comment merely because it characterized the revisions as compliance with Congress. The court vacated the regulations and remanded to the Commissioner, while resolving several additional objections to guide further proceedings. No judge wrote separately. (379–386, 393.)

## What happened after the 1974 remand

The agency's supplement-regulation effort began in 1962, eventually generating twenty-two months of hearings and 32,405 transcript pages. In 1974 the Second Circuit broadly sustained FDA's identity standards but stayed enforcement and required particular corrections: meaningful applications for dosage and combination exceptions; relief for essential nutrients without established recommended allowances; invalidation of automatic drug classification above allowance limits; properly qualified iron labeling; exemption of natural fresh produce; and reopened cross-examination of the critical allowances witness. That earlier judgment was the starting point, not the judgment under review here. (379–380 & nn. 1–4.)

In May 1975 FDA announced opportunities to apply for additional formulations, proposed amendments, and a reopened hearing. It would allow higher-dose single nutrients without special applications. Because Dr. Sebrell was no longer a Food and Nutrition Board member, FDA tendered his successor as chair, Dr. Alfred Harper. Participants could explore scientific methodology, appropriate regulatory uses of the allowances, and possible bias. The November 1975 hearing lasted six days and added 1,119 transcript pages. The ALJ found no established bias affecting judgment, considered the allowances a sound regulatory basis, and concluded that the detailed questioning did not require different rules. Parties filed exceptions. (380–381.)

Congress then changed the legal premise. Public Law 94-278, signed April 22, 1976, added § 411. For covered special-dietary foods it generally barred maximum-potency limits under the identified provisions, drug classification based solely on allegedly irrational or useless potency, and limits on ingredient combinations or numbers. Exceptions preserved authority for products represented for disease treatment or management, children under twelve, and pregnant or lactating women. Labeling authority was less severely restricted; ingredients could not be wholly suppressed merely because not vitamins or minerals, but prominence and presentation remained regulated. Section 501(b) expressly directed that amendments implementing the new § 411 be promulgated under APA § 553. Note 5 reproduces the complete new statutory scheme, including its definitions. (381–382 & n. 5.)

Without further participation concerning that legislation, FDA issued final rules on October 19, 1976. It largely readopted the earlier scheme with revised organization, corrections, and an exemption from restrictions Congress had forbidden. It thought almost all formulation applications moot, allowed one new infant-and-child combination, rejected a higher B6 preparation for pregnant and lactating women, and treated estrogen-containing multinutrient products for oral-contraceptive users as drugs. It also added a food-additive provision and rejected exceptions to Harper's examination and the proposed amendments. (382–383 & n. 6.)

The manufacturers' November 30 urgent petition sought withdrawal and a new proposed order allowing participation. FDA answered only in April 1977, invoking good cause to dispense with participation because Congress had already determined the changes. It also rejected renewed trial-type hearings. Its rules were subsequently postponed until July 1, 1979. (382–385 & nn. 7–8.)

## A substantial statutory change required public participation

Section 501(b)'s reference to amending promulgated regulations could not reasonably exclude these rules because the earlier court had stayed them pending remand. Congress plainly addressed the controversial nutrition program. FDA itself did not press that literal escape; it argued instead that § 553's exceptions came along with Congress's reference to that section. The court accepted that abstract proposition but rejected application of the exceptions here. (383–384 & nn. 9–11.)

The original final rule contained no proper good-cause finding and explanation. A statement that FDA had revised rules in recognition of Congress described what it did, not why participation should be omitted. The court considered belated justification suspect but expressly left open whether an agency could ever supply such a finding on reconsideration. Even if timely included, these reasons would fail substantively. (384–385 & nn. 12–14.)

The legislative history made impracticability, lack of necessity, and public interest narrow exceptions. Fourteen years of proceedings followed by the agency's own extended stay hardly established an emergency or unavoidable interference with agency functions. Nor was this a minor technical adjustment of no public interest. Congress removed old powers, imposed new labeling duties, and left FDA choices about whether and how to regulate the remaining protected groups. A record supporting an all-consumer identity scheme might not support narrower rules for disease-related uses, children, and pregnant or lactating women. The urgent petition made public interest in those choices unmistakable. FDA's task involved a new regulatory judgment, not simply cutting forbidden provisions from a completed rule. (384–386 & nn. 14–16.)

Notice and comment was required, but renewed trial-type proceedings were not. Congress specifically chose § 553 rather than the formal procedure that had produced this lengthy record. The agency did not have to reopen objections previously available but unraised, rejected without appeal, or resolved in its favor unless the new legislation gave them a significantly new aspect. The court left many additional objections for the agency to address in the new proceeding. (386.)

## Recommended allowances and surviving minimum requirements

Robinson's renewed objections to Harper's alleged bias, evidentiary rulings, and general allowance methodology were meritless insofar as the issue remained the same as before. FDA need not repeat that inquiry. However, notice and comment had to address whether allowances adequate for the earlier broad program were suitable for the three categories still subject to maximum and combination restrictions. The court did not equate rejecting the old methodological objections with approving every new statutory application. (386–387 & n. 17.)

Minimum potency limits survived. Congress's conference report expressly preserved them to prevent uselessly small ingredients. Petitioners argued FDA had changed from a consumer-confusion rationale to a significance rationale without new evidence. The earlier findings already addressed nutritionally insignificant amounts and the impractical number of units needed to deliver useful amounts. Distinguishing nutrient amounts in ordinary meal components from amounts in a product sold specifically as a supplement was reasonable. The court adhered to its earlier approval of minima rather than requiring a new factual justification for that unchanged rationale. (387–388.)

## Specific compliance problems and objections

FDA had not adequately justified continued exclusion from standardized combinations of essential nutrients lacking recommended allowances. Permission to include them in products Congress newly exempted did not answer the court's prior instruction. The agency repeated the old claim that diets were not deficient in them, rather than providing a new sufficient basis. It could produce additional evidence in the changed statutory setting; the court did not insist on mechanically applying its old directions despite a genuinely supported new justification. (388 & n. 18.)

The variance standard also needed correction. FDA could decline to recite every factor from the prior opinion, but it could not demand proof that an exception improved honesty and fair dealing beyond the existing rule. A proponent met the relevant burden by showing that the proposed change achieved the agency's objectives to the same extent, including as knowledge advanced. (388–389.)

The iron proviso failed to capture the prior ruling. Saying it was often impractical to supply iron needs from conventional food could imply that an obtainable balanced diet otherwise met those needs. The evidence had shown difficulty even with such a diet for infants, children, and women of childbearing age. Absent adequate contrary evidence, FDA had to implement the actual qualification directed. In contrast, the court found no basis for similar relief concerning B6, folic acid, and magnesium after examining the newly supplied testimony. A recognized difference involving vitamin K also did not require qualification of the general natural-versus-synthetic rule for listed nutrients; sellers could seek warranted individual relief. (389.)

## Food-additive authority remained available for safety

Petitioners accused FDA of recreating the forbidden potency limits by classifying unsafe supplements as food additives. The new provision applied when qualified experts did not generally recognize safety under intended conditions, triggering the Act's food-additive approval rules. Petitioners conceded authority to prevent dangerously potent products but urged FDA to use ordinary adulteration proceedings rather than the more readily enforced additive regime. (389–391 & nn. 19–21.)

The court rejected the supposed categorical incompatibility between food and food additive. A substance qualifying as food could still become a component of another food and lack the required general recognition of safety. Statutory language, public-health purposes, and general rulemaking authority supported that treatment. More importantly, the 1976 legislative materials preserved additive regulation for potentially toxic ingredients while rejecting its use simply to enforce disfavored nutritional-potency or combination policies. The court found no infirmity in § 105.85(f); it did not determine that every high-dose supplement was unsafe or automatically an additive. Note 19 assumed removal of cross-references to separately invalidated regulations and declined an untimely collateral attack on other underlying rules. Note 21 also explained that the prior remand allowed consistent additional changes with participation, which these petitioners had received for the new additive provision. (391–392 & nn. 19–22.)

## Closing disposition and assigned scope

The objection to guaranteeing labeled potency on an expiration date without a tolerance allowance came too late: the provision existed during the prior review and could have been challenged then. FDA remained free to consider it voluntarily. Despite resolving several objections in the agency's favor, the court's express final judgment vacated the regulations and remanded for further proceedings consistent with its opinion. (392–393.)
