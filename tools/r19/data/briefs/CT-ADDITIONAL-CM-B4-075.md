# Astra USA, Inc. v. Santa Clara County
563 U.S. 110 (2011) | Contracts

## Decision and litigation posture
On March 29, 2011, the Supreme Court held that covered healthcare entities could not use third-party-beneficiary suits on federal pharmaceutical pricing agreements to enforce the same ceiling-price obligations for which Congress had supplied no private statutory action. Justice Ginsburg wrote for all eight participating Justices; Justice Kagan took no part. The Court reversed the Ninth Circuit's judgment allowing Santa Clara County's contract action. It did not determine whether the manufacturers had actually overcharged the County or award compensation. The case concerned the availability of this enforcement route on a dismissed complaint. (112–114, 116–117, 122.)

## The two interdependent programs and the contract
Congress's 1990 Medicaid Drug Rebate Program required a manufacturer seeking Medicaid payment for covered drugs to enter a standardized agreement with HHS. The manufacturer undertook to rebate part of participating States' drug purchases. The rebate calculation used statutory and regulatory definitions of average and best prices. Manufacturers supplied detailed sales and pricing information quarterly; HHS ordinarily could not disclose it in a manner revealing a manufacturer's identity or particular prices. (114–115 & n. 1.)

The 340B program, added in 1992, required Medicaid-participating manufacturers to offer discounted drugs to specified healthcare facilities, including public hospitals and community health centers serving the poor. Manufacturers entered nationwide form Pharmaceutical Pricing Agreements, or PPAs, with HHS. These were the mechanism for opting into the statutory scheme, with ceiling prices derived from the larger Medicaid program's pricing methodology. They were not individually negotiated transactional bargains. Note 2 identifies a substitute statutory calculation for covered over-the-counter medications that lacked Medicaid rebates. (113, 115 & n. 2.)

HRSA, within HHS, administered the program. It could require reimbursement for overcharges or terminate the PPA, which also terminated the manufacturer's eligibility for Medicaid coverage. At the time of this decision, overcharge complaints were handled informally. The 2010 healthcare legislation directed development of formal dispute procedures, compensation and penalty mechanisms, and administrative resolutions subject to APA judicial review; the new procedures were not yet in place. These temporal statements describe the scheme as it stood in 2011, not its present implementation. (115–116, 121–122 & n. 8.)

## County's claim and lower courts
Santa Clara County operated several covered facilities and sued Astra and eight other pharmaceutical companies, alleging overcharges contrary to their PPAs. It styled the suit as a class action for California covered entities and the counties funding them and sought compensatory damages. The asserted right was contractual: those entities and counties allegedly were intended beneficiaries of the manufacturers' promises to HHS. The District Court dismissed because the PPAs conferred no enforceable beneficiary rights. The Ninth Circuit reversed, accepting a contract action despite the absence of a statutory right to sue. Its judgment was reported at 588 F.3d 1237. (116–117.)

The County conceded that section 340B itself provided covered entities no private action. It nevertheless argued that the PPAs named those entities as recipients of discounted drugs and existed to secure those benefits. Under its federal common-law beneficiary theory, a government's use of a contract to obtain a benefit gave the intended recipient an enforceable right. The Court distinguished intent to confer a benefit from intent to let the beneficiary enforce it, especially where the promisee is governmental. (117–118 & n. 3.)

## Why the contractual label could not create the remedy
Congress placed oversight in HHS without assigning covered entities an auxiliary enforcement role. Recognition of a statutory private action depended on congressional intent to provide a remedy. The PPAs contained no negotiable terms: they incorporated the statute's requirements and recorded manufacturers' agreement to comply. A suit to enforce their ceiling-price provisions was therefore substantively a suit to enforce the statute. Permitting precisely the same obligation to be sued upon merely by changing the claim's contractual label would defeat the conceded absence of the statutory action. (113–114, 117–119.)

The complaint itself made the identity of the obligations decisive. It alleged violation of section 340B's ceiling price, repeatedly identified that statute as the source of the contractual term, and did not plead breach of an independent substantive obligation arising only from a PPA. The Court accordingly did not announce that every third-party suit on every federal contract is barred. Note 4 expressly reserved whether a contracting agency could authorize beneficiary enforcement, while explaining that no authorization could be inferred from a form contract that merely restated required terms and otherwise manifested no such intent. (118–119 & n. 4.)

## Central administration, conflicting interests, and information limits
The Ninth Circuit believed private suits would distribute the enforcement burden and advance the discount program's goals. The Court accepted the United States' contrary explanation: Congress had centralized enforcement of two interdependent programs whose interests could conflict. A lower average price generally helped a 340B buyer, while a higher average price generally increased a State Medicaid agency's rebate. HHS could evaluate and balance those interests; dispersed courts deciding individual covered entities' suits risked incompatible nationwide pricing determinations. (119–120 & n. 6.)

Congress's pricing-confidentiality restrictions further undercut an inferred private remedy. Potential beneficiary plaintiffs lacked access to information needed to establish the alleged violations. Note 7 reports HHS's interpretation of that restriction, while note 8 acknowledges that the 2010 law would provide some information access in conjunction with the new administrative process. The Court did not treat confidentiality as an absolute, timeless rule. (115, 121 & nn. 7–8.)

Finally, Inspector General reports finding inadequate oversight did not justify judicial creation of a substitute enforcement system. Congress had responded by strengthening HRSA's authority, requiring audits and formal adjudication, and making agency resolutions binding subject to APA review. Those enacted remedies, rather than lawsuits across multiple district courts, were the chosen response to enforcement deficiencies. (121–122.)

## Limits, disposition, and assigned account
Note 5 reserved the distinct question of States' fraud claims based on false best-price reporting under the Medicaid program; the United States' position in that separate litigation did not establish the County's beneficiary remedy here. Note 3 described disagreement among courts about beneficiary suits on government contracts but did not resolve every formulation of that broader doctrine. (117 n. 3; 120 n. 5.)

The final judgment simply reversed the Ninth Circuit. There was no remand instruction printed in the dispositive sentence and no quantified damages or costs award. The absence of a viable beneficiary enforcement route disposed of the case without finding the overcharge allegations true or false. (122.)

The assigned account at printed 1178 correctly identifies the attempted contract route around the absence of a statutory action. Its distinction for obligations independent of statutory requirements reflects the Court's emphasis on what this complaint actually alleged. It should not be elevated into an affirmative holding that any asserted independent term will necessarily be enforceable: beneficiary intent and any applicable limits would still need to be established.
