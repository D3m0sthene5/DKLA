# Chamber of Commerce v. SEC
412 F.3d 133 (D.C. Cir. 2005) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Court, questions, and result

On June 21, 2005, Chief Judge Douglas Ginsburg wrote a unanimous D.C. Circuit opinion with Rogers and Tatel in No. 04-1300. The Chamber challenged conditions requiring mutual funds using specified statutory exemptions to have at least seventy-five percent independent directors and an independent board chairman. The court upheld SEC's authority and substantive preventive rationales, but found inadequate consideration of costs and a serious disclosure alternative. It granted review in part and remanded the two conditions for further consideration without vacating them. There were no separate judicial writings. (136–140, 143–145.)

## Fund structure, exemptions, and the new conditions

Mutual-fund shareholders elect the investment company's board, which usually delegates management to a separate adviser. That adviser can have interests conflicting with shareholders' returns. The Investment Company Act addresses those conflicts partly by prohibiting transactions benefiting advisers at funds' expense. Longstanding SEC exemptive rules permit some otherwise prohibited transactions if specified safeguards are satisfied. (136–137.)

In 2001 SEC had required a majority of independent directors as a condition of ten exemptions. Reports of late trading, market timing, and misuse of nonpublic portfolio information later convinced it that management controls had broken down. SEC proposed five new or revised conditions in early 2004. After comment and a public meeting, it unanimously approved three, but approved the seventy-five-percent independence and independent-chairman conditions by a three-to-two agency vote. The agency majority sought stronger independent control of board agendas and improved oversight of advisers. The dissenting Commissioners questioned costs, justification, and alternatives. Their agency dissent was part of the rulemaking record; it was not a dissent from this unanimous judicial judgment. (137–138.)

The Chamber claimed interests both as an investor and as an association whose members included advisers. The court found standing on the investor theory: it wanted to invest in funds undertaking exemptive transactions without the challenged governance restrictions. Loss of the opportunity to purchase that desired product was a concrete injury even without proof such funds would perform better. A favorable ruling would redress that restriction. The court therefore did not need an additional associational-standing basis. (138.)

## Statutory authority extended to these exemption conditions

The Chamber argued that corporate governance belonged to state law and that Congress's requirement of forty percent independent directors foreclosed SEC's higher percentage. Section 6(c), reproduced in the first asterisk note, gave SEC broad conditional exemption authority subject to the public interest, investor protection, and the Act's purposes. Unlike the Securities Exchange Act provisions involved in Business Roundtable and Teicher, this statute itself addressed internal fund management and used independent directors to check advisers' conflicts. The conditions operated within that domain, rather than leveraging one licensing regime to regulate another. (138–140 & first asterisk note.)

The statutory forty-percent figure was a minimum, expressed as no more than sixty percent inside directors. It did not prohibit a higher independent percentage, and the conditions applied only to funds electing to undertake exemptive transactions. The court thus rejected both statutory-authority objections. This was authority to condition the specified exemptions, not an unqualified holding that SEC may regulate every aspect of all corporate governance. (140.)

## Preventive rationales satisfied the APA

The Chamber emphasized that the documented recent abuses had not involved transactions covered by the exemptive rules and that SEC already tightened the rules in 2001. The court accepted SEC's explanation that those abuses exposed broader governance weaknesses and justified preventive safeguards before conflicts also damaged exemptive transactions. Prior controls did not establish their continuing adequacy in light of new evidence. (140–141.)

Raising independence to seventy-five percent reasonably strengthened outside directors' control over the agenda and management dealings. An independent chairman could help set that agenda, check the adviser, negotiate contracts, and focus leadership on investors' long-term interests. SEC had separately required a chief compliance officer to supply information; these governance conditions aimed to make independent directors better able to use it. The court had no basis to substitute its policy judgment. The second asterisk note rejected an argument that Congress's later demand for justification necessarily proved the APA explanation inadequate: Congress could demand more detail than judicial review required. (141–142 & second asterisk note.)

## Empirical uncertainty did not defeat every agency judgment

The Act required SEC to consider whether public-interest rulemaking promoted efficiency, competition, and capital formation. The Chamber faulted SEC both for not commissioning its own fund-performance study and for discounting a Fidelity-sponsored study. Neither objection succeeded. Agencies cannot base every policy judgment on conclusive empirical evidence; expertise, comments, limited data, and informed conjecture may support predictive decisions when the agency identifies its considerations. Independent leadership might provide benefits beyond measured returns. (142–143.)

SEC also supplied adequate reasons for finding the Fidelity study unpersuasive. It did not control for other important differences potentially affecting performance and used an unreliable expense calculation; other reviewers interpreted its data differently. The chairman's dismissive comments about empirical studies were not the agency's actual stated basis. Although a fuller discussion could have helped, the explanation was adequate. The court did not require a definitive study proving independent chairmen always improve fund returns. (142–143.)

## The two conditions' costs still required meaningful estimation

SEC did not adequately estimate the economic implications of seventy-five-percent independence. It described three possible compliance methods but claimed it lacked reliable knowledge of which methods funds would choose. That uncertainty could limit it to a cost range, but did not excuse doing the best analysis possible under the statutory mandate. (143.)

For independent chairmen, SEC acknowledged possible additional staffing costs but stopped because it could not reliably estimate them. Even if it could not aggregate costs across an industry with unknown hiring choices, it could estimate costs for an individual fund. Counsel's appeal to boards' preexisting hiring authority both referred to a different condition and failed logically to answer whether this condition would cause new expense. Appellate rationalization could not supply the missing agency analysis. SEC therefore violated § 80a-2(c), and consequently the APA, by inadequately considering both conditions' costs. Uncertainty narrowed the feasible analysis; it did not erase the duty to perform it. (143–144.)

## Disclosure was a serious alternative requiring consideration

The dissenting Commissioners proposed prominently disclosing whether a chairman was inside or independent so investors could choose. Commenters also urged leaving selection to market forces. Disclosure was a familiar, significant option, not a remote conceivable alternative that an agency could reasonably ignore. (144.)

Counsel argued Congress rejected a purely disclosure-based regulatory regime. The court agreed the Act did more than require disclosure but rejected the inference that disclosure was unsuitable for every particular issue. The Act required extensive disclosure elsewhere, and SEC had recently expanded those obligations. The agency could ultimately conclude disclosure inadequately protected shareholders, but it had to consider the alternative or give a reason for declining it. Neither counsel nor the court could make that expert choice for SEC. (144–145.)

## Remedy and assigned scope

The final judgment granted the petition in part and remanded to SEC to address defects concerning the two challenged conditions. It did not order vacatur, reject exemption authority, mandate the disclosure alternative, or decide the outcome of the corrected economic analysis. (145.)
