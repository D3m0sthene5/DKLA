# Wilkins v. United States

**598 U.S. 152 (2023) • Supreme Court of the United States • March 28, 2023**

**Assigned source:** Civil Procedure Supplement, printed p. 506 (PDF 342). Justice Sotomayor wrote for six Justices, joined by Kagan, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, Barrett, and Jackson. Justice Thomas dissented, joined by Chief Justice Roberts and Justice Alito. The decision was 6–3.

## Facts and procedural history

Larry Steven Wilkins and Jane Stanton owned property along Robbins Gulch Road in rural Montana, acquired in 1991 and 2004. Their predecessors had granted the United States a road easement in 1962. The Government interpreted that easement to permit public access. The owners disputed that interpretation and asserted obligations to maintain and patrol the road. They alleged trespass, theft, and other intrusions associated with public use. These allegations describe their grievance; the Supreme Court did not adjudicate the easement's proper scope. 155.

In 2018 they sued under the Quiet Title Act. The Government sought dismissal under 28 U.S.C. § 2409a(g), which bars covered civil actions, except actions by a State, unless commenced within twelve years after accrual. The statute identifies accrual with actual or constructive knowledge by the plaintiff or predecessor of the United States' claim. The district court treated the time bar as jurisdictional and dismissed for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. The Ninth Circuit affirmed under circuit precedent, reading Block v. North Dakota as having established the jurisdictional characterization. The Supreme Court granted certiorari to resolve a circuit split. 156 & n.2.

## Issue and holding

Is the Quiet Title Act's twelve-year time bar a restriction on subject-matter jurisdiction or a nonjurisdictional claims-processing rule?

**Holding:** Section 2409a(g) is a **nonjurisdictional claims-processing rule**. 165. Its text and context contain no jurisdictional clear statement, and earlier Supreme Court decisions had not definitively settled the contrary characterization. The Court did not decide the parties' disagreement about the precise consequences of this characterization on remand. 156 n.1. Nor did it hold that the suit was timely, establish an entitlement to equitable tolling, or resolve the land dispute.

## Reasoning

**1. The jurisdictional label changes litigation.** Subject-matter jurisdiction concerns the classes of cases a court may entertain. Claims-processing requirements generally promote orderly litigation by prescribing procedural steps and timing. A jurisdictional objection can arise late or sua sponte and ordinarily resists forfeiture and related equitable doctrines, potentially wasting years of litigation. The Court therefore treats a procedural requirement as jurisdictional only when Congress clearly says so, using ordinary statutory interpretation rather than requiring particular magic words. 156–159.

Section 2409a(g) speaks to timeliness: an action is barred unless commenced within twelve years. Such language is ordinary limitations language, not an express restriction on adjudicatory power. Congress separately supplied the jurisdictional grant in § 1346(f), which gives district courts exclusive original jurisdiction of the covered quiet-title actions. That grant does not condition jurisdiction on compliance with § 2409a(g)'s limitations period. The separation and absence of a linking condition support the nonjurisdictional reading. 158–159 & n.4.

**2. Sovereign immunity does not make every condition jurisdictional.** The dissent argued that the period conditions the United States' waiver of immunity and should therefore be jurisdictional. The majority rejected an automatic inference from waiver condition to subject-matter jurisdiction. Irwin and later decisions permitted analysis of equitable exceptions in suits against the Government; strict construction of a waiver did not itself answer every procedural question. The clear-statement framework still applied. 158 n.3, 161–162.

**3. Earlier uses of “jurisdiction” were not definitive holdings on this issue.** Stare decisis protects a definitive prior jurisdictional interpretation, but courts have often used the word loosely. The question is whether the earlier decision actually addressed the technical distinction and whether its result turned on that characterization. 159–160.

In **Block**, the Court decided that the Act supplied the exclusive procedure for challenging federal title and that its then-existing time bar applied to States. Its closing statement about absence of jurisdiction if the claim was barred did not analyze the technical classification. The majority treated that statement as a passing jurisdictional characterization without precedential force on the question now presented. 160–162.

In **Mottaz**, the Court selected the applicable statute based on the nature of the claim and then determined that the plaintiff had notice more than twelve years before suit. Neither inquiry depended on whether the period was jurisdictional. The Government's apparently late reliance on the Quiet Title Act did not establish an unexpressed holding about forfeiture. Footnote 5 lists possible nonjurisdictional explanations; it does not announce which explanation the earlier Court actually adopted. 162–164 & n.5.

In **Beggerly**, the Court examined the statute's accrual rule, generous period, and interest in settled land rights before rejecting additional equitable tolling. That analysis would have been unnecessary had earlier cases definitively made the period jurisdictional. Beggerly's rejection of tolling therefore does not conflict with Wilkins' nonjurisdictional holding. Other possible equitable exceptions were not comprehensively decided in Beggerly. 164 & n.6.

Because none of these decisions supplied a definitive interpretation, Congress' later amendments did not establish acquiescence in one. General lower-court references and Dalm's discussion of a different provision could not fill that gap. 165 & nn.7–8.

## Thomas dissent

Thomas argued that the Government's immunity and the expressly limited terms of its consent required jurisdictional treatment. The statute provided several special protections for the United States, including the time limit, and did not generally place it in the same position as a private defendant. Irwin should not displace the established immunity rule in this context. 166–170 & n.1.

Alternatively, Block and Mottaz had already definitively interpreted the time bar as jurisdictional. Their discussions of sovereign immunity and remand instructions gave the characterization substantive force, rather than making it a casual remark. Mottaz's treatment of the Government's late argument supported that conclusion. Beggerly rejected tolling and was, at most, ambiguous about jurisdiction, insufficient to overcome the earlier precedents. The dissent read precedent in its historical context and would preserve the bar's jurisdictional status. 170–174 & nn.2–3.

## Exact disposition and course significance

The Court **reversed the Ninth Circuit's judgment and remanded** for further proceedings consistent with its opinion. 165. It corrected the jurisdictional classification; it did not award the owners title or conclusively defeat the limitations defense.

The assigned passage illustrates the modern clear-statement approach and its application even where sovereign immunity is involved. Keep three questions separate: whether a limitations rule affects jurisdiction, whether the litigant satisfied it, and whether a particular exception is available. A nonjurisdictional classification does not automatically supply equitable tolling.
