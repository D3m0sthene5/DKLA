# Fuller v. De Paul University
293 Ill. App. 261, 12 N.E.2d 213 (Ill. App. Ct. 1938) | Contracts

## Holding and litigation posture
The Illinois Appellate Court affirmed judgment for De Paul University notwithstanding a jury's $2,000 verdict for its prospective teacher. On the undisputed evidence, the applicant intentionally concealed background facts material to employment at this Catholic institution; the concealment vitiated the oral employment contract. Justice McSurely wrote the January 10, 1938 opinion, with Presiding Justice O'Connor and Justice Matchett concurring. The original report's final concurrence line supplies Matchett's name; the native reproduction incorrectly reads Hatchett. (262, 266–269.)

## Employment and discovery
The university was a nonprofit Catholic institution whose curriculum combined academic teaching with religious exercises and character formation. Of roughly 5,700 students in 1935–1936, approximately 85 percent were Catholic, including about 1,000 nuns and 50–70 priests. Its dean testified that faculty qualifications included scholastic attainment, character, and suitability for association with faculty and students. These institutional circumstances explain the court's materiality analysis; they do not show that every undisclosed event in every teacher's past defeats a contract. (263.)

In August 1935 Fuller sought a German teaching position. Calling himself John B. Fuller, he told the dean and president that he had taught at Amherst for eight years, held a doctorate, and was Catholic. He knew the university's religious character. It orally hired him for September 1935 through June 1936 at $2,000, later sending a catalogue, class schedule, and notice of the faculty meeting and start of classes. Before those events, its president learned that Fuller had formerly been Father Bernard Fuller, a priest associated with the Catholic community at Techny. Investigation revealed his earlier religious life and departure from the priesthood. A September 10 letter informed him that his services would not be needed. (264.)

The evidence showed that he had joined the Society of the Divine Word in 1906, been ordained in 1910, and taken vows of obedience, poverty, and chastity. He had lived and worked as a priest at Techny through 1927. Without informing his superiors, he obtained an Amherst position, subsequently left the priesthood, and married. A message from Greece announcing his death had led to a requiem mass, although he was alive; the opinion does not determine who sent that message. Fuller did not disclose this history when applying to De Paul, including when asked about references. The court used the historical religious terminology of an apostate priest and a fugitive from religious discipline; it did not find that he was a fugitive from criminal prosecution. (264–265.)

The president testified that discovery of this history after hiring would seriously damage the institution and cause its priests and nuns to withdraw. Fuller responded that it need not become known. His September 12 letter proposed that he teach while both parties kept his past hidden, and his testimony indicated that he would not have expected to stay if the facts became public. The president rejected that proposal. The evidence that full disclosure would have prevented the hiring was uncontradicted. Fuller also testified to unsuccessful efforts to find other employment. (265–266.)

## Trial and arguments
Fuller sued for damages for breach of the oral agreement. At the close of evidence the university asked for a directed verdict. The judge reserved ruling, allowed the jury to return a $2,000 verdict for Fuller, and then entered judgment for the university notwithstanding that verdict. On appeal Fuller argued that the materiality of his silence belonged to the jury. He also suggested that the concealment was unintentional or concerned immaterial facts. This was a decision after presentation of evidence, not a pleading dismissal or summary judgment on disputed allegations. (262, 266–268.)

## Governing reasoning
The court reasoned that when evidence, facts, and permissible inferences are uncontroverted, the legal effect may be decided by the judge. Concealment must concern something material to the transaction. Here the university would not have contracted had it known the omitted history, and the court found that the silence deceived it into hiring Fuller. The opinion also quoted an employment treatise distinguishing mere prior wrongdoing from concealment of circumstances whose public discovery would injure the employer's business. Its conclusion depended on the undisputed institutional context and inducement, rather than a general rule that any past misconduct supplies a ground for discharge. (266–267.)

The court rejected innocent silence. As a former active priest familiar with the institution, Fuller must have understood the importance of the omitted history. It treated his presentation of himself as a Catholic layman as purposeful deception, supported by his later proposal to keep the facts concealed and his acknowledgment of the consequences of disclosure. Its discussion of teaching character and the university's religious atmosphere supplied the court's historical explanation of materiality. The opinion favorably quoted the university's acknowledgment that leaving the priesthood and marrying concerned Fuller's civil rights and conscience, while maintaining that deliberate concealment impaired suitability for this particular position. It did not adjudicate an independent religious offense or impose a penalty for marriage. (267–269.)

## Judgment and assigned account
Because the affirmative defense was conclusively established by uncontradicted evidence, the court held that the trial judge could properly enter only judgment for the university. It affirmed that judgment, leaving Fuller without the jury's proposed $2,000 recovery. There was no remand, award to the university, or separate writing. (269.)

C&M's Career Changes note asks readers to compare this applicant's nondisclosure with other silence cases. The actual opinion adds the decisive post-evidence posture, uncontradicted institutional testimony, and subsequent concealment proposal. The assigned short account's reference to discharge should not obscure that the university canceled the employment before the scheduled start of classes. (264; C&M PDF 205/printed 512.)
