# Nick v. Morgan's Foods, Inc.
270 F.3d 590 (8th Cir. 2001) | Civil Procedure | Sanctions for failing to participate in court-ordered mediation

## A settlement process without an informed decision-maker

Gee Gee Nick sued Morgan's Foods in June 1998, alleging Title VII sexual harassment and retaliation. Robert Seibel served as the company's outside counsel; in-house counsel Barton Craig made its business decisions. At a May 1999 pretrial conference, the parties consented to mediation with a court-appointed mediator. District Judge Rodney Sippel's August 2 referral order required compliance with the local ADR rules, a memorandum describing disputed facts and positions on liability and damages at least seven days before the first conference, and attendance and good-faith participation by counsel and representatives with settlement authority. Noncompliance could result in sanctions. 270 F.3d at 592-593.

At the company's request, the conference was postponed to October 18. Morgan's Foods did not supply its required memorandum. It sent Seibel and a corporate representative who had no independent knowledge of the case and authority to settle only for $500. Any greater offer required a telephone call to Craig, who stayed away on Seibel's advice. Nick made two offers, both rejected without a counteroffer. Mediation ended without settlement. These undisputed events supported the sanctions dispute; the appellate court did not determine whether Nick's employment allegations were true. Id. at 593.

The mediator reported the company's minimal participation. In response to the court's show-cause order, Morgan's Foods described the referral requirements as nonbinding guidelines and said compliance would waste time and money. Nick also moved for mediation expenses and attorneys' fees. After a hearing, the district court found a lack of good-faith participation. Id. at 593-594.

## The awards and the limited appeal

The district court divided $2,781.25 in mediation and attorneys' fees between the company and Seibel: $1,390.63 against Morgan's Foods and $1,390.62 against counsel. Each also owed Nick $30 for attending. Separately, the company owed a **$1,500 fine to the clerk** for failing to prepare the memorandum and sending a representative unable to participate meaningfully. These amounts served different purposes and should not be collapsed into a single undifferentiated award. Id. at 594.

Morgan's Foods moved for reconsideration. The court denied that motion and imposed an additional **$1,250 fine each on the company and counsel**, payable to the clerk, for vexatiously increasing litigation costs through a frivolous motion. On appeal, the company challenged its own clerk-payable fines; it did not challenge the sums payable to Nick and her counsel. Seibel was neither an appellant nor counsel on appeal. Craig represented the company. Appellate jurisdiction rested on 28 U.S.C. § 1291 and the collateral-order doctrine. Id. at 592-594.

The company advanced three arguments: Rule 16(f) and inherent authority did not authorize the sanctions; Rule 16 did not permit fines payable to the court; and all misconduct was outside counsel's responsibility, unknown to the company. Judge McMillian addressed those arguments under abuse-of-discretion review. The purpose of sanctioning authority includes controlling litigation and preserving the integrity of court proceedings. Id. at 592, 594-595.

## Rule 16 and local rules supplied authority

Rule 16 authorized pretrial conferences to improve preparation and facilitate settlement. The local rules allowed referral of suitable civil cases to mediation, and the referral order incorporated those procedures while adding specific requirements. Then-Rule 16(f) authorized just orders when a party or attorney disobeyed a scheduling or pretrial order, failed to appear, was substantially unprepared, or failed to participate in good faith. It also permitted reasonable expenses caused by noncompliance, absent substantial justification. The local rule directed the mediator to report failures and allowed appropriate sanctions. Id. at 595.

The court consequently held that Rule 16(f) and the local rules authorized sanctions for violation of this referral order. Footnote 2 explains that it did not need to rely on inherent power. It nevertheless noted that procedural rule authority and inherent authority were not mutually exclusive. The holding therefore rests on the rule and local requirements; it should not be recast as an independent determination that inherent power alone was necessary or sufficient here. Id. at 594-595 & n.2.

The company also tried to distinguish Rule 11's express monetary fines from Rule 16. The court rejected the contention that Rule 16(f) allowed only compensatory expenses. It read the provision as permitting other appropriate sanctions in addition to or instead of expenses and upheld the fines associated with noncompliance and the frivolous reconsideration motion. Id. at 595-596.

## Reliance on counsel did not excuse the company

Morgan's Foods said Seibel had not explained the memorandum requirement and had advised that Craig's attendance was unnecessary. Craig had been advised of the referral order and local rules but had not read them. The company argued that those circumstances made sanctions against the client an abuse of discretion. The court applied circuit law permitting a party to be held responsible for its chosen lawyer's conduct and noted that genuinely victimized clients could seek malpractice remedies. Rule 16(f) expressly allowed sanctions against the party, lawyer, or both. Id. at 596-597.

The record also complicated the suggestion of complete corporate ignorance. Footnote 3 recounts Craig's assertion that he was closely aware of the facts, discovery, and legal issues and had extensive communications with Seibel before mediation. More fundamentally, the company sent a participant who could not independently reconsider its position in response to the discussion. Craig's availability by telephone did not replace participation in the mediation: a summary relayed by counsel could not have the same effect on the decision-maker as hearing the exchange directly. Id. at 596-597 & n.3.

Footnote 4 clarifies that the issue was **meaningful participation**, rather than the attending manager's rank within the corporation. A high-ranking representative could still lack relevant knowledge and practical authority. The court's reasoning does not establish that an officer of a particular title must always attend, or that good faith requires accepting the opponent's demand. The failures were preparation, compliance with the specific order, and the ability to reconsider the settlement position during the process. Id. at 596 & n.4, 597.

## Proportionate sanctions and the judgment

The court considered proportionality. It described the sanctions as reflecting money saved by avoiding required preparation and attendance, the plaintiff's costs and fees, and the additional burden caused by the frivolous motion. Footnote 5 distinguished authorities involving defaults, summary judgment, or dismissal that prevented litigants from being heard. The monetary sanctions here addressed specific pretrial conduct and allowed the employment case to continue. Id. at 597 & n.5.

The Eighth Circuit **affirmed** the order denying reconsideration and imposing additional sanctions. McMillian wrote for the three-judge panel, with no separate writing. The result upheld the challenged monetary penalties; it neither compelled settlement nor decided Nick's employment claims. Id. at 597.
