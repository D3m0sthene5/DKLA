White v. Benkowski
37 Wis. 2d 285, 155 N.W.2d 74 (1967)
Citation, court, and assigned scope
The Supreme Court of Wisconsin decided this appeal on December 22, 1967. Justice Wilkie
wrote the opinion. The course excerpt appears at printed pages 30–33. This brief draws the
agreement's background from the casebook's expressly bracketed factual account and the
appellate reasoning and disposition from the full judicial opinion. Pinpoints below refer to
Wisconsin Reports pages.
Parties and their objectives
Virgil and Gwynneth White were homeowners entitled under an agreement to receive water
from a well on their neighbors' property. Paul and Ruth Benkowski controlled that well. The
Whites sought compensation for interruptions of the promised supply and punitive damages
based on the jury's finding that the interruptions were malicious. After the trial court
reduced the verdict, the Whites appealed to restore both components. The case concerns the
permissible remedies for the contract breach established in the action. (Casebook pp. 30–31;
37 Wis. 2d at 288–92.)
Material facts
The Whites' home lacked its own water supply but was connected to the Benkowskis' well. A
written agreement provided water for ten years, subject to contingencies such as municipal
supply, an inadequate well, or the Whites' acquisition of their own well. The Whites agreed to
pay $3 monthly and share future repair and maintenance costs. The initially friendly
relationship deteriorated, and the water was shut off on several occasions. The casebook
identifies nine interruptions of less than an hour and reports the Benkowskis' explanations
involving sand in the pipes and excessive use. (Casebook pp. 30–31.)
The full opinion identifies three concrete inconveniences. On one occasion, lack of water in
the bathroom produced an odor. On two others, Mrs. White had to take her children to a
neighbor's home for bathing. Those were actual adverse consequences even though the
plaintiffs supplied no precise market calculation of their value. Their significance was
obscured when the trial judge focused only on the very small portion of the monthly service
price attributable to the interruption periods. (37 Wis. 2d at 288–89.)
The jury found that the shutoffs had been imposed maliciously to harass the Whites. It
awarded $10 in compensatory damages and $2,000 in punitive damages. The malicious
motive supported the plaintiffs' request for punishment, but the action was pleaded and
proved in contract; no tort was pleaded or proved. That limitation on the theory of the case
ultimately controlled the punitive-damages issue. (Casebook p. 31; 37 Wis. 2d at 290–92.)

Procedural history and review posture
The trial court instructed that compensatory damages could include pecuniary loss and
inconvenience naturally resulting from breach, but also told the jury that unproved actual
damages could produce nominal recovery. The Whites did not object to the instruction. After
the jury returned its verdict, the court reduced compensation to $1 and struck punitive
damages. Its explanation valued the undelivered service at approximately twenty-five cents
and treated the injury as nominal. (37 Wis. 2d at 288–89.)
On appeal, the Supreme Court separated the two remedial questions. It evaluated the
evidence supporting the jury's $10 estimate in the light favorable to the plaintiffs and then
considered the independent legal availability of punitive damages in the cause of action
before it. Establishing actual injury did not necessarily entitle the Whites to punitive relief;
removing the trial court's nominal-damages rationale still left the contract-law limitation to
be addressed. (Id. at 289–92.)
Issues
Was the trial court entitled to reduce a modest jury award to a nominal sum despite evidence
of inconvenience from the interrupted water supply? And did a malicious, willful breach
justify punitive damages in an action where no tort was pleaded or proved? The issues
concern different remedial purposes and do not rise or fall together. (Id. at 288, 290.)
Rules and holdings
The evidence of inconvenience established actual injury. Damages did not have to be proved
with mathematical precision before the jury could assign a reasonable compensatory amount.
The $10 award was actual compensation despite its small size, and the trial court erred in
replacing it with $1. (Id. at 289.)
Punitive damages were unavailable on the contract claim presented, even though the breach
was willful. The court distinguished compensation from punishment and recognized that
conduct arising in a contractual relationship may sometimes also constitute a tort. That
possibility did not help the Whites because no such tort was pleaded or proved here. The rule
should therefore be stated with that procedural and doctrinal qualification, rather than as a
declaration that wrongful conduct in every contractual setting can never support punitive
relief. (Id. at 290–92.)
Actual judgment
The Supreme Court reversed in part, reinstating the jury's $10 compensatory award, and
otherwise affirmed. The $2,000 punitive award remained eliminated. It awarded costs to the
appellants. It did not reinstate the entire verdict or affirm the reduction of actual
compensation to a nominal dollar. (Id. at 292.)

Reasoning
Inconvenience supplied a compensable injury beyond the service price
The value of the interrupted water was not exhausted by prorating the monthly charge. The
agreement supplied a household necessity, and the interruptions disrupted bathing and
sanitation. The trial judge's arithmetic overlooked the testimony describing those effects. The
jury could attach a modest reasonable amount to them even without receipts or an exact
price for the inconvenience. The court therefore sustained the jury's assessment as
compensation for demonstrated injury. (Id. at 288–89.)
This distinction prevents confusing the amount of an award with its function. A small award
can compensate a real injury, while a nominal award recognizes a violation without
measuring a demonstrated loss. The court contrasted a prior wrongful-entry case in which no
actual injury had been shown. The Whites' evidence placed their award in a different
category; calling $10 “nominal” simply because it was small would erase the factual basis the
jury had accepted. (Id. at 289.)
Punishment and compensation remain distinct even when the breach is malicious
Punitive damages serve to punish and deter wrongdoing beyond making the injured party
whole. The opinion reviewed the general availability of that remedy in tort and the separate
tradition limiting recovery for ordinary contractual breach to compensation. It found the
overwhelming weight of authority against punitive damages for breach of contract and
regarded willfulness as insufficient to change that result in the case before it. The label
malicious described motive; it did not create a new cause of action. (Id. at 290–92.)
The authorities included historical discussion of breach-of-promise-to-marry actions. Those
references show that the court's discussion was situated in the doctrine and categories then
before it. They should not be rewritten into a comprehensive present-day survey of every
statutory or common-law exception. The holding necessary here was that a willful
water-supply breach, pleaded and proved only as contract, did not sustain the punitive
verdict. (Id. at 291–92.)
The possibility of an independent tort did not supply an unpleaded basis for this
verdict
The court acknowledged that a contract can establish a relationship out of which a duty of
care arises. A breach of that duty might also support tort liability in an appropriate case. But
the Whites had not pleaded or proved such a tort. The reviewing court would not convert
evidence of a malicious contractual breach into a separate tort judgment merely to preserve
punishment. Whether particular different pleadings and proof would establish a tort was left
outside the decided case. (Id. at 292.)

The two components required different dispositions
The trial court had reached the correct practical result on punitive damages but had
undervalued the compensatory proof. The Supreme Court could therefore restore $10 while
leaving the deletion of $2,000 intact. That mixed result is an important part of the case: proof
of actual injury defeats the nominal-damages reduction, but does not resolve whether
punishment is legally available. Each component needs its own rule and explanation. (Id. at
289–92.)
Separate opinions
No separate concurrence or dissent appears in the reported decision. The discussion of other
cases' rules concerns cited authorities, not disagreement within the deciding court.
Use in class and source qualifications
When applying this case, distinguish the price of the promised service, consequential
inconvenience demonstrated by evidence, and a request to punish the defendant. Then
identify the pleaded source of liability. A supported estimate of compensatory harm can be
modest and imprecise without becoming nominal; malicious motive alone does not establish
the additional legal basis required for punitive relief in this action.
The online host's annotation describes the $10 award as nominal or exemplary in places.
Those labels conflict with the judicial opinion, which expressly classifies it as actual
compensatory damages. This brief uses the opinion's terminology. The casebook's bracketed
account supplies details of the agreement and the original verdict; those editorial facts are
cited as such rather than falsely attributed to a passage in the abbreviated electronic opinion.
