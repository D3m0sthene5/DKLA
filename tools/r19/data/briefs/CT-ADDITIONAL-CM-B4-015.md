# Lamm v. Shingleton
231 N.C. 10, 55 S.E.2d 810 (1949) | Contracts

## Court and result
The North Carolina Supreme Court decided the appeal on November 2, 1949. Justice Barnhill wrote the court’s opinion; Justice Seawell wrote a dissent agreeing that a new trial was necessary but objecting to the majority’s restrictions on the widow’s contract claim. The court sustained the nonsuit on the separately pleaded vault-warranty claim and ordered a new trial on the remaining burial-contract claim. It held that mental suffering could be compensable for breach of this personal contract without requiring the jury to find unlawful, willful negligence. The report does not supply a complete numerical voting lineup. (10–11, 13–17.)

## Facts and competing evidence
Blummer Waddell Lamm’s husband, Larry Waddell, died on August 3, 1946. She employed the defendants, who operated Hunt Funeral Home, to conduct the funeral and bought a casket and burial vault from them. The vault had a base and a metal cover secured by ratchet locks at each end. The defendants represented and warranted that it was watertight and would protect the body from water for years. (11.)

During a rainy period on the Wednesday before Thanksgiving, she discovered that one end of the vault’s top had risen about six inches above ground. She reported the condition to the defendants and cemetery authorities. On the following Saturday, employees of both met to move the vault to an adjoining grave while she was present. When it was raised, water and mud were discovered inside, and the casket was wet. (11–12.)

The parties disputed how that condition arose. Her evidence tended to show that the cover had not been locked to the base at the original burial. The defendants’ evidence instead tended to show that it had been securely locked, but a lever used during removal slipped and dislodged the locks, allowing water and mud to enter then. They also offered evidence that cemetery rules reserved actual interment and removal to cemetery authorities. These were competing evidentiary accounts, not factual findings establishing that the funeral home caused the condition. (12.)

The plaintiff testified that seeing the protruding vault shocked her, made her extremely nervous, and impaired her condition. She also described additional distress when defendant Shingleton refused to remove the mud and angrily disclaimed concern for the matter. The case therefore concerned both the character of the promised burial service and the resulting personal injury she claimed, rather than the market value of a defective product alone. (12–15.)

## Procedure and questions presented
She pleaded two causes of action: breach of the agreement to conduct the funeral and inter her husband’s body, and breach of warranty in the sale of the vault. At the close of her evidence, the trial court nonsuited the warranty claim but allowed the burial-contract claim to proceed. After the defendants presented evidence, it again refused to dismiss that remaining claim. The first jury issue asked whether the defendants caused injury through unlawful, willful negligence and carelessness in the burial. The jury answered no, and judgment provided that she take nothing. She appealed. (11–12.)

The Supreme Court had to decide whether her proof supported the separately pleaded warranty claim, whether the surviving action was contractual despite allegations of negligence, and whether mental anguish could be recovered without proving a willful tort. These questions required separate treatment: reversal of the charge on the burial claim did not automatically revive the already-dismissed warranty claim. (12–14.)

## The warranty nonsuit remained effective
The majority concluded that the plaintiff’s evidence attributed the water entry to failure to lock the vault, not to a defect making the vault non-waterproof when properly assembled and locked. On that evidence she had not proved breach of the separately treated product warranty. Although the defendants later testified that the vault had originally been locked, that later evidence could not help her avoid the earlier nonsuit. The warranty cause was no longer pending when it was introduced; review of a nonsuit entered when the plaintiff rested depended on her evidence at that point. The majority therefore sustained that ruling. (12–13.)

This reasoning did not establish that the vault had in fact been properly locked or that no contractual duty to lock it existed. It distinguished the warranty cause as the majority understood it from the remaining claim that the funeral home had failed to perform the promised burial service. (13.)

## Contractual duty and emotional-distress damages
The court recognized the widow’s primary right to possess her deceased husband’s body and control its burial, but no mutilation was alleged. Her remaining claim rested on an agreement to furnish the necessary articles, conduct the funeral, and inter the body, breached by failing to secure the vault. Calling that failure negligent and careless did not convert the action into tort. Undertakers who undertook this service impliedly promised to perform it in a good and workmanlike manner; the relevant obligation arose from the contract. Requiring unlawful, willful negligence in the jury issue and charge was consequently erroneous. (13.)

The ordinary contractual damages rule allowed injuries naturally and proximately resulting from breach and reasonably within the parties’ contemplation when they contracted. In ordinary commercial agreements, pecuniary interests predominated, and the anxiety accompanying financial loss was generally considered too remote for compensatory recovery. The court nevertheless rejected an absolute prohibition. Where a personal contract was closely connected with mental concern, solicitude, or the promisee’s sensibilities, and its nature made resulting anguish reasonably foreseeable, compensation for that suffering could be within the contemplated consequences of breach. The opinion used marriage promises and appropriately disclosed death-message contracts as examples of that distinction. (14–15.)

The burial agreement fell within the personal-contract exception. The defendants knew that the plaintiff was the widow. Her concern for the proper burial of her husband, rather than an expectation of substantial pecuniary gain, was the central reason for contracting. A failure of the kind alleged would naturally and probably cause her mental suffering, and the nature of the agreement itself put the undertakers on notice of that consequence. A separate showing that the breach amounted to a willful or independent tort was therefore not the prerequisite imposed by the trial charge. (14–15.)

The court also gave an additional reason that the suggested tort limitation would not defeat recovery on this record: North Carolina treated impairment of health proximately resulting from nervousness caused by shock or fright as physical injury. It reserved whether it might adopt the willful-or-independent-tort doctrine in a proper case; it did not announce that all ordinary contract anxiety constituted a physical injury. Nor did it decide the amount of this plaintiff’s injury. (15.)

## Cemetery-control defense and remand
The defendants’ evidence described the cemetery as City of Wilson property, with actual interments performed exclusively by municipal personnel. Undertakers could lower a casket or vault into the grave but were required to leave the remaining work to those personnel. The court treated this as a defense for the jury upon proper evidence. In light of the plaintiff’s testimony, it did not compel nonsuit of the remaining contract claim. The erroneous issue and charge required remand for a new trial on the issues raised by the pleadings. The mandate did not award a fixed sum, establish that the funeral home caused the failure, or revive the separate warranty cause that the majority had sustained dismissing. (15–16.)

## Seawell’s separate opinion
Justice Seawell agreed that the case should be retried but rejected the majority’s division of the transaction. In his view, the vault was an accessory to a specific undertaking to bury the body in a watertight vault, not a separate sale performable merely by supplying unassembled components. Its ability to exclude water when properly locked was useless unless the defendants performed the necessary assembly and locking. A failure to do that could breach the specific promise intended to prevent the very injury alleged. (16.)

He accordingly regarded a general implied promise of workmanlike service as too narrow a substitute for the express burial obligation. The majority’s treatment, he argued, dismissed the plaintiff’s principal cause and handed her a different claim with additional hurdles. His disagreement concerned the scope of contractual liability available at retrial, rather than opposition to remand or to compensation for the ensuing suffering if the jury found the requisite breach. (16–17.)
