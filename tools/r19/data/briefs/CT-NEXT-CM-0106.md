# Dalton v. Educational Testing Service
87 N.Y.2d 384, 663 N.E.2d 289, 639 N.Y.S.2d 977 (1995) | Contracts

## Score dispute and contractual options

Brian Dalton took the SAT as a high-school junior in May 1991 and again as a senior that November. His combined score rose 410 points, crossing ETS’s 350-point threshold for security review. Staff compared the answer sheets, observed handwriting disparities, and obtained an examiner’s opinion that different individuals had completed them. The Board of Review preliminarily found substantial evidence for cancelling the November score. It had not finally cancelled that score when litigation began. Opinion Part I; Levine dissenting note.

The registration contract reserved ETS’s right to cancel a score when it had reason to question validity, but also promised notice and five options: submit additional relevant information, take a free retest, accept cancellation and a fee refund, obtain review by a score-receiving institution, or arbitrate. The accompanying pamphlet allowed supplemental information in combination with other options and warned that character references alone did not explain handwriting disparities. ETS’s notice identified suspected impersonation—someone else may have completed the answer sheet—as the concern. Part I.

Dalton submitted medical verification of mononucleosis during the first examination, preparatory-course diagnostic scores consistent with the later improvement, statements from a proctor and two students confirming his presence at the November test, and a family-retained examiner’s opinion that he wrote both answer sheets. ETS held several meetings and obtained a second examiner’s opinion supporting its original conclusion. It continued to question the score. The litigation did not ultimately establish whether Dalton cheated or actually took the November examination; the disputed issue was the company’s promised review process. Parts I, III.

## Trial findings and review

Dalton’s father filed an article 78 proceeding, converted to an action at law, seeking to prohibit cancellation and compel release. After a twelve-day nonjury trial, the judge found that ETS had failed even to evaluate or investigate the submitted information. Board members regarded presence at the examination as irrelevant to handwriting and believed only a retest could validate the score. The trial court ordered release, and the Appellate Division affirmed. The Court of Appeals accepted the supported, affirmed finding that relevant information had not been evaluated, although it rejected any supposed duty to conduct an external investigation. Parts I–II.

The difference mattered to the appellate standard. The Court of Appeals could not reweigh conflicting testimony where supported factual findings had been affirmed below. Its majority footnote quotes board members treating presence at the test as a nonissue, supporting the finding of nonconsideration despite other testimony that documents had been reviewed and rejected. The majority concluded that the correct factual question had already been resolved; it did not require another trial merely because the lower courts also invoked an erroneous investigation theory. Part II & majority note.

## Good faith within the actual bargain

Every contract included a covenant of good faith in performance: neither party could destroy the other’s entitlement to the bargain’s benefits, and promised discretion could not be exercised arbitrarily or irrationally. That covenant could not imply an obligation inconsistent with the agreed terms. ETS had not promised to prove cheating before withholding a score, to gather independent evidence, or to interview everyone whose statement the student supplied. Imposing such investigative duties would contradict the allocation of the burden to the test-taker to address the security concern. Part II.

ETS did promise an opportunity to submit relevant information. That option would be empty unless it genuinely considered relevant submissions. Presence at the examination, illness during the lower-scoring sitting, and later diagnostic performance were relevant to the suspected-impersonation question ETS itself posed. Its internal manual recognized medical information and other test results as examples of pertinent evidence. The company could not narrow the issue to handwriting alone and thereby exclude material that bore on whether another person took the test. Part II.

The court distinguished two stages. Once ETS considers relevant material in good faith, it ordinarily controls evidentiary weight and score validity, subject to review for arbitrary or irrational action. Refusing to exercise the promised discretion at all instead raises the direct question whether an express or implied contractual obligation was breached. On the binding findings here, ETS committed the latter breach. This did not authorize a court to decide the score’s academic or security validity itself. Part II.

## Remedy, disagreement, and disposition

Specific performance had to supply what performance would have supplied: good-faith reconsideration of the submitted material. ETS never promised to release an invalid or still-questioned score. Automatically certifying it would exceed the bargain and undermine the credentials on which colleges, students, and others relied. The remaining options, including third-party review and arbitration, remained available alongside reconsideration; the majority rejected the assumption that an order requiring that process must be futile. Part III.

On December 7, 1995, Chief Judge Kaye wrote for five judges, joined by Titone, Bellacosa, Smith, and Ciparick. Levine dissented with Simons. The dissent agreed that no investigative duty existed but thought the lower courts’ reliance on one required at least new findings. It would ultimately have dismissed the complaint, viewing the additional examiner and resubmitted handwriting samples as concrete evidence of consideration and the board’s focus on handwriting as a rational exercise of reserved discretion. The majority, in its view, improperly substituted its own judgment about relevance. Levine dissent and note.

The court modified the Appellate Division order to require good-faith consideration rather than immediate release, and affirmed as modified without costs.
