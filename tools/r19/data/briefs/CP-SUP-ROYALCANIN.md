# Royal Canin U. S. A., Inc. v. Wullschleger
604 U.S. 22 (2025) | Civil Procedure

## Facts and procedural posture

Royal Canin manufactured dog food sold only with a veterinarian’s prescription and at a premium price. Anastasia Wullschleger bought it believing it contained medication absent from ordinary dog food. After learning that it did not, she sued in Missouri state court, alleging that the prescription requirement served to make ordinary food appear medically necessary and induce consumers to pay more. Her original complaint asserted claims under the Missouri Merchandising Practices Act and state antitrust law and relied on alleged violations of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act. These were allegations underlying the litigation; the Supreme Court did not determine whether the product’s marketing was unlawful. Slip opinion at 4–5.

The original complaint did not plead a freestanding FDCA cause of action. Its FDCA allegations supported the state causes of action. In an earlier appeal, the Eighth Circuit held that the federal issues were sufficiently embedded in, and integral to, those claims to create federal-question jurisdiction. The Supreme Court accepted that earlier jurisdictional determination as given rather than reviewing whether this particular state-law complaint satisfied the embedded-federal-question doctrine. The opinion’s references to the original pleading’s “federal claims” are acknowledged shorthand for that more complicated arrangement. Slip opinion at 4–5 & n.1.

Royal Canin removed the case under 28 U.S.C. § 1441(a). The federal questions supplied original jurisdiction, while the factually related state claims came within supplemental jurisdiction. Wullschleger then properly amended her complaint to remove every FDCA reference and asked the District Court to remand her now exclusively state-law action. The court refused, reasoning that jurisdiction was measured at removal and that later elimination of the federal questions did not extinguish supplemental jurisdiction. It subsequently dismissed the amended complaint on the merits. The refusal to remand itself was not immediately appealable; the jurisdictional dispute reached the Eighth Circuit through the appeal following that merits dismissal. Slip opinion at 5 & n.2.

The Eighth Circuit reversed and ordered remand, holding that the amended complaint displaced the original complaint and eliminated both federal-question and supplemental jurisdiction. Other circuits had treated removal-time jurisdiction as sufficient to preserve federal adjudicatory power despite such an amendment. The Supreme Court granted review to resolve that conflict. Slip opinion at 5–6 & n.3.

## Issue, holding, and judgment

When a plaintiff properly amends a removed action to eliminate every federal question and leave only state-law claims, may the federal court retain the case through supplemental jurisdiction? No. The amendment eliminates original federal-question jurisdiction and, with it, supplemental jurisdiction over the remaining state claims. The federal court must remand the suit rather than exercise discretion to retain it. The Court unanimously affirmed the Eighth Circuit’s judgment. Justice Kagan wrote for all nine Justices; there were no separate opinions. Slip opinion at 6, 19–20.

## Governing jurisdictional framework

Federal courts have only the adjudicatory authority that the Constitution permits and Congress confers. Section 1331 supplies original jurisdiction over suits arising under federal law. Usually federal law creates the cause of action; exceptionally, a state cause of action necessarily raises a substantial and actually disputed federal question. In either setting, the well-pleaded complaint controls, rather than a federal issue introduced only through a defense. Section 1441(a) permits a defendant to remove an action falling within original federal jurisdiction. Section 1447(c) requires remand if the court lacks subject-matter jurisdiction before final judgment. Slip opinion at 1–3.

Section 1367(a) extends supplemental jurisdiction to other claims sufficiently related to claims within original jurisdiction to belong to the same Article III case or controversy. Under *United Mine Workers v. Gibbs*, factual relationship through a common nucleus of operative fact supplies the necessary connection. This derivative authority depends on something in the action that falls within original jurisdiction. Section 1367(c), by contrast, concerns discretion to decline supplemental jurisdiction that otherwise exists. It addresses, among other things, novel or complex state issues, substantial predominance of state claims, and a court’s dismissal of all claims within original jurisdiction. Its additional exceptional-circumstances provision was not relevant to either party’s position here. Slip opinion at 2–3, 7–8 & n.4.

## Parties’ arguments and statutory reasoning

Royal Canin relied on § 1367(a)’s broad grant of jurisdiction over related claims. Congress had not expressly stated that an amendment deleting federal questions extinguished supplemental jurisdiction. The company therefore argued that jurisdiction validly acquired through removal survived the amendment. Its proposed rule depended on distinguishing removed cases from cases originally filed in federal court. Slip opinion at 8.

The Court rejected that distinction because § 1367’s text contains no such division. *Rockwell International Corp. v. United States* had already held that, in an action originally filed in federal court, jurisdiction follows the amended complaint. Royal Canin conceded that an amendment deleting the federal basis in that setting would remove the court’s authority over the remaining state claims. The same statute could not produce the contrary result merely because the action began in state court. Once the operative complaint contains no claim supplying original jurisdiction, its state claims are supplemental to nothing. Slip opinion at 8–10.

The structure of § 1367(c) reinforced that conclusion. Congress expressly made supplemental jurisdiction discretionary when the court dismisses all original-jurisdiction claims. Judicial dismissal may be reversed on appeal, reviving the federal claims. A plaintiff’s proper amendment withdrawing those claims removes them from the operative pleading instead. Had § 1367(a) continued to confer jurisdiction after that withdrawal, the Court reasoned, § 1367(c) would have addressed this especially strong occasion for discretionary remand. Its omission of plaintiff-initiated excision, alongside its express treatment of judicial dismissal, supports absence of jurisdiction rather than an unmentioned discretionary power. Slip opinion at 9–10.

## Why the amended pleading controls

An amended complaint supersedes the original pleading. The plaintiff controls the claims and parties included in the action, and changing those claims or parties can change the suit’s jurisdictional basis. The Court illustrated that principle through several statutory and judicial rules. Section 1653 permits amendment of defective jurisdictional allegations; § 1446(b)(3) recognizes that an amended pleading can make a previously nonremovable case removable; and § 1332(d)(7) expressly addresses jurisdiction created by amended class-action pleadings. These provisions undermine the idea that Congress universally freezes jurisdiction by reference to the first pleading. Slip opinion at 10–12.

The Court also examined analogous changes in claims and parties. Adding federal claims can supply jurisdiction to an action previously lacking it. Adding a nondiverse party ordinarily defeats complete diversity, while eliminating such a party can cure its absence. Comparable rules operate after removal: adding a federal claim can establish jurisdiction going forward, and § 1447(e) requires remand when permitted post-removal joinder destroys diversity. These examples support applying the operative-pleading principle to deletion of federal questions in a removed case. Slip opinion at 12–14.

The analogies come with limits. The time-of-filing rule ordinarily fixes actual citizenship facts at the outset; it does not necessarily freeze the selection of claims and parties in superseding pleadings. Substitution of a successor in interest may preserve diversity because the successor steps into the original party’s position. A post-filing reduction in the amount in controversy will usually leave diversity jurisdiction intact, reflecting the changing and difficult-to-measure value of a case and the waste of constantly relitigating that factual measure. The Court distinguished those situations rather than holding that every later pleading change necessarily destroys jurisdiction. It also noted that no appellate decision known to it addressed the particular removed-case scenario in which an amendment eliminates a nondiverse party. Slip opinion at 12–15 & nn.5–8.

## Treatment of earlier Supreme Court language

Royal Canin relied on *Carnegie-Mellon University v. Cohill*, which involved withdrawal of federal claims after removal and contained language suggesting that retention of the remaining state claims was discretionary. The actual question there, however, was whether a court already choosing to relinquish the suit could remand it instead of dismissing it. Dismissal might have prevented renewed state litigation because the limitations period had expired. *Cohill* did not analyze whether a federal court could retain jurisdiction after the amendment; the assumption of that authority supported neither its result nor its reasoning about dismissal versus remand. Moreover, § 1367 and *Rockwell* postdated *Cohill*. Slip opinion at 15–17.

Royal Canin’s strongest support was a *Rockwell* footnote stating that deletion of federal claims generally does not defeat jurisdiction after removal because of concerns about forum manipulation. The Court rejected that statement as dictum: *Rockwell* involved an original federal action, so its removed-case observation was unnecessary to deciding the case and barely reasoned. The Court’s warning against unexamined jurisdictional assertions applied particularly strongly to a footnote addressing an issue neither presented nor relevant. Slip opinion at 17–19.

The policy justification also failed. Plaintiffs can often obtain a state forum by voluntarily dismissing and filing a new state-law action, unless limitations have expired. Section 1447(e) already permits a jurisdiction-destroying amendment in the comparable setting of joinder after removal. Most fundamentally, concerns about manipulating forums cannot override § 1367’s equal treatment of original and removed actions. The Court expressly declined to fault lower courts that had followed its earlier *Rockwell* language: the Supreme Court’s authority to reconsider its own dictum did not make lower courts’ reliance on that language inappropriate. Slip opinion at 18–19 & nn.9–10.

## Application, distinctions, and limits

The original complaint supplied a federal question, and the related state claims initially fell within supplemental jurisdiction. The proper amendment removed every federal question. Without a remaining claim within original jurisdiction, the District Court lacked supplemental authority over the exclusively state-law pleading and should have remanded it. The decision affirms the Eighth Circuit’s jurisdictional judgment; it does not decide whether Wullschleger’s consumer-protection or antitrust allegations succeed on their merits. Slip opinion at 20.

The central comparison is between a court dismissing federal claims and a plaintiff properly amending them out. Section 1367(c)(3) permits discretion in the former situation; this decision requires remand in the latter when no other original-jurisdiction basis remains. The holding also assumes a proper amendment rather than resolving when amendment must be allowed. Its treatment of the original FDCA allegations does not broaden embedded-federal-question jurisdiction, because that issue had already been decided below and was outside the question presented.
