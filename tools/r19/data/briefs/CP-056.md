Parties
Dice was an injured railroad fireman who sued his employer under the Federal Employers’ Liability Act, or
FELA. He was the plaintiff in Ohio court and petitioner in the Supreme Court. The railroad denied negligence
and relied on a signed release as a defense to further liability. The disputed release concerned the federal
employment-injury claim; the suit was not an independent state-law contract action between strangers to
FELA. 342 U.S. at 360–361.
Material Facts
Dice was seriously injured when the engine in which he was riding left the track. He alleged that the railroad’s
negligence caused his injury. The railroad offered a document that purported to settle all claims for $924.63.
Dice admitted signing receipts connected with payments following his injury, but denied knowingly agreeing
to a complete settlement. He alleged that the railroad’s representatives deliberately misrepresented the
document as a receipt for back wages. 342 U.S. at 360.
The fraud issue involved conflicting evidence about what the document was represented to be when Dice
signed it. His ability to read and failure to examine the document became important because the Ohio Supreme
Court treated those circumstances as binding him to the release despite the alleged deception. The question
therefore concerned both the legal effect of deceptive procurement and the institution authorized to decide
disputed facts about it. 342 U.S. at 360–363.
Procedural History
Dice filed in an Ohio court of common pleas. After both sides presented evidence, a jury returned a $25,000
verdict in his favor. The trial judge then entered judgment notwithstanding the verdict. He reappraised the
fraud evidence, characterized Dice’s failure to read the release as negligence, and found the proof insufficient
under a clear-and-convincing formulation. 342 U.S. at 360 & n.*.
The intermediate Ohio appellate court reversed, reasoning that federal law governed the release and that
evidence supported the jury’s fraud finding. The Ohio Supreme Court then reversed that court and reinstated
judgment for the railroad. It held that Ohio law controlled, that a literate signer was bound despite the alleged
misrepresentation, and that the relevant fraud issues belonged to the judge under state practice. 342 U.S. at
360–361.
The United States Supreme Court granted certiorari to review that treatment of the federal claim. It agreed with
the intermediate appellate court, reversed the Ohio Supreme Court, and remanded for proceedings consistent
with its opinion. The federal decision addressed both the legal standard for the release and the improper
removal of the fraud issue from the jury. 342 U.S. at 361–364.

Issue
May a state court apply its own rule concerning the validity of a release to defeat a FELA claim allegedly
settled through deliberate misrepresentation? When the state provides jury trials for FELA negligence actions,
may it assign the disputed factual question of fraudulent procurement to a judge and set aside a supported jury
verdict on that basis? 342 U.S. at 361–364.
Rule and Holding
Federal law governs the validity of releases of FELA rights, including when the action proceeds in a state
court. A release is invalid when the railroad’s authorized representatives induce the employee to sign through
deliberately false and material statements intended to deceive the employee about its contents. The employee’s
failure to read does not entitle the railroad to defeat the federal claim through its own deliberate fraud. 342 U.S.
at 361–362.
Ohio also could not take this supported fraud determination away from the jury. The Court regarded jury
determination as a substantial component of the FELA remedy in the circumstances presented, where the state
furnished jury trials for the negligence action but separated out this aspect of the release defense for judicial
resolution. Ohio’s characterization of the allocation as local procedure did not settle the federal question. 342
U.S. at 362–364.
Judgment
The Supreme Court reversed the Ohio Supreme Court and remanded to that court for further proceedings
consistent with the opinion. It expressly stated that the intermediate Ohio appellate judgment was correct and
should not have been reversed. The decision rejected the grounds on which the state courts had taken away
Dice’s favorable verdict; it did not award a new or different damages amount. 342 U.S. at 363–364.
Reasoning
The first step was to identify the source of the right being enforced. Congress had given railroad workers a
federal damages remedy for injuries negligently caused by their employers. State law could not have the final
word on a defense that determines whether that federal right survives. Otherwise, identical FELA claims would
receive different protection depending on which state supplied the release doctrine. Uniform federal standards
were necessary to preserve the right Congress created. 342 U.S. at 361–362.
The Court treated releases as an integral part of administering the federal remedy. A release can liquidate or
extinguish an injury claim, so its validity is not a collateral state-law matter merely because it resembles a
contract. Permitting a state to uphold a fraudulently obtained release would allow local law to defeat the federal
right indirectly through a defense. The same federal source that governs the underlying liability therefore
governs this incident of the claim. 342 U.S. at 361–362.
On the substance of fraud, the Ohio rule allowed the railroad to invoke the worker’s failure to read even when
its representatives deliberately deceived him about the document. The Court considered that result inconsistent
with FELA’s compensatory purpose. The relevant federal standard focused on deliberate, material
misrepresentation by authorized representatives and the employee’s induced signature. It did not make every
unfavorable settlement invalid, but denied effect to a release obtained through the particular kind of deception
alleged and found here. 342 U.S. at 362.
The allocation of factfinding raised a separate question. Ohio ordinarily used juries for negligence, but its
law–equity division treated some fraudulent-release disputes as matters for the judge. The railroad invoked
Bombolis, which allowed state jury arrangements differing from federal practice in FELA litigation. The

majority distinguished that decision because Ohio already supplied a jury for these actions while removing one

Drawing on Bailey, the Court described the jury’s role as a substantial feature of the FELA remedy. The fraud
issue had been tried on conflicting evidence, and the jury had found for Dice. A judge’s reassessment under a
contrary local allocation could not simply be labeled procedural and insulated from federal review. The Court
accordingly rejected both the state substantive rule and the state practice used to override the jury’s supported
determination. 342 U.S. at 363–364.
The reasoning does not rest on a holding that the Seventh Amendment directly imposes every federal jury
requirement on state courts. The Court discussed Bombolis and the specific federal statutory remedy. Its
rationale concerned the substantial role of jury determination in administering FELA under the circumstances
before it, and the separate opinion sharply disputed that statutory inference. 342 U.S. at 362–364, 364–369.
Separate Opinions
Justice Frankfurter and the three joining Justices agreed that the Ohio judgment had to be reversed because a
federal standard governed release validity. They disagreed about the required factfinder. In their account, Ohio
applied its traditional distinction between legal and equitable issues to all negligence cases, without singling
out FELA claims for disadvantage. Concurrent state jurisdiction should permit those ordinary local
arrangements unless they diminish the substance of the federal right. 342 U.S. at 364–369 (Frankfurter, J.,
concurring for reversal but dissenting from the opinion).
Frankfurter read Bombolis as preserving state control over jury arrangements, including allocation between
judge and jury. He thought the majority effectively required a hybrid procedure for one category of negligence
action despite Congress’s decision to permit litigation in either court system. The disagreement therefore
concerned whether jury resolution of the release issue was itself part of the federal substantive protection or
remained a permissible local method of enforcing it. 342 U.S. at 365–369.
He nevertheless would have remanded because the Ohio Supreme Court used state release law and because the
trial judge’s fraud-proof formulation did not assure application of the correct federal standard. The separate
opinion stated that a preponderance of relevant evidence sufficed and would have returned the case for further
proceedings on the release’s fraud issue. The majority did not decide a challenge to the jury’s
clear-and-convincing instruction: Dice had won the verdict and did not contest that instruction as too
demanding. The separate opinion’s proof formulation should not be mislabeled as an expressly decided
majority holding. 342 U.S. at 360 n.*, 369–370.
Notes
Dice illustrates the reciprocal problem to Erie: a state court enforcing federal law must preserve the substance
of the federal right, including procedural features the governing federal law treats as integral. Both questions
require identifying the source of the right and the function of the disputed rule. A state court’s procedural label
is relevant context but cannot conclusively define the scope of a federal remedy. 342 U.S. at 361–364.
The result is narrower than a rule requiring juries on every issue in every state-court federal action. It concerns
FELA, a state that supplied juries for the negligence litigation, and an attempt to remove a supported fraud
issue that determined whether the employee’s statutory claim survived. The majority and Frankfurter agreed on
federal release law while disagreeing about that institutional consequence. 342 U.S. at 362–370.
