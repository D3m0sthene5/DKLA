# Hubner v. Spring Valley Equestrian Center — Appellate Division
408 N.J. Super. 626, 975 A.2d 992 (App. Div. 2009) | Contracts

## Court, stage, and result

Judge Grall delivered the New Jersey Appellate Division’s August 4, 2009 opinion on a panel with Judges Graves and Ashrafi. No separate writing appears. The court reversed summary judgment for Spring Valley on both grounds supporting it: statutory protection for inherent equine risks and Gloria Hubner’s ordinary-negligence release. This was a ruling that plaintiffs could proceed, not a finding that the stable had committed negligence or owed a particular damages award. The New Jersey Supreme Court subsequently reversed this appellate decision and reinstated summary judgment on the statutory ground in 203 N.J. 184 (2010), expressly leaving the release question undecided. The two decisions must be read as distinct stages. (408 N.J. Super. at 626, 629, 635–637; 203 N.J. at 188, 208.)

## Facts and procedural setting

Gloria and Michael Hubner and four other members of their party visited Spring Valley to ride its horses on trails under an employee’s guidance. Gloria signed a rental agreement and a release covering injury caused by the stable’s ordinary negligence in relation to its premises and operations. They then entered a barn containing an open mounting area, movable mounting steps, and six to eight pine poles lying parallel on the floor approximately fifteen feet from the mounting area. The poles, roughly six inches in diameter and fourteen to sixteen feet long, simulated trail obstacles so that horses and riders could encounter them before going outside. (Id. at 629–630.)

Employee and trail guide Kate Martin brought Gloria the saddled and bridled horse Lady. Gloria mounted from the ground while Martin stood nearby. Lady initially faced the poles; after Martin led her away from the mounting area, horse and rider faced the opposite direction with the poles behind them. At this stage of the litigation, the appellate opinion did not resolve whether Martin had turned Lady or Lady had turned herself. Martin went to obtain another horse. Lady whinnied, moved her head, and backed up. Martin returned and instructed Gloria to release the reins, which she immediately did. Lady nevertheless backed into the poles, tripped, and fell, throwing Gloria onto the movable steps, landing on her, and rolling over her to stand. Gloria suffered fractures and other injuries. The court stated these facts and reasonable inferences in the light most favorable to plaintiffs because it was reviewing summary judgment. (Id. at 629–630.)

Plaintiffs’ expert Andrew Elder attributed the accident to unsecured poles near the mounting area and behind the horses. He relied on horse-training guidance that ground poles should be secured against rolling when stepped upon and reasoned that horses could not see the poles behind them; stepping on an unsecured pole could further frighten a horse and cause a fall. This was the plaintiffs’ expert evidence, not an adjudicated finding that Spring Valley’s arrangement was negligent. The trial court concluded that the statutory allocation of equine risks barred Gloria’s personal-injury claim and Michael’s consequential-loss claim. It also accepted the ordinary-negligence release as an independent defense. (Id. at 629, 631–633.)

## The statutory issue and competing positions

New Jersey’s equine-activities statute allocated risks because the activities attracted residents and visitors, supported the economy and open space, and involved dangers operators could not practically eliminate. Participants assumed inherent risks, and resulting injuries could trigger a complete defense. But section 5:15-9 preserved operator liability in specified circumstances, including knowingly supplying faulty equipment that caused or contributed to injury, an unwarned known dangerous latent condition, negligent disregard for participant safety causing injury, and intentional injury. The opinion’s first two notes define participants and operators; its third identifies the operator’s warning-posting obligation. (Id. at 631–632 & nn. 1–3.)

Spring Valley maintained that the complete bar for assumed risks, together with the statute’s treatment of comparative negligence, precluded liability here. Plaintiffs’ evidence instead characterized the equipment’s installation and placement as controllable additional dangers falling within the faulty-equipment or negligent-disregard exceptions. The court applied the same summary-judgment standard as the trial court and asked whether the evidence permitted a jury to find an exception, rather than resolving liability itself. (Id. at 632–635.)

## Statutory reasoning and holding

The appellate court distinguished dangers integral to equine activities from every possible danger found on a riding track or in a barn. The statutory examples, including natural ground conditions, animal behavior, potential collisions, and other riders’ negligence, did not establish that dangerous conduct or instrumentalities within an operator’s control were necessarily inherent risks. The exceptions operated notwithstanding a participant’s statutory assumption of risk. The court reasoned that an operator could knowingly provide faulty equipment by using it improperly installed or for an inappropriate purpose; positioning equipment to create an unnecessary injury risk could likewise constitute negligent disregard even where the general possibility of collisions was inherent. A participant would still have to prove the applicable exception’s elements and that the specified act or omission caused the injury. (Id. at 633–634.)

Spring Valley’s broader immunity interpretation would leave little incentive to eliminate known, unnecessary risks readily within an operator’s control. That result would contradict a statutory scheme aimed at shifting to participants the risks operators could not practically eliminate. Elder’s opinion concerning the unsecured poles and placement of poles and steps created a sufficient jury question under subsection (a) or (d). Accordingly, the appellate court reversed statutory summary judgment. Its holding concerned the sufficiency of this record to proceed; it did not impose absolute liability whenever training equipment was present or an accident occurred. The later Supreme Court decision rejected this application of the statute. (Id. at 634–635; 203 N.J. at 202–208.)

## The ordinary-negligence release

The release was broad enough to cover the ordinary-negligence claim. It released the stable and persons acting for it from claims arising from its ordinary negligence in relation to premises and operations, while excluding gross negligence and willful and wanton misconduct. The appellate court therefore focused on enforceability against the statutory exception claims, rather than finding the language too narrow or ambiguous. (408 N.J. Super. at 635.)

The court explained that exculpatory clauses receive close scrutiny for public-policy conflicts and unconscionability. Although adhesion waivers can be objectionable, a person’s voluntary execution of a release to engage in recreation does not generally make the release unenforceable merely on that ground. Nor did the equine statute expressly impose affirmative operator duties in the same manner as the ski-area statute. The controlling point was the legislative allocation of risks: section 5:15-9’s exceptions were an important component of the balance that the legislature had adopted. (Id. at 636–637.)

Allowing a release to eliminate liability that those exceptions authorized would upset that balance. Absent further legislative direction, the court held that the release could not defeat personal-injury liability within section 5:15-9 and reversed summary judgment based on the release as well. This conclusion was limited to that policy conflict; the opinion did not invalidate every recreational release or every part of this agreement. The later Supreme Court’s statutory reversal did not decide the release’s validity. (Id. at 637; 203 N.J. at 188.)
