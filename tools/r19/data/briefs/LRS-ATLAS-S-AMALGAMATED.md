# Amalgamated Meat Cutters and Butcher Workmen of North America v. Connally
337 F. Supp. 737 (D.D.C., three-judge court, Oct. 22, 1971) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Facts and the wage freeze

The Union and its affiliated locals had negotiated collective bargaining agreements with major meat packers in April 1970. A general wage increase of twenty-five cents an hour was scheduled for September 6, 1971. The Union alleged that this concession, obtained after difficult negotiations and exchanges of alternative benefits, was decisive to accepting the agreements. President Nixon's August 15, 1971 Executive Order 11615 intervened before the increase became effective. It imposed a ninety-day freeze on prices, rents, wages, and salaries, using the highest rates applicable to a substantial volume of actual transactions during the thirty days ending August 14 as the usual ceiling. The employers contended that paying the promised increase would violate the order. 337 F. Supp. at 742–743; Annex B, 765–767.

The order rested on the Economic Stabilization Act of 1970, as amended. Section 202 authorized the President to stabilize prices, rents, wages, and salaries at levels no lower than those prevailing on May 25, 1970, and permitted adjustments to prevent gross inequities. A 1971 amendment restricted controls directed at a particular industry or economic segment unless the President determined that its prices or wages had increased disproportionately, taking specified mitigating factors into account. Section 203 permitted delegation to federal officers, departments, and agencies; sections 204–205 provided fines and injunctions for violations. The authority then expired on April 30, 1972. The executive order established the Cost of Living Council as a federal agency, chaired by Treasury Secretary Connally, and delegated the President's statutory powers to it. 743, 747; Annex A, 764–765; Annex B, 766–767.

## Proceedings and competing positions

Count II sought enforcement of the employers' wage obligations, challenging the statute and order that supplied their defense. Count I sued Connally and other Council officials for a declaration of invalidity and an injunction against administration of the stabilization program. A three-judge district court was convened on October 3 under the then-applicable statutes, and heard argument on the preliminary-injunction motion on October 8. Circuit Judge Leventhal wrote for the panel, which also comprised District Judges Robinson and Richey. The complete report contains one judicial opinion and no separate writing or reported numerical vote. 742–743.

The government's threshold objection was that ordinary remedies against employers were adequate, defeating the equity jurisdiction necessary for the three-judge proceeding. The court rejected it. Allegedly more than 150,000 employees were affected across numerous industries. Requiring individual wage suits would multiply litigation, while later recovery did not provide the same protection as receiving scheduled increases when employees had planned to use them. Equity jurisdiction did not disappear merely because the court ultimately might decline an injunction on the merits or balance of conveniences. 743–744.

The Union's central argument was unconstitutional delegation: Congress had given the President discretion over whether, when, and how broadly to regulate virtually the entire economy without prescribing adequate standards. It relied particularly on Schechter and Panama Refining, contrasted the Act's abbreviated wording with earlier stabilization laws, and argued that omission of an express fairness command and special review machinery gave the Executive power to act arbitrarily. It separately challenged interference with existing contracts and coverage of fringe benefits. The government principally relied on Yakus, historical stabilization practice, the authority's short duration, and opportunities to raise objections during enforcement. 744–745, 749, 754–755, 760, 763–764.

## Holding and exact order

The court rejected the facial constitutional and statutory challenges and denied the Union's motion for injunctive relief. Although a preliminary injunction ordinarily requires a probability-of-success assessment, the parties' presentation contemplated no further evidentiary stage and the court treated the legal issues as calling for a determinative ruling. The reported order denies injunctive relief; it does not award unpaid wages, damages, or a remand, or separately announce dismissal of every claim in the complaint. 743, 764.

Congress could delegate this stabilization authority because its text, purpose, historical setting, duration, and implied procedural and substantive constraints supplied an ascertainable legislative policy. The court construed the Act to require broad fairness and avoidance of gross inequity for controls following the initial freeze, ongoing administrative standards, and meaningful review under the Administrative Procedure Act. It sustained the delegation on that construction rather than accepting unlimited presidential discretion. 746–748, 754–762.

## Why the delegation had sufficient direction

The question was accountability to legislative will, not whether delegated authority was broad. Under Yakus and Hampton, Congress had to determine the basic policy and sufficiently identify the field of action so Congress, courts, and the public could assess compliance. Standards could acquire meaning from statutory purpose, history, and established administrative experience. The challenger therefore needed to show an absence of standards that made compliance impossible to ascertain; breadth alone did not establish unconstitutional delegation. 745–747.

Several features directed the initial freeze. The statutory floor and special restrictions on sector-specific controls limited presidential choice. Earlier wartime stabilization programs supplied a body of experience with general freezes, subsequent adjustments, and judicial interpretation. The 1970 Act did not duplicate those programs or permanently freeze their policies, but neither did it begin without a recognizable regulatory context. Its cost-of-living purpose and House committee report identified inflation control and protection of consumers, wage earners, and persons on fixed incomes. The report expressly contemplated a short general freeze. 747–750.

Congress also had reasons to leave timing to the President. It sought standby direct controls when fiscal and monetary approaches might impose intolerable unemployment or recession. Sudden action could interrupt inflationary expectations; advance congressional proceedings might instead encourage anticipatory increases. Congress might be out of session when action or termination became necessary. The statute did not require a newly declared emergency, and the court declined to impose one constitutionally or independently adjudicate whether the President's economic diagnosis was best. Creeping inflation could warrant prompt action even without a sudden wartime price surge. International monetary and trade consequences strengthened the case for coordinated presidential timing. 750–754 and notes 22–28.

Limited duration was material because expiration forced Congress to revisit the delegation affirmatively rather than acquiesce indefinitely through inaction. Congress had granted short extensions and rejected the administration's request for two years. But short duration did not itself answer the objection to arbitrary power. The court expressly rejected the government's suggestion that a sunset and formally open courthouse doors were sufficient if judges lacked standards against which to test action. 754–755 and notes 29–30.

## Fairness, administrative policy, and review

The court read section 202's inequity-adjustment authority, the stabilization purpose, prior programs, and constitutional considerations to imply a duty of broad fairness and avoidance of gross inequity. A general freeze inevitably contained some arbitrariness that could be tolerated during a short opening period; perpetuating it indiscriminately risked departing from responsible government. Follow-on controls therefore required more refined standards. The court did not define every application of fairness in advance: it left specific challenges for later cases and recognized that practical limits could justify aggregate treatment, postponed issues, and refusal to give every seller an individualized adjustment. 755–758 and notes 31–35.

Subsidiary administrative policies further constrained subsequent action. Once developed, those policies limited executive latitude and made conformity to the statutory objective assessable. The Act did not require formal findings for every choice, but it did require intelligible ongoing policy. This judicial interpretation forms an essential part of the court's justification; the opinion did not hold that an agency could replace Congress's ultimate policy with any objective of its own. 758–759 and notes 36–38.

The court also rejected confinement of review to defendants willing to risk violating controls. APA sections 701–706 permitted appropriate declaratory or injunctive proceedings, including pre-enforcement review when agency action was final, ripe, and directly consequential and other requirements were met. The court reserved how those requirements would apply to particular future actions. It found no legislative intent to remove stabilization action from the ordinary presumption of review. 759–761.

The Council was unquestionably a federal authority subject to the APA. Though commentators treated the President himself as an agency, the court expressly found it unnecessary to decide whether review could be brought against him by name: officials exercising delegated functions were proper targets. The Defense Production Act exception did not cover this separately enacted stabilization title. APA administrative procedures applied as well, but applicability did not guarantee notice, comment, and formal adjudicatory hearings in every instance. Section 553's good-cause exception and section 554's hearing prerequisites remained relevant. An administration proposal to exempt Phase II from the APA was a proposed future change, not law governing this judgment. 760–762 and notes 39–44.

## Remaining objections and boundaries

Yakus did not depend exclusively on wartime powers. Youngstown involved executive seizure without statutory authorization, while this case involved an actual legislative grant. The court distinguished Schechter's comprehensive industrial codes and delegation to private groups from this historically grounded, temporary public stabilization program. It reserved a separate question about proposed Price Commission and Pay Board arrangements: that claim was not presented here, the future structure was unknown, and an unlawful later implementation would not necessarily invalidate the Act from inception. 762–763 and note 45.

Existing contracts did not immunize promised increases from federal regulation. The constitutional Contracts Clause applied to states, so the federal objection implicated due process instead. This order operated prospectively on increases scheduled after issuance, and the general freeze fell plainly within congressional contemplation. The court left the weight given earlier agreements in later administration to subsidiary policy. It also held that statutory wages and salaries covered fringe benefits: omission of the phrase other compensation did not compel exclusion, especially given earlier decisions treating similar formulations as substantively equivalent and Congress's concern with cost-push inflation. 763–764.
