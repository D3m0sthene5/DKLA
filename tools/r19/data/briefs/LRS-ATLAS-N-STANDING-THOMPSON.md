# Thompson v. North American Stainless, LP

**562 U.S. 170 (2011) — Supreme Court of the United States, January 24, 2011.** Justice Scalia wrote for all eight participating Justices. Justice Kagan did not participate. Ginsburg concurred separately, joined by Breyer. Assigned in LRS November-b, physical PDF 168, printed 1258.

## Facts and procedural chronology

Eric Thompson and his fiancée, Miriam Regalado, both worked for North American Stainless. In February 2003, the EEOC notified the company that Regalado had filed a sex-discrimination charge. Three weeks later, the company fired Thompson. He alleged that his firing was intended to retaliate against her for filing that charge. He filed his own EEOC charge and, after unsuccessful conciliation, sued under Title VII in the Eastern District of Kentucky. The Supreme Court was required to assume the alleged retaliatory motive in the procedural posture before it; it did not independently find that motive proved. (172–173.)

The district court granted summary judgment to the employer on the ground that Title VII did not permit third-party retaliation claims. A Sixth Circuit panel reversed, but the en banc court affirmed the district court by 10–6. It reasoned that Thompson had not himself engaged in protected activity, whether for himself or Regalado, and thus belonged to no class of persons Congress authorized to sue for retaliation. The Supreme Court separated two questions: whether the alleged firing was unlawful retaliation and whether Thompson personally possessed a cause of action for that violation. (172–173.)

## Holdings

Assuming Thompson’s allegations were true, firing him to punish his fiancée violated Title VII’s antiretaliation prohibition. The statute broadly covered employer conduct that could dissuade a reasonable worker from making or supporting a discrimination charge, and firing the worker’s fiancé plainly could do so. The company did not dispute that the alleged firing met that deterrence standard. (173–175.)

Thompson also could sue as a person aggrieved under Title VII. The Court rejected both equating that phrase with every person having Article III injury and limiting it to the employee who personally engaged in protected conduct. It adopted the zone-of-interests approach for the statutory cause of action. Thompson was an employee deliberately injured as the means of retaliation against another employee, placing him within the interests Title VII sought to protect. The Court reversed the Sixth Circuit and remanded for further proceedings. (175–178.)

## Retaliation and the employer’s line-drawing objection

The Court relied on Burlington Northern’s distinction between substantive employment discrimination and retaliation. The latter prohibition was not limited to conduct altering an employee’s terms or conditions of employment. Its text and purpose supported an objective deterrence test encompassing a broad range of employer conduct. That framework did not exclude injury inflicted through a third party. (173–174.)

The employer argued that this would create uncertainty about girlfriends, close friends, and trusted coworkers and expose employers whenever a dismissed employee knew someone who had filed a charge. The Court acknowledged the line-drawing problem but found no textual basis for a categorical exemption from a broadly worded prohibition. It declined to create a fixed list of protected relationships. Firing a close family member would almost always satisfy the deterrence standard; a mild reprisal against a mere acquaintance almost never would. Other cases depended on their circumstances, assessed objectively rather than by an unusually sensitive claimant’s subjective reaction. (174–175.)

That reservation matters. The case did not hold that any adverse action against anyone connected to a complainant automatically amounted to retaliation. It held that these alleged facts met the governing standard and rejected an absolute bar based on the identity of the directly injured employee.

## Why Article III injury was necessary but not sufficient

The Court regarded Thompson’s constitutional injury, causation, and redressability as clear. It nevertheless distinguished those minimum requirements from the statutory authorization to sue. Earlier Trafficante dictum had suggested that Title VII’s person-aggrieved language extended as broadly as Article III; the Court declined to follow that dictum. It explained that a shareholder suffering a reduced share value because a company unlawfully fired a valuable worker could have constitutional injury yet fall outside Title VII’s cause of action. Its discussion concerned Title VII, the statute before it, rather than a wholesale revision of every similar phrase in other statutes. (175–177.)

The employer’s opposing construction was also too narrow. Person aggrieved was not an established term meaning only the employee who engaged in protected activity. Congress could have used language expressly limited to the person discriminated against, but it had not. The Court found the APA’s usage of aggrieved helpful: the zone-of-interests test excluded interests so marginally related or inconsistent with statutory purposes that Congress could not reasonably be understood to permit suit. Interests arguably sought to be protected were sufficient. (177–178.)

Thompson did not qualify simply because harm to a third party was foreseeable. On the assumed facts, injuring him was the very device by which the employer punished Regalado. He was an employee of the defendant, and his employment interest was directly implicated in the alleged unlawful act. This intentional role distinguished him from an incidental or accidental victim whose interests might be unrelated to the statutory prohibition. (178.)

## Ginsburg’s concurrence

Ginsburg and Breyer joined the Court’s opinion in full. Their concurrence emphasized that the result accorded with the EEOC’s longstanding interpretation. The agency’s Compliance Manual treated retaliation against closely connected individuals as unlawful when it would discourage exercise of statutory rights and recognized claims by both the protected employee and an employed relative. Ginsburg considered those interpretations worthy of Skidmore respect and noted analogous interpretations under other labor statutes. This additional agency-based justification was the concurrence’s observation, not a claim that the manual conclusively controlled the Court. (179.)

## Judgment, assignment, and limits

The judgment was reversed and remanded. No damages were awarded, and the Supreme Court’s assumed retaliatory facts remained allegations to be addressed in further proceedings. Its analysis established both the potential unlawfulness of the alleged act and Thompson’s entitlement to bring the statutory claim. (178.)
