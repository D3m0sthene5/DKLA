# Matsushita Electric Industrial Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp.
475 U.S. 574 (1986) | Civil Procedure

## Court and precise result
Justice Powell wrote the Supreme Court's 5–4 decision of March 26, 1986, with Chief Justice Burger and Justices Marshall, Rehnquist, and O'Connor joining. White dissented with Brennan, Blackmun, and Stevens. The case concerns how substantive antitrust law constrains the inferences available to a plaintiff resisting summary judgment. The Court reversed the Third Circuit and remanded. It allowed that court to consider sufficiently unambiguous additional evidence of the alleged predatory conspiracy; absent such evidence, defendants were entitled to reinstatement of summary judgment. The judgment was not a Supreme Court trial finding about every transaction or an unconditional immediate judgment disposing of every claim in the litigation. (475 U.S. at 576, 597–598.)

The complete judicial report contains Powell's opinion, all 21 Court footnotes, White's dissent with five footnotes, and an opening amici note. The separate 40-volume party appendix mentioned in the opinion is an evidentiary record, rather than an appendix to a judicial writing reproduced in the report. (Id. at 576–577, 598–607.)

## Alleged scheme and long procedural history
Zenith manufactured and sold television sets in the United States. National Union Electric was successor to Emerson Radio, which left television manufacturing in 1970 after substantial losses. They alleged that Japanese manufacturers and their American affiliates conspired over many years to eliminate American competitors. The alleged arrangement combined artificially high prices in Japan with low prices in the United States, using Japanese profits to fund American losses. Once American competitors disappeared, the alleged conspirators would raise American prices and recover those losses through monopoly profits. These were plaintiffs' allegations, not established historical findings of the Court. (Id. at 577–578, 584.)

National Union Electric first sued in New Jersey in 1970; Zenith filed separately in Pennsylvania in 1974. The cases were consolidated in the Eastern District of Pennsylvania that year. The alleged starting dates also differed: National Union Electric used 1960, Zenith 1953. The 21 petitioners were the remaining defendants after the Third Circuit upheld judgment for three of the original 24. Claims involved the Sherman Act, Robinson-Patman Act, Wilson Tariff Act, and a separately adjudicated Antidumping Act theory. (Id. at 577 n.1, 578–580 nn.3–4, 591 n.13.)

After years of discovery, the district court required final pretrial statements identifying documentary evidence with preclusive effect. It excluded much proposed evidence, including governmental records, business documents, and expert material, then granted summary judgment. It considered the admissible evidence both by category and as a whole. Some material suggested agreements that would not injure these competitors; price cutting was more plausibly ordinary competition than a plan to monopolize. The dependent monopolization and price-discrimination claims failed along with the conspiracy theory. (Id. at 578–579 & n.2.)

The Third Circuit restored substantial excluded evidence and reversed the pertinent summary judgment. It identified possible concerted conduct: a concentrated Japanese market with barriers to American entry, relatively high fixed costs and excess capacity, export minimum or check prices arranged with Japan's Ministry of International Trade and Industry, a five-company rule limiting each producer's American distributors, and rebates that undercut check prices and were concealed from governmental authorities. It thought those circumstances together could support the alleged scheme. The Supreme Court's grant excluded the evidentiary rulings themselves. It also declined review of the separate Antidumping Act decision because the petition's questions and parties' argument did not properly present it. (Id. at 579–582 & nn.3–5.)

## An unlawful agreement is not enough without injury to these plaintiffs
The majority first separated proof of concerted conduct from proof of a conspiracy causing cognizable injury. Cartelization confined to competitive conditions in Japan would not alone support these American claims. Footnote 6 qualified that proposition: foreign conduct can fall within the Sherman Act when it affects American commerce. The plaintiffs still needed to connect the asserted arrangements to injury from American predatory pricing. (Id. at 582–583 & n.6.)

Likewise, agreements raising American prices or restricting output could be unlawful yet benefit competing American producers by allowing higher prices. Check prices and distributor restrictions were therefore not themselves direct evidence of a conspiracy injuring these plaintiffs. Calling all arrangements one overarching conspiracy did not remove the requirement to show injury of the kind antitrust law redresses. The alleged low-price campaign supplied the potentially injurious theory. (Id. at 583–586 & n.7.)

The Court did not select a universal cost test for predatory pricing or decide whether recovery could ever rest on pricing above an incremental-cost measure. It addressed the alleged horizontal conspiracy and the sufficiency of its evidentiary support. Defendants had not appealed the Third Circuit's characterization of a proven predatory agreement as a per se violation. (Id. at 584–585 nn.8–9.)

## Rule 56 and permissible antitrust inferences
Once the movant satisfies its burden, the opponent must identify specific facts creating a genuine issue; speculative doubt is insufficient. The entire record must permit a rational factfinder to return a favorable verdict. Here that meant evidence of both a predatory agreement and resulting antitrust injury. The movants' initial burden had already been resolved below and was not before the Court. (Id. at 585–587 nn.10–12.)

The normal requirement to favor the opponent's reasonable inferences remains, but substantive antitrust law limits inferences from ambiguous behavior. Conduct equally consistent with independent price competition and unlawful conspiracy does not, standing alone, establish the conspiracy. Plaintiffs need evidence tending to exclude independent action. Accordingly, economic context matters: a theory making no economic sense requires more persuasive support than an inference readily consistent with commercial incentives. The issue is not whether any conceivable agreement can be imagined; it is whether this record reasonably supports the injurious agreement alleged. (Id. at 587–588.)

## Why the majority found this alleged conspiracy implausible
A predatory campaign imposes present losses in exchange for uncertain future gains. Success requires eliminating competitors, establishing monopoly power, preventing entry, and maintaining higher prices long enough to recover accumulated losses and earn a return. A many-firm cartel adds coordination problems: members must allocate losses and eventual gains, and each has reason to let others bear losses while later sharing profits. If cheating leaves enough goods selling at ordinary prices, the targeted firms can remain in business. (Id. at 588–590.)

The alleged campaign had continued for roughly two decades without accomplishing its stated purpose. RCA and Zenith retained the two largest individual retail shares, together around 40%, even as the Japanese companies' collective share approached 50%. Plaintiffs continued to assert an ongoing attempt to drive Zenith out. The majority saw no demonstrated barriers ensuring that later monopoly prices could be sustained. Increasing market share during loss-making sales would also increase the losses that eventual profits had to repay. These features made prolonged predation especially unlikely in the majority's view. (Id. at 590–593 & nn.13–17.)

Possible Japanese monopoly profits and excess productive capacity showed an ability to export or finance losses, but not a reason to undertake losses unlikely to be recovered. The majority also considered erroneous conspiracy inferences particularly costly where the challenged conduct was low pricing: chilling competitive discounts would undermine what antitrust law protects. It viewed failed predatory cartels as costly to their own members and successful recoupment cartels as independently unlawful and detectable. (Id. at 593–595 & n.18.)

The check-price floor, distribution restrictions, and Japanese price agreements thus suggested either price-raising arrangements or normal competition rather than this prolonged loss-making scheme. The majority discounted expert below-cost evidence that relied on constructed cost assumptions rather than actual cost data, accepting the district court's criticism of its probative value even though the appellate court had restored admissibility. A plausible motive alone would still not make ambiguous conduct sufficient; footnote 21 retained the need for additional evidence beyond equally consistent behavior. (Id. at 594 n.19, 595–597 & nn.20–21.)

## White's dissent: sufficiency without judicial weighing
White agreed that the whole record must permit a rational factfinder to find for plaintiffs and that favorable inferences govern. He disputed the majority's implementation. Comparing how plausible conspiracy was against independent action, he argued, could become judicial weighing rather than asking whether a permissible favorable inference existed. Earlier cases rejecting particular insufficient evidence did not authorize balancing all competing proof as though conducting a trial. (Id. at 598–601 & n.1.)

He also disputed the majority's account of injury. Economist DePodwin's report described how restricting Japanese domestic demand could increase American exports and depress American prices, and how restrictions on competition among Japanese firms could enable coordinated attacks on established American customers. White regarded that expert account as a genuine issue for a factfinder, not something defeated by the Court's preferred economic model. His lengthy second, third, and fourth notes preserved the expert explanation and the Third Circuit's reasons for restoring it. (Id. at 601–604 & nn.2–4.)

White further denied that the Third Circuit had unthinkingly treated any agreement as injurious. It had explained how the several arrangements could operate together and had identified evidence of long-term below-cost sales. Whether manufacturers emphasized growth over immediate profit, and whether the expert's methods were reliable, belonged in factfinder consideration on this record. The majority's criticisms were arguments for trial, rather than reasons to remove the dispute from trial. White would affirm the Third Circuit and remand for trial; his fifth note likewise left sovereign compulsion undecided. (Id. at 604–607 & n.5.)

## Remand, reserved matters, and assigned scope
The majority permitted the Third Circuit to identify other sufficiently unambiguous evidence of predation on remand. It did not decide sovereign compulsion: the claimed immunity concerned government-required minimum prices, while the potentially injurious theory concerned distinct low-price conduct. Evidentiary admissibility rulings and the separate Antidumping Act decision also remained outside this review. (Id. at 579–580 n.3, 597–598.)
