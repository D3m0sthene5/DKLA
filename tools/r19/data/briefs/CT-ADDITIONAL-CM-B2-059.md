# Compass Point Condominium Owners Association v. First Federal Savings & Loan Association of Florence
641 So.2d 253 (Ala. 1994) | Contracts

## Holding and disposition
The Alabama Supreme Court affirmed summary judgment for the seller and other defendants on the condominium purchasers' fraudulent-concealment claim. Evidence that defendants deliberately hid an engineering report did not itself supply substantial evidence of actionable inducement and resulting damage. Water intrusion was observable, purchasers had an opportunity to inspect and accepted the units and common elements as is, and they did not establish that disclosure of the report would have changed their purchase decisions. Justice Maddox wrote the April 15, 1994 opinion, joined by Shores, Houston, Steagall, and Cook. Chief Justice Hornsby concurred specially; Kennedy and Ingram dissented without reproduced writings. The listed result alignment was six to two, with five joining the majority reasoning. (253–257.)

## Auction, report, and concealment evidence
First Federal acquired 68 condominium units in Gulf Shores through litigation settlement and foreclosure and sold them at an August 28, 1988 auction. Its hired management group commissioned Thompson Engineering to investigate leaks. After five days of testing, engineer William Kivette found the building saturated with moisture and recommended recoating with a suitable elastomeric coating. He delivered the report to senior property manager Ray Stronsky. (254.)

On-site manager Terrie Campfield testified that Stronsky instructed her to lock up the report and keep it secret because the auction was two days away and he did not want potential buyers to learn its contents. She did so, and also testified that she once handed it to First Federal executive vice president Thomas Ward. The report was not disclosed to purchasers. There was evidence that Stronsky also told the two testing engineers not to discuss it. The association and 54 individual owners sued First Federal, Ward, and others, asserting deceit and suppression and demanding $350,000 compensatory and $2 million punitive damages. These sums were demands, not awards. The court accepted that substantial evidence supported the described knowledge and concealment conduct, rather than finding that no report had been hidden. (254–255.)

## Summary-judgment burdens and fraud requirements
The trial court entered summary judgment for defendants, and plaintiffs appealed. The Supreme Court applied the same two-part inquiry as the trial court: absence of a genuine material factual issue and entitlement to judgment as a matter of law. Movants first had to make a prima facie showing before the evidentiary burden shifted. The court viewed the record favorably to the nonmovants and resolved reasonable doubts against movants, but allegations alone could not defeat a supported motion. Alabama's substantial-evidence requirement demanded evidence from which impartial, fair-minded persons could reasonably infer the disputed fact. (254–256.)

Fraudulent concealment required a duty to disclose a material fact, failure to disclose or concealment, induced action or inaction, and resulting damage. Active suppression also required knowledge or inferable knowledge of materiality and a purpose to perpetuate a buyer's mistaken impression or suppress inquiry to effect a sale. The analysis therefore distinguished proof of a withheld report from proof of every element of a compensable concealment claim. (255.)

## Observable water intrusion and the as-is agreement
Twenty-one of the 49 plaintiffs who inspected before purchase noticed water intrusion. Engineers testified that a lay observer in the parking lot could not discern incomplete exterior coating, but the court treated coating's visibility as different from the visibility of water intrusion and related damage. The purchasers failed to supply substantial evidence that withholding the report prevented them from discovering water intrusion before closing. The court did not find that all purchasers actually observed every defect; its conclusion concerned the condition's observability, inspection opportunity, and the missing evidentiary links. (255–256.)

Alabama had abolished caveat emptor for a builder-vendor's sale of a new house, but retained it and the absence of an implied habitability warranty for used residential real estate. These plaintiffs had ample opportunity to inspect. After bidding they signed agreements disclaiming warranties of existing condition, accepting both unit and common elements as is, and acknowledging prior inspection. The majority treated that setting as supporting its result. The decision must be read with the special concurrence's explanation of exceptions, rather than turned into a rule that every as-is clause defeats intentional concealment. (255–257.)

The plaintiffs later had the building recoated. Defendants presented uncontradicted testimony that elastomeric recoating was ordinary maintenance, required on average every five years. The court found no evidence that suppression induced the purchasers to act or refrain from acting or caused resulting damage. It accordingly affirmed a supported summary judgment; it did not award recoating costs or punitive damages. (256.)

## Why Soniat did not control
The court distinguished Soniat, where a graph locating old termite damage was allegedly intentionally removed from closing materials. The Soniat purchasers had hired an independent inspector who did not report the damage, the defect was not readily observable, and purchasers said they would not have bought had they seen the graph. Those facts created genuine issues about intentional concealment and purchase inducement. Here water intrusion was observable, a significant number of inspecting purchasers noticed it, and plaintiffs did not state that seeing the engineering report would have deterred purchase. The distinction concerned the evidence of concealed latent condition and reliance, not merely the existence of a report. (256.)

## Hornsby's special concurrence
Hornsby agreed with affirmance but clarified the limits of caveat emptor. Under Fennell Realty, a purchaser's direct inquiry about a material condition could trigger truthful disclosure where an agent assumed an obligation to inspect and disclose findings. A seller or agent also had a disclosure duty for a known health-or-safety defect that the buyer did not know and could not readily observe. (256–257.)

The plaintiffs had expert evidence that water saturation and damage affected health and safety. That did not alter the outcome because they failed to present substantial evidence either of a direct inquiry followed by denial or of unobservable water damage. Evidence that laypersons could not discern deterioration of the coating did not rebut the showing that water intrusion and its damage were readily observable. Hornsby therefore emphasized observability as the reason caveat emptor applied here, preserving a duty for genuinely latent health-or-safety defects despite the majority's reference to the as-is clause. (257.)
