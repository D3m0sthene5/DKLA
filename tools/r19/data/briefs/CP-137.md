# Mathews v. New York Racing Association, Inc.

**Civil Procedure · CP-137 · 193 F. Supp. 293 (S.D.N.Y. 1961) · May 1, 1961**

Mathews granted summary judgment because the plaintiff’s new suit against corporate principals sought to relitigate the same racetrack removal and ensuing prosecution already adjudicated in an action against their employees. The factual transaction defined the claim. Changing from assault and libel theories to false arrest and malicious prosecution did not create a new opportunity for recovery.

New York Racing Association operated Jamaica Race Track and retained Thoroughbred Racing Protective Association, a private detective agency, for security. Joseph Mathews alleged that Thoroughbred’s employees assaulted, kidnapped, falsely arrested, and imprisoned him at the track on April 4, 1958. He also alleged that the defendants maliciously caused his disorderly-conduct prosecution and conviction on April 10. He requested damages and injunctions against interference with attendance at racetracks, libelous publication, and purported exercise of peace-officer authority. These were allegations; the court did not accept them as established misconduct. (193 F. Supp. at 294.)

An earlier federal action had already tried the April 4 removal and statements associated with the April 10 proceeding. Mathews sued three individuals, of whom only two were properly served; those two were employees of the present corporate defendants. He pleaded assault and libel, requested damages and injunctive protection for racetrack attendance, and tried the case before Judge Palmieri without a jury. Judgment entered June 30, 1960 rejected relief. The earlier judge specifically found that Mathews physically resisted removal and that the employees used no more force than reasonably necessary. The prior action also included three separate claims based on different facts; their presence did not change the adjudication of the transaction repeated here. (Id. at 294–95.)

The corporations moved under the then-existing Rule 56(b), relying on that judgment as res judicata. Judge MacMahon identified two connected questions: whether the present corporate parties could invoke the earlier employees’ judgment and whether the new complaint concerned the same claim. He held that the corporations and their agents were identified in interest for this asserted derivative liability. The track owner acted through Thoroughbred, which acted through the individual employees. Because the corporate liability alleged here depended on those agents’ actionable wrongdoing, the principals stood or fell with them. The opinion applied this relationship to the specific respondeat-superior claim, rather than declaring that every employer and employee necessarily shares preclusion for all possible disputes. (Id. at 294–95.)

A claim, the court explained, consists of the facts of an occurrence or transaction rather than the legal theory selected. Although the events took place nearly a week apart, the removal was the subject of the ensuing disorderly-conduct trial and the events were sufficiently interconnected to form one claim. The witnesses to the removal supplied the same underlying account whether the plaintiff called it assault or false arrest. The employees’ statements were the same whether he invoked libel or malicious prosecution. A litigant cannot reserve alternative reasons for recovery from the same alleged invasion and try them serially after losing. Preclusion reached issues tried and other issues that could have been addressed within that claim. (Id. at 294–95.)

The court also relied upon the earlier actual findings, describing Mathews as estopped both directly and collaterally. His pro se status did not require another trial. MacMahon acknowledged the lack of counsel but emphasized that fair adjudication must eventually end litigation. The full opinion adds that Mathews had been given an opportunity to consolidate this action with the earlier suit and had received advice to retain counsel in another related action. An earlier ruling finding the new complaint sufficient did not eliminate the later effect of the completed merits judgment. (Id. at 295–96.)

The court **granted the defendants’ summary-judgment motion**. This is a single district-judge opinion without judicial footnotes or separate writings. The assigned principal passage, printed pages 1270–1272, omits some of the privity discussion, prior findings, and final consolidation analysis. Its appended editorial note explains that the relevant summary-judgment provision later moved to Rule 56(a); that editorial update is not part of the 1961 judgment. The brief describes the historical decision and does not claim an independently checked later procedural or citator status.
