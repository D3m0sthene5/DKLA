# CC/Devas (Mauritius) Ltd. v. Antrix Corp. Ltd.

**605 U.S. 223 (2025) • Supreme Court of the United States • June 5, 2025**

**Assigned source:** Civil Procedure Supplement, printed p. 514 (PDF 346). The opinion also resolves consolidated No. 24–17, Devas Multimedia Private Ltd. v. Antrix Corp. Ltd.; the captioned case is No. 23–1201. Justice Alito delivered the unanimous opinion. There are no separate opinions.

## Facts and procedural history

Antrix was an Indian corporation owned by India for its Department of Space, serving as the commercial arm of India's space program. In 2005 it agreed to build and launch a satellite network and lease capacity to Devas Multimedia, a private Indian company developing satellite telecommunications. Devas would provide multimedia broadcasting in India. Approvals, payments, development, and trials followed, but in 2011 India's Government decided that it needed the relevant spectrum for its own use. Antrix terminated at officials' direction, invoking force majeure. 226–227.

Devas disputed that excuse as self-induced and commenced contractual arbitration. In September 2015, a three-member panel unanimously found a wrongful termination under Indian law and awarded $562.5 million plus interest. Devas obtained confirmation in France and the United Kingdom and in 2018 sought confirmation in the Western District of Washington under the FSIA arbitration exception. The district court confirmed the award and entered a $1.29 billion judgment. The award principal and later judgment therefore describe different amounts at different stages. 228, 230.

An Indian corporate-law tribunal subsequently found that Devas had procured the agreement by fraud, appointed an official to take control, and ordered its affairs wound down. Shareholders and an American subsidiary intervened in the federal proceedings over objections, pursued postjudgment discovery, and registered the judgment in Virginia. The High Court of New Delhi then set aside the arbitration award based largely on the fraud finding. These developments supply procedural background; the Supreme Court did not adjudicate the fraud finding's correctness or the award's enforceability. 230–231.

The Ninth Circuit reversed the confirmation and registration orders for lack of personal jurisdiction. It did not question Antrix's status as a foreign state under the FSIA, proper service, or an applicable immunity exception. Nevertheless, circuit precedent required a separate International Shoe minimum-contacts analysis, and the court found insufficient suit-related contacts. The Supreme Court granted review of that additional statutory requirement. 231.

## Issue and holding

Does the FSIA require proof of minimum contacts beyond the contacts required by its immunity exceptions before a federal court may exercise personal jurisdiction over a foreign state?

**No.** Under § 1330(b), statutory personal jurisdiction exists when an applicable immunity exception supplies subject-matter jurisdiction and service complies with § 1608. Congress did not incorporate an additional minimum-contacts test into that provision. 232–234, 237.

This is a statutory holding. It **does not decide** whether the Fifth Amendment independently requires minimum contacts for a company owned by a foreign sovereign. That constitutional contention was expressly left for remand, along with other defenses. 236–237.

## Reasoning

The Court traced foreign sovereign immunity from case-specific executive suggestions through the restrictive approach to commercial conduct and then the FSIA's statutory framework. The Act sought predictable judicial standards after inconsistent, politically influenced executive practice. It ordinarily makes foreign states immune unless an enumerated exception applies, and links those exceptions to jurisdiction. 228–230.

Section 1330(b)'s operative text identifies two requirements: subject-matter jurisdiction under subsection (a), and service under § 1608. Once both exist, personal jurisdiction shall exist. The text contains no separate minimum-contacts condition. Reading that condition into a statute intended to clarify standards would add something Congress omitted. 232–233.

The Act's exceptions themselves can require domestic connections of varying intensity, such as domestic commercial activity, a direct domestic effect, or relevant domestic property. The Court did not say domestic contacts were irrelevant throughout the FSIA. It said any overlap with International Shoe flowed from the enacted exceptions, not a hidden additional test in § 1330(b). 233–234.

The same conclusion followed from structure. Congress deliberately tied immunity, subject-matter jurisdiction, and personal jurisdiction together, while expressly specifying qualifications such as service and the kinds of civil actions covered. Adding a condition to only the personal-jurisdiction component would create a gap in that comprehensive framework. 234–235.

The Ninth Circuit's predecessor decision relied on a commercial-activity exception's direct-effect language and legislative history. Language in one exception did not establish a categorical additional requirement elsewhere. Even on its own terms, the House Report explained that the immunity exceptions provided the necessary connections and that service supplied notice. It reflected Congress' belief that those provisions satisfied due process, rather than adding an unexpressed statutory test. Whether Congress' assumption was constitutionally correct remained a separate question. 235–236.

## Reserved defenses and exact disposition

Antrix advanced three alternative grounds: a Fifth Amendment minimum-contacts requirement, inapplicability of the arbitration exception, and forum non conveniens. The Ninth Circuit had relied exclusively on its statutory reading and had not addressed those alternatives. The Supreme Court declined to decide them first and allowed their litigation on remand consistently with waiver and forfeiture principles. 236–237.

The Court **reversed the Ninth Circuit's judgment and remanded**. 237. It did not order collection of the judgment, definitively resolve the arbitration exception's application, or hold that every foreign-state-owned entity lacks due-process rights.
