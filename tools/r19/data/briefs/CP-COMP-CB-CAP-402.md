# McCants v. Ford Motor Co.

**781 F.2d 855 (11th Cir. 1986) · February 3, 1986 · Civil Procedure**

## Facts and procedural posture

Johnny McCants died on July 24, 1982, while serving a two-week Army Reserve assignment in Mississippi. His administratrix, Reta McCants, alleged that a defect in the military jeep caused his death. She initially sued AM General in Alabama on July 20, 1983, believing it had manufactured the jeep. After learning that Ford was the manufacturer, she sought to amend that complaint. The court denied amendment and dismissed that action without prejudice. She filed the present action against Ford on November 14, 1983. (855–856.)

Ford initially omitted a limitations defense from its answer. Discovery continued from December 1983 through 1984. Ford amended its answer on July 18, 1984, to assert Alabama's one-year limitations period and moved for summary judgment on August 24. McCants disputed that limitations barred her claim. She moved for voluntary dismissal without prejudice on January 15, 1985, three days before the scheduled summary-judgment hearing, intending to sue in Mississippi under its longer limitations period. Ford opposed dismissal or, alternatively, sought protective conditions. The district court denied summary judgment and then dismissed without prejudice, imposing no conditions and explaining neither decision. Ford appealed. (856–857.)

## Issues and rules

**Does the prospect of losing a limitations defense in a later forum, alone or combined with litigation expense, require denial of dismissal under Rule 41(a)(2)?** No. After the defendant has answered, voluntary dismissal requires a court order on terms the court considers proper. The district court exercises broad equitable discretion, principally to protect the defendant from unfair consequences. Dismissal ordinarily should be permitted absent clear legal prejudice beyond the prospect of another suit. A possible tactical advantage to the plaintiff does not itself require refusal. (856–858.)

**Was the unconditional dismissal adequately justified?** The appellate record did not permit review of the district court's implicit rejection of Ford's requested conditions. The court therefore vacated and remanded for an express ruling with supporting findings and conclusions. (860–861.)

## Reasoning

The court assumed, without deciding, that Alabama's one-year limitations period barred this suit and that Mississippi's longer period would permit a new action. It rejected an inference that the unexplained denial of summary judgment showed the district court had resolved limitations on erroneous legal grounds: the motions were effectively considered together, with summary judgment denied the day before dismissal. The appellate court did not decide which limitations law ultimately governed. (857–858.)

Authorities differed on whether loss of a limitations defense constituted legal prejudice. The court favored decisions allowing dismissal absent intentional abuse and found support in binding former Fifth Circuit precedent, *Durham*. There, voluntary dismissal could allow a plaintiff to pursue a claim overcoming an advantageous contributory-negligence defense. Here, too, the record showed no bad faith by McCants or counsel. Ford's potential loss of its limitations defense therefore did not itself make dismissal an abuse of discretion. (858–859.)

Ford's substantial litigation expense did not require a different result when dismissal was considered apart from its conditions. Costs or other protections could alleviate the practical prejudice Ford identified. This distinction matters: permissibility of dismissal does not establish that unconditional dismissal properly protects the defendant. (859–860.)

A plaintiff ordinarily cannot dismiss without prejudice after putting a defendant to considerable trial-preparation expense without reimbursing at least some litigation expenses. Recoverable expenses may include reasonable attorney fees; where another similar action is contemplated, a court may limit reimbursement to work that will not be useful in the later case. Nonmonetary conditions may also protect the defendant. These principles did not establish a fixed award or require payment of every dollar Ford requested. (860.)

Ford also asserted that a discovery order would have prevented McCants from presenting experts because she had not supplied ordered information, and asked to preserve that benefit in later litigation. The opinion reports Ford's contention; it does not independently adjudicate the asserted expert sanction or order its automatic transfer. (860.)

The January opposition memorandum was absent from the appellate record. Only Ford's opposition to an earlier, withdrawn dismissal motion supplied some information about requested protections. Although discovery and depositions had generated considerable expense, the appellate court could not determine which work was wasted or reusable. Without an explicit ruling or explanation, it could not assess the district court's exercise of discretion. (860–861.)

## Disposition and assigned-text reconciliation

**Vacated and remanded.** The district court must rule on Ford's request for conditions and state its findings and conclusions. It may hold additional hearings if useful but need not if the existing record suffices. The Eleventh Circuit retained appellate jurisdiction and requested the resulting order within 45 days if the district court's calendar permitted. The decision did not direct dismissal with prejudice, finally award fees, or mandate another evidentiary hearing. (861.)

The assigned textbook passage at PDF page 528 describes McCants as filing in Mississippi. The actual appellate account describes her intention to do so, not a completed refiling. Its description of simultaneous rulings also compresses the actual sequence: summary judgment was denied the day before dismissal. The fee proposition is qualified by the equitable inquiry and potential reuse of work. The sole judicial asterisk identifies district judge Moye's designation; there are no numbered judicial notes or separate opinions.
