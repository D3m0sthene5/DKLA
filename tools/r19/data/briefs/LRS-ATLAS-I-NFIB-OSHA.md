# National Federation of Independent Business v. Department of Labor, OSHA

**595 U.S. 109 (2022) — Supreme Court of the United States — January 13, 2022.** Per curiam; **6–3** stay decision. Justice Gorsuch concurred, joined by Thomas and Alito. Justices Breyer, Sotomayor, and Kagan jointly dissented. The instrument resolves two stay applications, Nos. 21A244 and 21A247. Assigned material: November second casebook, physical PDF pages 29 and 37, printed pages 973 and 981. Pinpoints below use each writing's separately numbered official slip pages.

The Court stayed OSHA's broad COVID-19 vaccination-or-testing emergency standard because the challengers were likely to establish that it exceeded the agency's statutory authority. OSHA could regulate occupational dangers, including particular workplace COVID risks, but the Court viewed this standard as a general public-health measure lacking clear congressional authorization. This was emergency relief pending review, not a final judgment resolving every objection to the regulation. (Per curiam 5–9 & n.*.)

## Regulation and procedural history

The Occupational Safety and Health Act ordinarily required notice, comment, and an opportunity for hearing before promulgating standards. Section 655(c)(1) allowed immediate emergency temporary standards when employees faced grave danger from toxic or physically harmful substances or agents, or new hazards, and emergency protection was necessary. The Court emphasized that this exceptional power had been used nine times before COVID-19; six rules were challenged and only one sustained in full. (Per curiam 2–3.)

After President Biden announced the planned policy in September 2021, OSHA published the emergency standard November 5. It applied to employers with **at least 100 employees**, covering approximately 84.2 million workers. Covered employers ordinarily had to establish a vaccination policy, verify vaccination status, and preserve records. They could instead offer weekly testing and workplace masking for unvaccinated employees; employers were not required to provide that alternative. The Court described testing as at workers' expense and on their own time. Noncompliant unvaccinated employees had to be removed from the workplace, and employers faced substantial penalties. (Per curiam 1, 3–4.)

Exemptions included employees working remotely all the time or exclusively outdoors. The Court considered their practical reach narrow and criticized treating workers in very different settings alike. The dissent stressed additional exemptions and safety variances, as well as the option for employers to select testing and masking instead of compulsory vaccination. These competing descriptions were central to whether the standard was an indiscriminate public-health intervention or a tailored workplace response. (Per curiam 3–4; joint dissent 3.)

States, businesses, trade groups, and nonprofits petitioned regional courts of appeals. The Fifth Circuit stayed the standard before random consolidation in the Sixth Circuit. The Sixth Circuit divided 8–8 over initial en banc hearing, denying that request; a panel then dissolved the Fifth Circuit stay over Judge Larsen's dissent. The Supreme Court consolidated the NFIB and Ohio-led state stay applications and heard expedited argument January 7. (Per curiam 4–5.)

## Likely lack of statutory authority

The applicants challenged the Secretary's statutory authority and raised other objections. The Government characterized COVID exposure as a work-related danger falling within OSHA's assigned mission. The Court agreed that COVID occurred in workplaces, but distinguished occurrence at work from an occupational hazard justifying this broad measure. The virus also spread at homes, schools, sporting events, and other gathering places. Treating all everyday hazards as occupational merely because workers encounter them while employed would greatly expand OSHA's power. (Per curiam 5–7.)

Given the measure's enormous economic and political significance, the Court demanded clear congressional authorization. The Act repeatedly addressed occupational safety, health, and employee dangers, rather than general public-health regulation. Vaccination also differed from conventional workplace rules because its effect could not end when the workday ended. The agency's unprecedented assertion of such broad public-health power over its half-century history reinforced the statutory conclusion. The Court did not accept COVID funding in the American Rescue Plan as authorization for this later vaccination policy and noted the Senate's vote disapproving the regulation. (Per curiam 6–8.)

The Court expressly preserved OSHA's power to address **occupation-specific COVID risks**. Researchers working with the virus and employees in particularly crowded or cramped environments illustrated risks different in degree and kind from ordinary community exposure. Its objection was to the standard's indiscriminate coverage rather than a complete lack of agency authority concerning COVID. The sole judicial footnote also expressly withheld any conclusion on issues the opinion did not address, rebutting the dissent's suggestion that the remaining emergency-standard conditions had been accepted. (Per curiam 7, 8 n.*.)

Applicants asserted billions in unrecoverable costs and large numbers of employee departures. The Government predicted more than 6,500 lives saved and hundreds of thousands of hospitalizations prevented. Those were competing submissions, not independently established Supreme Court findings. The Court considered the equities insufficient to withhold relief and stressed that deciding broad public-health tradeoffs belonged to democratically authorized decisionmakers; OSHA lacked this assigned power. (Per curiam 8–9.)

## Gorsuch's concurrence

Gorsuch framed the problem as who had legal authority to decide, rather than which response best controlled the pandemic. State and local governments possessed general governing powers, while federal powers were enumerated and divided. The major-questions doctrine required a clear delegation for a decision of this scale, and the emergency-standard subsection supplied none. He emphasized historic agency practice, the mismatch between workplace authority and general public-health intervention, and express vaccination provisions elsewhere in federal law. (Gorsuch 1–4.)

He also connected the major-questions doctrine to nondelegation. One prevented agencies from discovering vast legislative powers in unlikely or obscure delegations; the other limited Congress's intentional transfer of legislative power. Both preserved democratic responsibility for important lawmaking. In his view, accepting OSHA's virtually unlimited account of the subsection would likely make it an unconstitutional delegation. That conditional constitutional analysis belonged to the three-Justice concurrence, rather than a Court holding invalidating the statute. (Gorsuch 4–7.)

## Joint dissent

Breyer, Sotomayor, and Kagan would deny a stay. They read the emergency provision as commanding protection when a physically harmful new hazard presented grave workplace danger and the standard was necessary. OSHA's extensive scientific record, reviewed under the substantial-evidence standard, satisfied those conditions. Workplace contact was prolonged and employees often could not avoid colleagues, customers, or patients. A hazard's presence outside work did not strip OSHA of authority to address its workplace effects. (Joint dissent 1–9.)

Existing regulations already covered dangers shared with ordinary life, including fire, unsafe water, noise, and electrical hazards. OSHA also regulated infectious disease and facilitated vaccination. Congress's recent funding for COVID worker protections supported the agency's role. The standard's reach reflected the breadth of the emergency; employers' masking-and-testing option and exemptions further undermined the characterization of a novel compulsory vaccination regime. (Joint dissent 3, 7–10.)

The dissent separately disputed the equities. OSHA considered threatened employee departures exaggerated and found countervailing benefits in retention and fewer sick days. Preventing disease, deaths, and hospitalization outweighed economic costs, particularly amid rapidly increasing cases. Courts lacked the agency's scientific expertise and political accountability and should not displace reasoned workplace protection within its statutory mandate. (Joint dissent 4, 11–14.)

## Exact order and assigned limits

The Court **granted both applications and stayed the emergency standard pending Sixth Circuit review and timely Supreme Court certiorari proceedings**. Denial of certiorari would terminate the order automatically; a grant would continue it until the Supreme Court's judgment was sent down. It did not finally adjudicate all regulatory challenges or prohibit all targeted COVID standards. (Per curiam 9.)

The assignment's separate account correctly describes likelihood of success, although its “more than 100” threshold should be **at least 100**. The generic statutory definition of an occupational standard quoted there comes from §652(8), while §655(b) supplies the ordinary promulgation procedure. The other assigned passage reproduces *West Virginia v. EPA*'s later discussion of this order; that later characterization does not change the emergency posture here. *Biden v. Missouri* and the eviction-moratorium order discussed alongside it concern separate instruments and authority.
