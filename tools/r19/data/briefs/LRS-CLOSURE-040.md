# Action on Safety and Health v. Federal Trade Commission
498 F.2d 757 (D.C. Cir. 1974) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## FTC procedure and the dispute

The FTC's consent-order procedure allowed staff to notify a proposed respondent of an intended complaint and proposed remedy before formal adjudication. If the respondent chose to negotiate within ten days, staff and respondent could attempt settlement. Failure could lead to formal adjudication; success could lead to a provisionally accepted agreement placed on the public record for thirty days. Interested persons could then comment, and the Commission could withdraw acceptance, modify the proposal, or accept it finally. The challenged procedure therefore permitted public comment before final acceptance, although it did not guarantee outside participation in negotiating the agreement. (759.)

On October 12, 1972, the FTC announced an intended complaint alleging Volvo and others had falsely advertised automobile economy and durability. Its proposed order would prohibit those claims without factual substantiation. Volvo elected pre-complaint negotiations. Action on Safety and Health, a consumer-protection organization, sought intervention during those negotiations on November 6. The Commission denied that request and a related request to stay negotiations on December 14. An agreement had been submitted to the Commission November 17. These dates concern submission and refusal of intervention, not final issuance of a decree in November. (759–760 & n. 1.)

ASH filed its district-court complaint January 10, 1973. It sought review of the intervention denial, a declaration that consent procedures were constitutionally defective because of inadequate public participation, and an injunction against using them until revised. The district court dismissed January 29, reasoning that intervention in nonadjudicative negotiations was discretionary and unreviewable and that the available comment procedure did not establish the claimed constitutional violation. It later denied interim relief. The FTC provisionally accepted the agreement March 13 and opened the public-comment period. The appellate court denied a requested stay, and the Commission issued its final order June 26, after comments closed. The underlying advertising allegations were not judicially tried in this appeal. (759–760 & nn. 2–4.)

## Reviewability of pre-complaint intervention

Judge Tamm wrote on April 17, 1974, for the Wright–Tamm–Wyzanski panel; Senior District Judge Wyzanski sat by designation. ASH acknowledged that the FTC Act's express direct-review provision concerned cease-and-desist respondents, but invoked the presumption favoring review despite silence about other actions. The court responded that the APA's presumption remained subject to § 701(a)(2)'s exception for action committed to agency discretion by law. The inquiry concerned this particular intervention decision, rather than an abstract right to review Commission orders generally. (757–761 & nn. 5–8.)

The Act provided for intervention in adjudication, not in these negotiations; the Commission's rules expressly excluded consent negotiations from adjudicative proceedings. Relying on Bristol-Myers, the court reasoned that negotiation was a means of avoiding adjudication altogether, not a trial stage requiring examinations, findings, and conclusions. Congress had left agencies room to design informal settlement procedures. If settlement failed, formal adjudication could then commence; if it succeeded, the public-comment procedure preceded final agency acceptance. (761–762 & nn. 9–12.)

The FTC's general enforcement authority included judgments about violations, the public interest in proceedings, and allocation of limited resources. Neither the Act nor its rules gave ASH a right to intervene in these negotiations. The court held that prescribing that procedure, and granting or denying intervention within it, belonged to agency enforcement discretion and was exempt from review under § 701(a)(2). It distinguished cases involving actual respondents, earlier decrees conferring procedural rights, or orders directly affecting intervenors. It did not convert those different procedural settings into indistinguishable requests to join a pre-complaint negotiation. (762–763 & nn. 13–16.)

## The constitutional claims

ASH separately invoked Fifth Amendment fairness and First Amendment participation. The court addressed those claims rather than assuming that discretionary enforcement insulated every possible constitutional objection. For procedural due process, it required identification of a deprivation of life, liberty, or property before determining what procedures were due. ASH had not identified such an interest with reasonable particularity. A general public desire to participate in enforcement was unlike termination of welfare benefits, deprivation of parental notice, denial of particular housing rights, or revocation of a valuable licensing status. The FTC Act supplied no private action whose disposition this proceeding would determine. These plaintiffs had therefore established no Fifth Amendment entitlement to expanded participation. (763–765 & nn. 17–22.)

The First Amendment claim was inadequately developed. ASH offered a brief reference to meaningful access and the Petition Clause; its cited antitrust case did not establish a public right to join these agency negotiations. The court held that, insofar as the right to petition applied, the Commission had not abridged it by failing to expand participation. It expressly left the precise boundaries of the Petition Clause for a properly clarified and briefed case. This was not a comprehensive holding that petitioning rights never protect agency access. (764–765 & nn. 23–24.)

## Judgment and separate writing

The court **affirmed the dismissal of both the intervention challenge and the broader constitutional claims**. It did not vacate the Volvo consent order, determine whether its advertising was deceptive, or compel revision of the FTC rules. (759, 765.)

Judge J. Skelly Wright concurred in Tamm's opinion, emphasizing that he understood it not to foreclose judicial review in all circumstances of FTC consent orders under § 554(c)(1). His brief separate writing reinforces the distinction between the challenged refusal to admit outsiders to negotiations and every conceivable challenge to a final consent order. There was no dissent. (765.)
