# Sawyer v. Mills
295 S.W.3d 79 (Ky. 2009), modified November 2, 2009 | Contracts

## Court, posture, and outcome
Justice Noble wrote for the Kentucky Supreme Court; all justices were sitting and concurred. The court affirmed the Court of Appeals’ decision sustaining judgment notwithstanding the verdict for Melbourne Mills. A jury had found an enforceable agreement to pay Barbara Sawyer a bonus and awarded its unpaid $900,000 balance, but the trial court set that verdict aside. The Supreme Court agreed that the payment terms brought the agreement within the statute of frauds, that the services invoked as consideration had already been performed, and that neither the secret recording, the checks, nor the claimed reliance supplied an enforceable alternative. It did not decide Sawyer’s request for prejudgment interest. This is an affirmance of the defense judgment, not an affirmance of the jury’s damages. (82–84, 91.)

## Facts and disputed agreement
Sawyer met Mills in 1991 and subsequently helped his practice secure mass-tort clients. In 1994 she asked him to become involved in already-pending breast-implant class litigation and solicited women she knew as clients. He promised a large bonus if one of his class actions produced a big payday. From 1994 through 2001 she performed marketing and research services, worked briefly as a contract employee, and received compensation. In 1997 she suggested and helped publicize the Fen-Phen litigation; about 2,600 claimants responded, including 400 in Kentucky, and she assisted with the office work that followed. The opinion also describes earlier financial benefits, including advertising payments, a studio payment, and vacations. Those earlier benefits should not be confused with the later installments of the disputed bonus. (82–83 & n.1.)

After the Fen-Phen settlement in May 2001 and receipt of fees in June, Mills distributed substantial bonuses to several employees. Sawyer initially received approximately $1,300, equal to two weeks of hourly compensation. On June 25 she and her husband invited him to their art studio and secretly recorded their conversation. She proposed a $1,065,000 bonus: $1 million plus $65,000 for a car. Although Mills had the financial ability to pay immediately, he rejected a lump sum and agreed to monthly payments of $10,000, expressing concern that other employees would learn of the larger payment. A lawyer later prepared a written memorialization, but Mills never signed it. The tape’s authenticity and the speakers’ identities were not the central defect; the question was whether the resulting promise was legally enforceable. (82–83, 85, 88.)

His periodic payments, including the initial $10,000 on June 25, totaled $65,000; he also made a separate $100,000 payment in October. The last payment was in February 2002. Sawyer’s employment ended on March 31, 2002; she had received $165,000 of the proposed bonus. The work said to justify that bonus had been completed before the June 25 conversation. Her later hourly employment was separately compensated. These facts mattered independently to the consideration and reliance issues. (83, 86–87, 90–91.)

## Proceedings and standard of review
The trial court denied summary judgment so the evidence could be developed at trial. After four days, the jury found an agreement, found that it could be completed within one year, and awarded the $900,000 unpaid balance. The court nonetheless granted Mills’s motion for judgment notwithstanding the verdict under Kentucky’s statute of frauds, and the Court of Appeals affirmed. The Supreme Court granted discretionary review. It described the demanding standard for setting aside a verdict: the evidence must be construed favorably to the successful party, and the court must determine whether a reasonable person could find for that party. The court nevertheless concluded that the agreed terms, rather than Mills’s abstract capacity to pay earlier, controlled the one-year inquiry. (83–85.)

## One-year provision and completed services
KRS 371.010(7) requires a signed writing for an agreement not to be performed within one year. Kentucky decisions recognize that an agreement capable of performance within a year may fall outside that provision, but Sawyer could not replace the parties’ actual arrangement with an imagined lump-sum payment. The conversation repeatedly contemplated installments over approximately ten years, and Mills specifically refused immediate payment. The proposed written agreement used 107 monthly installments, with the final installment adjusted to the balance; the opinion’s conversational references to ten years do not establish 120 payments of $10,000. The court looked to the contract’s terms and what the parties contemplated, not to the payer’s financial ability to adopt different terms. On that evidence the jury’s contrary special finding could not save the oral agreement. (84–86.)

Sawyer also invoked the exception associated with full performance by one party. The court answered that her relevant services preceded the June promise: they were not rendered in performance of that agreement and could not constitute consideration for it. Cases involving a promise accompanied by future requested services were therefore distinguishable. Her continued employment did not establish performance of the bonus agreement because that work was compensated separately and the promised bonus was not shown to depend on continued employment. The court distinguished an agreement whose consideration and essential written terms were already supplied from this promise for completed services. Thus the past-services problem was an additional ground, not merely another description of the statute-of-frauds defect. (86–87, 91.)

## Recording, electronic signature, and checks
The federal E-SIGN Act did not make the secretly recorded conversation a signed agreement. The tape qualified as an electronic record under 15 U.S.C. § 7006(4), but an electronic signature under § 7006(5) requires a sound, symbol, or process executed or adopted with intent to sign the record. Identifying Mills’s voice established who spoke; it did not establish his intent to execute or adopt a recording he did not know existed. The parties’ subsequent plan to prepare a written memorialization, which he refused to sign, reinforced that conclusion. The court’s forgery analogy addressed the absence of adoption; it did not adjudicate a criminal charge against Sawyer. Nor did the decision categorically prohibit a recording that the parties knowingly agree will embody their contract. The defect was lack of signing intent, rather than rejection solely because the medium was electronic. (87–89.)

An oral settlement entered under oath in a judicial proceeding did not establish a general exception for this private conversation. The court distinguished the judicial stipulation, estoppel, and administration-of-justice considerations in Calloway from a secretly recorded studio meeting. The signed payment checks also did not cure the problem. Decisions accepting checks as sufficient memoranda involved references to contract terms or other connected writings. Here the checks were as consistent with a gratuitous bonus as with an exchange, and the unsigned secret recording did not supply an adopted repository of terms to which they could be joined. Two insufficient components did not together provide the requisite signed memorandum. (88–89.)

## Promissory estoppel and reserved issues
The parties disputed preservation of promissory estoppel, and Kentucky precedent left uncertain whether that doctrine could defeat the statute of frauds. The court explained the distinction between promissory estoppel and equitable estoppel based on fraud; it treated the broader language in Rickert as dicta and discussed later limitations. Footnotes 3 and 4 further explain the doctrine’s recognition and the different approaches taken to unconscionable injury or injustice. But the court did not resolve a universal estoppel exception. Even assuming preservation and an available exception, Sawyer lacked sufficient proof for an instruction. (89–90 & nn.3–4.)

A promise must induce action or forbearance that the promisor should reasonably expect. Sawyer asserted that she stayed in the job and declined other opportunities because of the bonus, but the record did not substantiate those assertions beyond her own account. There was no showing that Mills conditioned payment on continued employment or asked her to decline other work. She received both the $165,000 and hourly compensation during the roughly nine additional months. The court concluded that he could not reasonably be expected to understand those circumstances as the alleged detrimental reliance. It affirmed on this record without deciding that reliance can never overcome the one-year provision. (90–91.)

## Assigned account and source scope
The casebook uses Sawyer as a short illustration of an oral bonus payable over ten years. The full opinion specifies $1,065,000 and distinguishes the installment terms, past consideration, signing intent, and deficient reliance evidence. All four judicial notes and the entire original report were checked. The report’s reference on page 90 to employment continuing through March 2003 conflicts with its March 31, 2002 termination chronology and its description of nine additional months; this brief follows that consistent chronology without silently treating the isolated date as an additional year of employment. This account describes the 2009 decision and does not certify current Kentucky law. Separate source review is recorded in the accompanying register.
