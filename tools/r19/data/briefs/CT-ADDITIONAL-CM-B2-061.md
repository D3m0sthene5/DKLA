# Jewish Center of Sussex County v. Whale — Chancery Division
165 N.J. Super. 84, 397 A.2d 712 (Ch. Div. 1978) | Contracts

## Decision and procedural setting
Judge Polow of the New Jersey Superior Court, Chancery Division, granted the congregation summary judgment rescinding its rabbi's employment contract and dismissing his counterclaims for specific performance and compensatory and punitive damages. The reported written opinion dated December 8, 1978 amplifies an oral decision rendered September 15, 1978, as the original report's caption footnote expressly explains. The complete written amplification is the judicial source for this brief; the earlier oral transcript is not reproduced in it. (84 & caption note, 86, 92.)

## Facts and competing positions
The Jewish Center of Sussex County conducted synagogue services and a part-time religious school in Newton. Responding to its advertisement for a rabbi, Chaim Whale submitted a short resume describing his degree, ordination in 1956, and rabbinical experience through 1977. It offered references on request, but the congregation did not request them. He interviewed, conducted Friday evening and Saturday morning services, and met members. Further telephone negotiations produced a January 1, 1978 employment agreement providing salary, a house without rent, utilities, and moving expenses. The relevant facts were stipulated. (86–87.)

In April the congregation learned that Whale had also been known as Louis R. Wolfish, had been convicted of a mail-fraud scheme involving $200,000, and had been disbarred in New York for bribing a police officer. He admitted these background facts. Its April 17 letter sought rescission. Its motion asked the court to end the employment contract and dismiss Whale's contractual counterclaims. The trial judge framed the decisive issue as whether the applicant had an affirmative duty to reveal the conviction and disbarment, and whether withholding them constituted equitable fraud. (86–87.)

Whale argued that fraud required a factual inquiry into his intent and that the existence of a disclosure duty itself required trial. He also disputed prejudice, damage, or an unconscientious advantage: he was ordained and capable of performing the promised services. Because the omitted matters were public records and the congregation could have investigated or requested references, he argued that its own opportunity to discover them defeated any duty to volunteer information. His easement authorities discussed equal access to information and estoppel by silence. (87–88, 89–90.)

## Rule and reasoning
The court treated the employment arrangement as a civil contract subject to rescission for fraud. It distinguished legal fraud, ordinarily involving a knowingly false material representation made to induce detrimental reliance, from equitable fraud, which does not require knowledge of falsity. An affirmative lie was not indispensable: withholding material truth could constitute fraud when conscience and the circumstances imposed a duty to speak. The issue was not whether every applicant must disclose every unfavorable fact, but whether these facts were material to this spiritual and educational leadership relationship. (88–89.)

The judge rejected the public-record and failure-to-investigate defenses. The easement cases did not directly decide a rabbinical employment contract; even their discussion recognized that common honesty and fair dealing could require disclosure. The congregation's reliance on a sparse resume did not excuse misleading concealment of obviously material facts. Drawing an analogy to relationships requiring utmost good faith, the court emphasized the contract's express expectation of spiritual leadership within the congregation and the broader community. These were the court's historical judgments about this particular relationship, rather than a determination of religious doctrine. (88, 89–91.)

Whale's capacity to conduct services did not establish that the congregation lacked legally relevant prejudice. The judge relied on authority that a fraudulently procured document could itself support a presumption of damage. He also regarded the concealed conviction and disbarment as probable sources of embarrassment and humiliation inconsistent with the congregation's choice of spiritual leader. The opinion analogized to the employment of a teacher at a religious university, quoting another court's treatment of concealed background information. That comparison supported this court's materiality analysis; it was not a separate damages award to the congregation. (90–92.)

The court accordingly held, as a matter of law, that an applicant for a rabbinical position involving spiritual, religious, and educational leadership had to disclose this criminal record and disbarment. It concluded that withholding them prejudiced the congregation and conferred an undue advantage on the applicant, leaving no genuine material factual issue requiring trial. That broad nondisclosure rationale is important to this trial stage. The later Supreme Court affirmance resolved the case on the narrower ground of affirmative resume misrepresentation and expressly declined to decide the breadth of this duty. (92; distinct subsequent decision, 86 N.J. 619, 624, 627.)

## Housing and exact result
Whale also invoked statutory tenant protections. The court rejected contractual housing benefits once the underlying employment agreement was void and pointed to the statute permitting removal where tenancy was conditioned on employment by the landlord. Its announced judgment granted summary judgment rescinding the employment agreement and dismissing the counterclaims for specific performance and damages. It did not award the congregation compensatory or punitive damages, or set a monetary recovery. (92.)
