# Fashion Page, Ltd. v. Zurich Insurance Co.

**50 N.Y.2d 265, 428 N.Y.S.2d 890, 406 N.E.2d 747 (1980) — April 29, 1980. Civil Procedure.** Judge Wachtler wrote the Court of Appeals majority joined by Chief Judge Cooke and Judges Jasen, Jones, Fuchsberg, and Meyer. Judge Gabrielli concurred in the result separately: all seven agreed to affirm, but only six joined the majority reasoning. Assigned account: CivPro, physical page 157, printed pages 265–266, Note 2 before the foreign-service subsection.

## Facts and service dispute

Fashion Page sought recovery under a fire insurance policy issued by Zurich, a foreign corporation authorized to conduct business in New York. The complaint alleged that an insured building burned on December 30, 1975, and that Zurich refused or failed to pay a claim exceeding $225,000. The Court of Appeals did not decide coverage or the amount owed. Its issue concerned how the action was commenced. [269.]

On December 13, 1976, the process server went to Zurich’s William Street offices in Manhattan and told its receptionist that he had a summons and complaint for Zurich and needed to know who handled them. She directed him down a corridor to Ann Robertson. After he identified his purpose, Robertson examined the papers and accepted them. He expressly asked whether she was authorized to accept service, and she said she could take them. Zurich moved to dismiss later that month, asserting that she was only the vice-president’s secretary and fell outside the statutory categories of proper recipients. [269–270.]

Robertson was the executive secretary to Joseph Scanlon, a director and the vice-president responsible for Zurich’s New York office. The office had over one hundred employees, but she was its only executive secretary. She denied being an officer or managing agent and denied express authorization to accept process. Her actual practice supplied a different picture: for at least five years she regularly accepted summonses when Scanlon was absent, taking approximately half of those brought to his office. In this instance she told Scanlon about the papers when he returned from lunch and, at his direction, sent them to the legal department. Scanlon confirmed this established practice. The receptionist likewise testified that she directed office visitors to the appropriate person. [270.]

## Procedural history and competing grounds

A Special Referee heard the evidence. Although Zurich’s witnesses disputed some of the process server’s account, the Referee credited his version of the delivery. The Referee found that the server acted diligently and used a method calculated to provide—and actually providing—fair notice. He also considered Robertson’s actual duties sufficient to make her a managing or general agent for service despite her title. New York Supreme Court, the trial court in this proceeding, confirmed the report and denied dismissal. The Appellate Division affirmed on the ground that Robertson was an agent appointed to receive process and allowed an appeal on a certified question. [270–271.]

Historical CPLR 311(1) permitted delivery to a corporate officer, director, managing or general agent, cashier or assistant cashier, or another agent authorized by appointment or law to receive service. Zurich argued that Robertson lacked the judgment and discretion characteristic of a managing agent. It also contended that appointment required express authority and that an appointed agent had to be formally designated under CPLR 318 through a written, acknowledged, filed instrument. The Court of Appeals rejected that narrow construction; the majority did not need to establish that every executive secretary is a managing agent. [271–272.]

## Majority holding and explanation

The statute aimed to give the corporation notice that litigation had commenced and required a liberal construction. Its history had expanded the kinds of corporate recipients rather than confined service to a small collection of titles. A corporation could itself select a person to accept process, without tying that selection to the person’s rank. Appointment under CPLR 311 was broader than formal designation under CPLR 318. The latter was an optional procedure with additional legal consequences; its formalities did not govern every appointment to accept process. [271–272.]

That distinction connected statutory interpretation to everyday office arrangements. A corporation could establish procedures in which a receptionist directed process servers to a secretary who forwarded legal papers internally. It could not freely use those procedures and then escape their consequences by insisting that the recipient lacked the right title or a filed designation. Process servers generally could not know the company’s private allocation of responsibilities. They could appropriately ask company employees who received process and follow their directions when the surrounding circumstances made that reliance reasonable. The majority therefore sustained service assessed objectively as calculated to provide fair corporate notice. [272–273.]

The rule was qualified, rather than a license to hand papers to anyone available. Delivery to a building receptionist who was not the defendant’s employee, without inquiry into her relationship to the company, would fail even if she later forwarded the papers correctly. Nor was every employee’s claim to represent the corporation a reasonable basis for service; the opinion gave the example of an employee temporarily visiting the state. A server’s prior experience or the current circumstances might reveal that an employee was improperly assuming authority. Thus later actual notice alone did not cure careless delivery to an improper recipient. [273.]

Here the server went to Zurich’s own offices, announced the purpose of his visit, asked the receptionist whom to see, followed her directions, and obtained Robertson’s confirmation that she could accept the papers. The receptionist’s role and Robertson’s acceptance reinforced each other. Both were longstanding employees of an insurer regularly involved in litigation. The credited facts supported reasonable reliance on an established office practice. If those employees nevertheless directed the server to an internally inappropriate recipient, the majority assigned that error to Zurich rather than to the server who had made the expected inquiries. [273–274.]

## Gabrielli’s concurrence: the same result on a narrower basis

Judge Gabrielli agreed that Robertson had been impliedly appointed to receive process, which was enough to sustain service under CPLR 311. He objected to the majority’s broader emphasis on the reasonableness or diligence of the server. In his view, the legislature had prescribed eligible recipients; delivery to such a recipient was ordinarily essential, regardless of how earnestly a server tried. Judicial willingness to revive failed service merely because the attempt was reasonable could encourage sloppy practices and additional litigation. Any broad change making diligence itself decisive belonged to the legislature. [274–276.]

His position did not eliminate all exceptions. A company that affirmatively obstructed service or misled the server through an agent with actual or apparent authority might be unable to insist on perfect compliance. But such an exception would arise from misconduct or estoppel against the recipient, not the server’s good faith alone. No expanded doctrine was necessary here because Robertson’s years of accepted practice already established implied appointment. The concurrence therefore joined the judgment while rejecting what it regarded as unnecessary expansion of the test. [274–276.]

## Judgment and relationship to the assignment

The Court of Appeals **affirmed the Appellate Division’s order, with costs, and answered the certified question affirmatively**. The service challenge failed; the court did not award policy proceeds or determine the merits of the fire claim. Neither opinion contains judicial footnotes or an appendix in the complete preserved report. [276.]

The textbook’s account describes Robertson’s established acceptance practice and quotes the majority’s explanation of corporate routing procedures. Its attribution of the agent-by-appointment formulation to the Court of Appeals compresses the procedural distinctions: the full report expressly identifies that formulation as the Appellate Division’s ground, while Gabrielli adopts implied appointment as his sufficient basis and the majority develops a broader reasonable-reliance analysis. A useful comparison with Hellenic Challenger should preserve those differences: the cases involve different procedural rules, and Gabrielli’s concurrence makes statutory agency an explicit limiting concern.
