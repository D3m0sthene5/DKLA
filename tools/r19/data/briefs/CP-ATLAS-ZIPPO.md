# Zippo Manufacturing Co. v. Zippo Dot Com, Inc.

**Citation and court:** 952 F. Supp. 1119 (W.D. Pa. 1997), Civil Action No. 96–397 Erie, decided January 16, 1997. District Judge McLaughlin issued this memorandum opinion deciding jurisdiction and venue objections, with seven footnotes and no separate writing. It does not adjudicate infringement or award damages.

**Assigned reading:** Civil Procedure casebook, physical PDF page 125, printed pages 200–201: the complete own-caption editorial account and quoted Internet-activity spectrum. The full reporter opinion, preserved through the Caselaw Access Project, supplies the facts, actual application, and separate venue holding. All seven judicial footnotes were read. Pinpoints below refer to the Federal Supplement.

**Facts and claims.** Zippo Manufacturing, a Pennsylvania corporation headquartered in Bradford, made the familiar Zippo lighters. Zippo Dot Com was a California corporation based in Sunnyvale. It operated a website and Internet news service and held the domain names zippo.com, zippo.net, and zipponews.com. Its offices, employees, and servers were in California; it maintained no Pennsylvania offices, employees, or agents. The two businesses nevertheless encountered each other through the name that Dot Com used online. Manufacturing alleged federal trademark dilution, infringement, and false designation, state trademark dilution, and entitlement to an equitable accounting and constructive trust. 952 F. Supp. at 1121–1122.

Dot Com offered a free public level and two paid subscription levels. A prospective paying subscriber completed an online application providing name and address, paid by credit card over the Internet or telephone, and received a password for viewing or downloading messages stored on Dot Com's California server. Approximately 3,000 of its roughly 140,000 paying subscribers were Pennsylvania residents, described by the court as approximately two percent of the total. Dot Com also contracted with seven Pennsylvania Internet access providers to supply the service to their subscribers; two providers were in the Western District. Id. at 1121.

The challenged name appeared in the domains, website, and identifying headers of messages posted by subscribers. When a subscriber viewed or downloaded a message, its identifying information included Zippo; some messages contained adult sexual material. Note 4 reproduces a sample header to explain the mechanism. These allegations matter because the forum contacts were transmissions of the very material said to infringe or dilute Manufacturing's mark, rather than business activity unrelated to the claim. Id. at 1121–1122 & n.4.

**Procedure and competing positions.** Dot Com moved to dismiss under Rules 12(b)(2) and 12(b)(3), alleging lack of personal jurisdiction and improper venue, or alternatively to transfer under § 1406(a). Manufacturing bore the burden of making a prima facie showing of sufficient forum contacts. It expressly relied on specific jurisdiction; it did not claim that Dot Com was subject to general jurisdiction in Pennsylvania. Id. at 1121–1122.

Dot Com characterized its conduct as operating a website or advertising, emphasized that Pennsylvania users initiated the relationships, and called the resulting contacts fortuitous. It also relied on the small percentage of Pennsylvania subscribers and argued that defending there would be unreasonable. On venue, it contended that the circuit's Cottman decision permitted a trademark suit only where a substantial part of the underlying events occurred. The court rejected each argument and denied the motion. Id. at 1126–1128.

**Issue and holding.** Could Pennsylvania exercise specific personal jurisdiction over an out-of-state Internet service selling continuing access to thousands of Pennsylvania subscribers, where the allegedly infringing messages were transmitted into that state? Yes. Dot Com knowingly conducted commercial transactions with forum residents, the claims arose from those transactions, and jurisdiction was reasonable. Venue was also proper under the then-applicable corporate-residence provisions of § 1391. Id. at 1122–1123, 1126–1128.

**Statutory and constitutional framework.** Pennsylvania's long-arm statute expressly reached contracting to supply services in the Commonwealth and also extended to the constitutional limit. The subscription and access-provider contracts satisfied the service provision. The court then asked whether Dot Com had purposefully established minimum contacts, whether the claim arose from those contacts, and whether exercising jurisdiction comported with fair play and substantial justice. It assessed the quality of the defendant's own connection to the forum, rather than mere foreseeability that someone there might see a website. Id. at 1122–1123.

Physical entry was unnecessary because an enterprise could create continuing interstate commercial obligations through communications. Conversely, contacts generated solely through someone else's unilateral activity would not establish purposeful availment. The reasonableness inquiry considered the defendant's burden, Pennsylvania's adjudicatory interest, Manufacturing's interest in effective relief, judicial efficiency, and shared policy interests. Id. at 1123.

**The Internet spectrum.** The court organized the early Internet cases by the nature and quality of online commercial activity. At one end, businesses enter contracts involving knowing, repeated transmission of computer files into the forum. At the other, a passive website simply makes information available. Between them, an interactive website permits exchanges with the host; jurisdiction depends on the degree of interactivity and the commercial character of the exchange. This framework applied existing jurisdiction principles to new conduct rather than treating cyberspace as exempt from them. Id. at 1123–1124.

The examples also limited what the court needed to decide. It described Inset's advertisement and toll-free-number rationale as an outer limit, contrasted Bensusan's information-only club website, and discussed Maritz's solicitation of users for a future service. Pres-Kap involved the customer of an online service, whose contracts were negotiated and serviced in New York, rather than a seller deliberately conducting business with forum residents. Zippo's own dispute fell at the commercial end; it did not require resolution of whether accessible advertising or interactivity alone was sufficient. Id. at 1124–1126. Footnotes 1–3 explain domain names, websites, and registration; notes 5–6 identify contemporary materials and explain shareware. Those descriptions belong to the opinion's 1997 technological setting.

**Why Dot Com's contacts were purposeful.** Dot Com repeatedly chose to accept Pennsylvania applications, issue passwords, and transmit files there. It controlled those transactions and could have declined to sell to Pennsylvania residents. The users' initial approach did not make the resulting deliberately accepted commercial relationships accidental. The court contrasted those relationships with a hypothetical Ohio subscriber forwarding a file into Pennsylvania or traveling there with a computer—contacts created by the subscriber instead of the seller. Id. at 1126–1127.

Neither the absence of a physical office nor the small fraction of worldwide subscriptions defeated that conclusion. The question was the character of the contacts, and even a single purposeful contact could sometimes suffice. Note 7 further explained that Dot Com's advertising authorities concerned continuous and systematic contacts for general jurisdiction, whereas this dispute involved specific jurisdiction tied to actual service sales. Id. at 1126–1127 & n.7.

**Connection to the claim and fairness.** The transactions aimed at delivering into Pennsylvania the messages bearing the challenged identifier. The alleged infringement and dilution therefore occurred through the forum-related service itself. Manufacturing's Pennsylvania location also supported the likelihood of injury there, but the court did not rely only on the plaintiff's residence: it identified the transmissions and their allegedly infringing content. Id. at 1127.

Pennsylvania had a strong interest in alleged infringement of a resident corporation's trademark, and Manufacturing chose to seek relief there. Those interests outweighed the defense burden given Dot Com's deliberate pursuit of Pennsylvania business. Interstate obligations voluntarily undertaken could not be avoided simply by pointing to the distant server and California location. Id.

**Venue and exact disposition.** Under the version of § 1391 applied in this case, a corporation was deemed to reside where it was subject to personal jurisdiction. Dot Com was the sole defendant, so the court's jurisdictional conclusion supplied a venue basis under § 1391(b)(1). Cottman had addressed the alternative events-based provision, § 1391(b)(2), on the arguments presented there; it did not eliminate the residence provision for all trademark cases. Because venue was proper, the court denied dismissal or compulsory transfer under the asserted § 1406(a) route. The motion was denied, and the underlying trademark dispute remained for adjudication. Id. at 1128.

**Course significance and limits.** The memorable spectrum should be connected to Zippo's concrete holding: repeated sales and delivery of the challenged material into the forum, with a claim arising from that business. The court expressly avoided deciding that every interactive site creates jurisdiction. It also decided a distinct statutory venue question. This brief states the 1997 district-court rules and the statute then applied; it does not certify that the spectrum or that statutory wording supplies the governing answer to every later Internet dispute.
