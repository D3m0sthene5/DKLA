# Foakes v. Beer
(1884) 9 App. Cas. 605 (H.L.) | Contracts

## Court, question, and result

The House of Lords decided the appeal on May 16, 1884, after argument on March 31 and April 1. The Earl of Selborne, Lord Blackburn, Lord Watson, and Lord Fitzgerald all supported affirmance. Blackburn expressed substantial disagreement with the traditional rule but ultimately concurred; his speech is not a dissent. The operative result affirmed the order appealed from and dismissed Foakes's appeal with costs. The question was whether payment of money already due on a judgment supplied consideration for an unsealed agreement that, on one construction, conditionally relinquished the judgment creditor's interest claim. All four Lords concluded that the claimed concession could not defeat Beer's recovery. They divided over whether the instrument had promised to relinquish interest at all. (605, 610–614, 623–630; report reproduction physical page 12, final order.)

## Judgment, agreement, and litigation

On August 11, 1875, Julia Beer obtained judgment against John Weston Foakes for £2,077 17s. 2d. in debt and £13 1s. 10d. in costs, totaling £2,090 19s. The judgment carried statutory interest at four percent under section 17 of 1 & 2 Vict. c. 110. Fitzgerald calculated that £113 16s. 2d. had accrued when the later agreement was made; the interest was a statutory incident of the judgment, not merely damages that a jury might award at its discretion. (605–606, 624–625.)

The parties signed a memorandum on December 21, 1876. It recited the judgment and Foakes's request for time. Beer acknowledged £500 paid immediately in part satisfaction and undertook not to take proceedings on the judgment on condition that Foakes pay £150 on each July 1 and January 1, or within one calendar month thereafter, beginning the following July, until the stated £2,090 19s. was fully paid. Payment could be made to Beer or her designated successors or nominee. The memorandum was not under seal. Importantly, the condition did not constitute a new promise by Foakes to pay installments: the obligation expressed was Beer's conditional undertaking to refrain from proceedings. Foakes already owed the entire judgment immediately, and he furnished no additional security or negotiable instrument. (605–606, 611, 624–626.)

Beer sought leave to proceed on the judgment in June 1882. An issue was directed concerning whether any amount remained due on July 1, 1882. At trial before Cave J., the principal and costs were proved fully paid, and the jury found that the payments complied with the agreement's amounts and times. Cave concluded that the agreement prevented execution for any remaining sum. The Queen's Bench Division, consisting of Watkin Williams and Mathew JJ., discharged an order for a new trial based on misdirection. The Court of Appeal—Brett M.R., Lindley and Fry L.JJ.—reversed and entered judgment for Beer for the interest due, with costs. Foakes appealed from that order. This was therefore not a dispute over whether he had actually complied with the installment condition; the payment finding stood, while the legal effect of that performance was disputed. (605–606.)

## Competing arguments

Foakes's counsel argued that prompt recovery of part of a debt could benefit a creditor more than insisting on the whole, particularly if insistence produced delay or bankruptcy. The law accepted genuinely different performances, including negotiable paper, as satisfaction even when their face value was smaller. Counsel criticized a rule that recognized a trivial different object as consideration but refused to recognize commercially valuable prompt money. He also questioned the precedential foundations of Pinnel's Case and Cumber v. Wane and invoked payment to a nominee. These were arguments for enforceability, rather than findings that Beer actually faced insolvency or received a new security. (606–608.)

Beer's counsel argued that the agreement gave time without forgiving interest and, alternatively, that any purported forgiveness lacked consideration. Foakes remained bound by an existing legal duty, and the longstanding authorities denied that payment of less money in the same manner discharged the greater liquidated liability. Counsel also appealed to settled practice and the analogous treatment of promises to pay more for an existing duty. Foakes's reply distinguished some authorities as cases of incomplete satisfaction or defective pleading. The Lords' speeches addressed both construction and consideration; the result should not be reduced to a unanimous construction that expressly preserved interest. (608–610.)

## Selborne: conditional waiver without consideration

Selborne agreed with the lower courts' construction of the instrument as a conditional waiver of interest. Its operative provision referred to the specific sum of £2,090 19s. and promised no proceedings whatever after the stipulated payments. Reading that sum as including accrued and future interest would insert material words that were absent and would cut down the operative promise. Although he doubted whether Beer actually had the interest waiver in mind, he concluded that the words could have that effect if the agreement was enforceable. (610–611.)

It was not enforceable on that construction. The £500 was a part payment of an already due debt. The future payments represented delayed satisfaction of another portion of the same obligation, permitted by Beer's indulgence. They did not supply a new detriment or independent benefit. Nor did subsequent compliance itself create an accord and satisfaction that had been absent at formation. Satisfaction could not be complete while installments remained payable, and Beer did nothing upon the last payment that independently amounted to a binding acquittance if the original agreement had not bound her. (611–612.)

Selborne retained the principle associated with Pinnel's Case that a lesser sum paid when due, in the same manner, cannot alone satisfy the greater money liability. He distinguished a composition involving several creditors, a sealed release, and some new performance or security different from money paid toward the original debt. He acknowledged that Cumber might have disregarded distinctions concerning negotiable paper without thereby disproving the underlying principle. The possibility that quick collection would be more convenient, or would avoid a debtor's future insolvency, was not the independent benefit recognized by the rule. (612–614.)

He recognized that the boundary between an unsealed agreement and a deed could appear arbitrary. He even thought the law might be improved by giving binding effect to certain written concessions without a seal. But that proposed improvement could not be accomplished by treating ordinary part payment of an existing monetary obligation as newly supplied consideration. He moved to affirm and dismiss with costs. (613–614.)

## Blackburn: serious reservations, ultimate concurrence

Blackburn likewise construed the undertaking as conditionally relinquishing interest. The language could not fairly be rewritten to require interest in addition to the stated sum or to reserve proceedings solely for interest. He noted the contrast between £500 in money and a horse or promissory note of the same value. That contrast prompted a lengthy examination of the authorities rather than an application of modern commercial-benefit doctrine. (614–616.)

His account of Pinnel separated its actual pleading issue and its recognition of beneficial early payment from the broader dictum about money paid when due. A genuinely different performance could have a possible advantage without courts weighing the adequacy of its value. But he questioned the factual premise that prompt part payment of a money debt could never benefit a creditor. He also examined the history of sham pleas involving hats or wine, the artificial device of treating money as paid in full and partly returned, and the actual pleading record in Cumber. Some cited decisions rested on incomplete accord, conditional acceptance, or other features that weakened their support for the categorical rule. He accepted that first-instance judges would nevertheless be constrained by the established line of authority. (616–622; reproduction reference note 79 identifies the Cumber plea roll.)

Blackburn thought the issue remained open to reconsideration in the House. In ordinary business, prompt recovery of a part might be preferable even from a solvent debtor, and more so where credit was doubtful. He had prepared reasons supporting that view, but the other Lords had not found them satisfactory. He expressly declined to persist in them and assented to the proposed judgment, although it was not the result he had originally considered proper. The assigned account's description of him as a concurring judge with reservations is accurate. His commercial criticism does not change the unanimous disposition into a dissenting vote or create an exception adopted by the House. (617–623.)

## Watson and Fitzgerald: a different construction, the same result

Watson understood the stated sum as the judgment debt bearing interest by law. In context, Beer had agreed to give time, not to abate any portion of her rights. Nonetheless, assuming the conditional release construction preferred by Selborne and Blackburn, Foakes had supplied no legal consideration for it. Watson declined to disturb the established doctrine and adopted the relevant reasoning of Selborne and Fitzgerald. (623–624.)

Fitzgerald developed the construction argument further. The recitals concerned time alone; Foakes had requested no remission. His solicitor had prepared the agreement, Beer's solicitor approved it, and interest had not been mentioned in their negotiations. The general undertaking should be read in light of the more specific recitals. He also rejected the lower-court suggestion that a promise to pay a nominee, or a new written security, supplied consideration: Foakes undertook no such new obligation. (624–627.)

On the alternative assumption that Beer had promised to forgo interest, Fitzgerald still found no consideration. Although the principal and costs had ultimately been paid to the last farthing, that performance did not furnish an additional legal advantage supporting relinquishment of the interest balance. He acknowledged Blackburn's criticism of Pinnel and the artificiality of certain distinctions. Nevertheless, the rule had long been received as law, and he considered it inappropriate to overturn it in this case. His references to approximately £350 of interest explain the practical stakes; the House's final order does not itself fix a new award of that amount. (627–630.)
