Des Moines Navigation v. Iowa Homestead
Des Moines Navigation & Railroad Co. v. Iowa Homestead Co.
Citation
Des Moines Navigation & Railroad Co. v. Iowa Homestead Co., 123 U.S. 552, 8 S. Ct. 217, 31 L. Ed. 202
(1887). Supreme Court of the United States; argued November 1, 1887; decided December 5, 1887. Chief
Justice Waite delivered the Court’s opinion. The report contains no separate opinion. Casebook pages 74-76.
The exact dates come from the full report. Full judicial opinion.
Parties
Iowa Homestead Company sought reimbursement from Des Moines Navigation and Railroad Company for
taxes it had paid. Homestead was the plaintiff in the present state-court action; Navigation and Railroad was
the defendant asserting a prior federal decree as a complete defense and seeking Supreme Court review.
Both companies were Iowa corporations. Other defendants in the earlier litigation, including the New York
citizens who obtained removal, are relevant to the prior federal court’s jurisdiction but were not the opposing
parties in this tax-recovery dispute. 123 U.S. at 553, 556.
Material Facts
Homestead sought to recover taxes for 1864 through 1871 that had already been included in litigation
between these same companies. Navigation and Railroad had obtained a decree in its favor in that earlier
litigation. In the later suit, Homestead argued that the earlier decree was void because the federal courts
lacked subject-matter jurisdiction, meaning authority over the category of controversy before them. The
asserted defect was that Homestead and Navigation and Railroad were citizens of the same state. 123 U.S. at
553, 556.
The earlier action involved separate controversies over tracts claimed individually by several defendants.
Three New York defendants filed the affidavit and petition required by the 1867 removal statute, which
allowed specified out-of-state parties alleging local prejudice to transfer litigation from state to federal court.
The whole action was transferred. All parties appeared, answered, and contested the merits without objecting
to federal jurisdiction; the case ultimately reached the Supreme Court on appeal and was decided. Id. at 556,
558-59.
Procedural History
In the present Iowa action, Navigation and Railroad pleaded the earlier decree as a bar. Homestead
responded that the decree was a nullity for lack of jurisdiction. The state supreme court entered a decree for
Homestead for the taxes and interest without giving the earlier decree the conclusive effect Navigation and
Railroad claimed. The federal Supreme Court reviewed that state judgment by writ of error, the
then-applicable means of appellate review. 123 U.S. at 553-56.
The full opinion, in a passage omitted from the casebook excerpt, also resolves whether the
prior-adjudication defense had been properly presented in the Iowa Supreme Court. That court declined to
consider it because of how it appeared in briefs for companion cases. The Supreme Court found the federal
claim fairly presented in the pleadings, proof, assignments of error, and argument, and treated the refusal to
decide it as a denial of the federal right claimed. Id. at 554-56.

Issue
Was the earlier federal decree automatically void, and therefore open to attack in a later suit between the
same parties over the same taxes, because the record disclosed that one defendant shared the plaintiff’s state
citizenship? Or did it remain binding unless reversed or otherwise judicially set aside, even if the original
exercise of jurisdiction had been erroneous?
Rule and Holding
The decree remained binding and barred the later action. In the circumstances presented, the federal circuit
court had authority to decide whether the suit was removable, in whole or in part. An erroneous decision to
retain the suit or adjudicate the controversy between the Iowa parties would have been a ground for reversal
on direct review, but did not make the resulting decree a nullity. The Supreme Court’s prior merits
adjudication therefore remained effective until judicially annulled, vacated, or set aside. 123 U.S. at 556-59.
The holding concerns the continuing effect of a completed adjudication, not a rule that parties’ consent
supplies otherwise missing federal jurisdiction. The Court distinguished the question whether jurisdiction
was properly exercised at the outset from the question whether these parties could disregard a still-operative
decree in later litigation. Id. at 556, 558-59.
Judgment
The Supreme Court reversed and remanded for further proceedings consistent with its opinion. The earlier
decree was a valid, subsisting adjudication binding these parties and a bar to the renewed tax claim. The
exact reversal-and-remand mandate comes from the full report; the excerpt ends before it. 123 U.S. at 559.
Reasoning
The Court first identified the difference between error and nullity. A judgment can be erroneous because a
federal court accepted a suit outside the proper jurisdictional limits and nevertheless have legal force until it
is reversed or set aside. The later lawsuit was not an appeal from the earlier decree. It was a collateral attack:
an effort to deny that decree effect in a separate proceeding. 123 U.S. at 556-58.
Prior decisions had treated federal circuit courts as courts of limited jurisdiction whose judgments were not
automatically void when the record failed to allege the jurisdictional facts. Such judgments could be
reversed on appeal, but meanwhile bound the parties and their privies, meaning persons legally identified
with a party’s relevant interest. The Court relied on that settled distinction in assessing the continuing effect
of its own earlier decree. Id. at 557-58.
Homestead tried to distinguish those decisions because this record affirmatively showed shared Iowa
citizenship rather than merely omitting citizenship allegations. The Court answered by considering the
whole procedural setting. New York defendants had invoked a removal statute that required the circuit court
to determine which controversies it could hear, and all parties actually litigated there without objection. The
existence and permissible reach of removal were questions that court had power to decide. Id. at 558-59.
The possibility of a wrong answer did not eliminate the power to decide the jurisdictional question. If the
court should have remanded the entire action, or should have confined itself to the disputes involving the
New York parties, its error could have been corrected on appeal. The Supreme Court had instead entertained
the earlier appeal, implicitly recognized the circuit court’s authority, and finally resolved the merits. That
disposition could not simply be treated as nonexistent in a new action. Id. at 559.

The state court’s refusal to honor the prior decree denied Navigation and Railroad the immunity from
renewed liability that the decree supplied. The Court described that refusal as a failure to give its decree full
faith and credit. It therefore enforced the decree’s finality without deciding anew whether the initial
assumption of jurisdiction had been correct. Id. at 559.

Notes
Narrow reading — interpretation. Where the parties actually litigated a removed suit, the circuit court had
to decide the scope of removal, and the Supreme Court finally disposed of the merits on appeal, an alleged
citizenship defect did not let the losing party ignore that decree in a later suit over the same taxes. The earlier
decree remained binding unless judicially set aside. 123 U.S. at 556-59.
Broad reading — interpretation. The opinion supports finality even when a completed judgment may rest
on a jurisdictional error: authority to decide jurisdiction is distinct from correctly exercising jurisdiction. It
does not establish that every judgment entered without subject-matter jurisdiction is immune from later
challenge. The casebook’s later Restatement note describes exceptions and asks how they should operate;
those are editorial materials, not exceptions formulated by this 1887 Court. Id. at 557-59; casebook pp.
76-77.
Comparison within the casebook. The editors contrast this later collateral attack with Capron’s challenge
through direct review of the original judgment. That procedural difference helps explain why correcting a
jurisdictional error on appeal and honoring an unreversed final decree need not be contradictory. Whether
the Supreme Court’s own involvement was essential is expressly posed as a question in the casebook; this
brief does not turn that question into an additional holding. Casebook pp. 73-77.
