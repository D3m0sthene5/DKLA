# Begovich v. Murphy
359 Mich. 156, 101 N.W.2d 278 (1960) | Contracts

## Decision and posture
The Michigan Supreme Court held that an estate's complaint seeking return of an allegedly unearned advance to a lawyer could not be dismissed without trial. Justice Edwards wrote the February 26, 1960 opinion; Chief Justice Dethmers and Justices Carr, Kelly, Smith, Black, Kavanagh, and Souris concurred. The court reversed and remanded for trial, with costs to the administrator. It did not award the claimed refund or decide the underlying facts. (359 Mich. 157, 161.)

## The representation, payments, and pleaded claim
According to the declaration, Nicholas Begovich was in the county jail on a murder charge around July 10, 1958, and Neil Murphy was his lawyer. Murphy demanded and received $2,500, followed by a further $4,000, for the defense. Before trial, Begovich died by suicide on August 7. The administrator alleged that Murphy's services consisted of an initial interview and consultation, an arraignment appearance waiving examination, and an appearance on the information arranging bail. Those services allegedly were worth no more than $2,500. The estate therefore sought the remaining $4,000, plus costs and attorney fees. These were allegations accepted as true for the appeal, not findings establishing the value of Murphy's work. (Id. at 157–158.)

The trial judge dismissed the declaration on motion and relied on a supposed definite agreement to pay Murphy $10,000 to represent Begovich at trial. That agreement was not alleged in the declaration. The Supreme Court instead read the pleading as an assertion that the second $4,000 payment was an advance for future services that never occurred. Its sufficiency had to be assessed on that basis rather than on an express fee bargain supplied by the trial court. (Id. at 158.)

## Implied compensation and recovery of the unused advance
Michigan law ordinarily implied an obligation to pay the reasonable value of legal services when express compensation terms were absent. Most cited precedents involved lawyers seeking fees; the court reasoned that the same principle should apply when a client sought repayment of an advance. Otherwise an advance could become an entitlement to compensation exceeding the services rendered without any express bargain supporting that result. The court's sole footnote reproduced Court Rule No. 4 (1945), permitting courts, after a client's verified complaint, notice, and hearing, to require an attorney to pay money or perform an act as law and justice required. It treated that rule as supporting the applicable public policy, rather than as an already adjudicated repayment order here. (Id. at 159 & sole footnote.)

The difficult point was the client's suicide, which prevented completion of the defense. For purposes of this record, the court presumed, as the trial judge had, that this amounted to a willful breach. Nevertheless, absent express terms addressing that contingency and a showing of damage to the lawyer, allowing retention of an unearned advance could produce a windfall. The court acknowledged an earlier Kentucky decision permitting a lawyer to retain a fee after a client's suicide, but distinguished its trial record and contractual terms from this pleading-stage case. (Id. at 159–160.)

## Restitution despite the assumed breach
The court endorsed the principles of Restatement of Contracts § 357. That provision measured restitution by the net benefit exceeding the loss caused by the claimant's own breach, subject to limits on the proportion of agreed compensation recoverable. Its quoted text allowed recovery for nonwillful breach or, alternatively, specified circumstances involving the other party's knowing acceptance of performance or retention of property. It also excepted earnest money and permissible contractual retention provisions, while recognizing that an excessive retention provision could be rejected as a penalty. The court linked those principles to an early Michigan decision. (Id. at 160–161.)

The holding was narrower than a general announcement that every willfully breaching client receives every unused dollar back. The pleaded facts did not suggest that the second $4,000 was earnest money or a retainer. No express compensation terms addressing suicide, actual damages to Murphy, or factual record resolving the services' value had been established. The estate could therefore pursue its asserted claim of unjustified retention, with the answers and trial determining the actual circumstances. The court did not assign a particular subsection of § 357 as a conclusively established factual basis for recovery. (Id. at 159–161.)

## Judgment and significance of the assigned problem
The administrator obtained reversal of dismissal and a trial, not a final $4,000 judgment. The assigned lawyer-client problem accurately supplies the alleged payments, services, suicide, and requested refund but presents them as a short hypothetical and calls Murphy's challenge a demurrer. The actual decision expressly speaks of a motion to dismiss and of allegations that remain to be proved. Its additional procedural point is essential: the trial court could not defeat that pleading through an unalleged $10,000 express fee contract. (Id. at 157–158, 161.)
