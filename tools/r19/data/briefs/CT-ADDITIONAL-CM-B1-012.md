# The Meyer and Anna Prentis Family Foundation, Inc. v. Barbara Ann Karmanos Cancer Institute
266 Mich. App. 39, 698 N.W.2d 900 (2005) | Contracts

## Court and posture
The Michigan Court of Appeals issued a unanimous per curiam opinion before Judges Markey, Fitzgerald, and Owens on February 10, 2005; the preserved court copy records approval for publication on April 19, 2005. The Foundation appealed dismissal of the law firm Honigman, Miller, Schwartz and Cohn (HMSC) and dismissal of its legal damages claim against the Institute. The Institute separately appealed the determination that it breached the naming agreement. The court affirmed in part and reversed in part: the Foundation's challenges failed, while the Institute prevailed on standing, consideration, and breach. There is no separate writing. Citations below use the court copy's thirteen-page slip pagination. (Slip 1–2, 10–13.)

## Transaction and litigation
The dispute arose from a 1985 endowment agreement calling for a $1.5 million charitable fund, contributed to Wayne State University in annual installments of at least $300,000 over no more than five years, beginning in 1985. The fund was to support cancer research exclusively. A second provision recognized the Foundation's longstanding leadership and financial contributions and expressed appreciation for the newly created fund. It then stated that the University, the center, and the Michigan Cancer Foundation agreed to rename the center the Meyer L. Prentis Comprehensive Cancer Center of Metropolitan Detroit. The assigned problem adds that the payments were completed in 1990. (Slip 2, 11–12; assigned PDF 119, printed 73.)

The Michigan Cancer Foundation merged with the center. The Foundation's board representative attended discussions of the merger in 1994 and discussions and voting concerning the Karmanos gift and renaming in 1995. The Foundation sued July 27, 2000. It alleged breach of the naming undertaking and challenged HMSC's conduct in connection with the original arrangement, the failure to legally change the name, the merger, and the later donor. The full appellate caption names the Institute and HMSC as defendants; Wayne State University's appearance as the contracting university in the teaching problem does not make it an appellee in this caption. (Slip 1–3, 5–8.)

## Naming promise, consideration, and breach
The central contract question was whether the Foundation's contribution was exchanged for an enforceable naming promise. Reviewing the unambiguous agreement de novo, the court sought intent in the language used. Consideration required a bargained exchange involving a benefit or detriment. The clause's first reference to years of past contributions could not provide consideration for a subsequent agreement. Its reference to the new fund still used recognition and appreciation, rather than language indicating that payment was exchanged for the naming provision. The court accordingly characterized the naming undertaking as gratuitous and legally unenforceable. It rejected the Foundation's unilateral expectations as a basis for rewriting the document and noted that the Foundation could have expressly provided for return of the funds if the name did not change, but had not done so. This is the court's interpretation of this agreement, rather than a holding that every charitable naming undertaking is unenforceable or that a particular formula is always indispensable to consideration. (Slip 11–12 & nn.7–8.)

The court also held that the trial court erred in finding breach. The enforceable restriction required exclusive use of the money for cancer research, and nothing in the record indicated an improper use. Once the naming provision was treated as unenforceable, the renaming did not establish breach of the obligation that remained. (Slip 12.)

## Standing to enforce the charitable trust
On the Institute's cross-appeal, the court also concluded that the Foundation lacked standing. It applied the then-governing Michigan standing formulation requiring concrete injury to a legally protected interest, causation, and likely redressability. Under the cited charitable-trust authorities, a charitable donation created a charitable trust regardless of the donor's motive. The fund's stated cancer-research purpose was charitable. Enforcement could be sought by the Attorney General, a co-trustee, or a person with a special interest, but not by the settlor or the settlor's heirs merely in that capacity. The court agreed with the Institute's standing objection on that basis. This historical ruling must be distinguished from the separate contract consideration analysis and from any later changes in donor standing or trust law. (Slip 10–11 & n.6.)

## Claims against the law firm
The Foundation contended that it reasonably relied on HMSC and that HMSC owed it fiduciary duties notwithstanding the absence of a formal retainer. The court held that reasonable trust and reliance could not be inferred merely because the Foundation's representative served on the center's board. Counsel for a corporation represents the corporation, rather than its shareholders or agents. The Foundation sought to memorialize a family name, while the center sought financing for cancer research; their interests were not completely identical. The Foundation identified no action showing that HMSC represented it independently, and the firm's agency agreement with the center did not create rights in a third party. Duties of loyalty, good faith, and avoidance of self-dealing owed to the center therefore did not become duties owed to the Foundation. (Slip 2–3, 6.)

The court also rejected the allegations of concealed conflicts and dual representation on the record presented. HMSC produced letters showing disclosure of, and consent to, its potential conflict in handling the Karmanos gift. A 1993 letter had already alerted the Foundation's board representative to a prospective donor who would receive name recognition. For the merger, HMSC obtained approvals and advised the center to consider independent counsel. The fact that a firm attorney, acting as board chairman, signed merger documents did not itself show legal representation of both parties; the Foundation offered no board minutes showing participation in the approval decision, and contemporaneous documents indicated discussion of the naming obligation. These are the court's reasons for finding the Foundation's evidence insufficient, rather than adoption of every allegation of misconduct as fact. (Slip 6–8.)

The court further held the fiduciary-duty claim time-barred. It rejected HMSC's proposed two-year malpractice period and applied the three-year period for the asserted tort injury, including loss of naming rights. The board representative's participation meant the Foundation should have known of a possible claim by 1995, more than three years before suit. Fraudulent-concealment tolling required affirmative concealment, not mere silence; discoverable public records and the Foundation's lack of diligence defeated tolling. The extended second footnote expressly addresses why the absence of a fiduciary relationship and the availability of public information required reasonable investigation here. This limitations ruling was additional to the absence of an independent fiduciary duty. (Slip 3–6 & nn.2–4.)

## Jury procedure, damages, and sanctions
The Foundation argued that the trial court could not revise an earlier determination or require a jury determination of legal liability after a bench trial on equitable matters. The court rejected that application of law of the case: an appellate ruling differs from a trial court's revisable decision. In a mixed legal and equitable case, court factfinding on equitable claims does not replace jury factfinding on damages claims. The Institute could rely on the Foundation's jury demand without filing a separate demand, and the record did not show waiver: the pretrial order contemplated a bench trial followed by a jury trial, and the judge's comments indicated that legal liability remained for the jury. (Slip 8–9 & n.5.)

The court upheld dismissal of damages as well. The first judge had found rescission unjustified and restitution unavailable in connection with it. The later summary-disposition ruling found no demonstrated method to measure the Foundation's claimed loss and considered the damages speculative. Although a jury generally determines amounts, the court determines legal entitlement. On appeal the Foundation cited no authority supporting its damages theory, and the appellate court declined to construct an argument for it. The opinion therefore should not be reduced to a universal rule that nonprofit entities cannot recover damages. (Slip 9–10.)

HMSC's sanctions request also failed. Trial-level sanctions were not properly before the court because HMSC had not cross-appealed that issue. Appellate sanctions required a separate motion; a request in a brief was insufficient, and appropriate supporting authority had not been provided. The final judgment states “affirmed in part and reversed in part,” without an express remand instruction. (Slip 13.)
