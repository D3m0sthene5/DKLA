# Schott v. Westinghouse Electric Corp.
436 Pa. 279, 259 A.2d 443 (1969) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result

Justice Pomeroy delivered the Pennsylvania Supreme Court’s majority opinion on November 28, 1969. The court upheld the rejection of Schott’s express-contract count but reversed the dismissal of his unjust-enrichment count and remanded for further proceedings. Justices Roberts and O’Brien concurred in the result on a contract rationale and rejected the majority’s restitution rationale. Chief Justice Bell dissented. The remaining four judges constituted the majority. No damages were awarded, and no finding established that Westinghouse had actually appropriated a novel idea. The question was whether the pleaded facts and reasonable inferences could support recovery. (436 Pa. at 281–282, 286–287, 293–296.)

## Program, submissions, and disputed use

From 1962 through 1965, Westinghouse invited employees to submit practical suggestions to improve production or reduce costs. Its forms offered cash awards of $5 to $15,000 for adopted suggestions. Immediately above the employee’s signature was an agreement that the local Suggestion Committee’s decisions on the suggestion, award eligibility, and amount would be final. Rejected suggestions could be reopened within twelve months or resubmitted afterward. (Id. at 282–283.)

In May 1962, Schott proposed replacing cast-aluminum circuit-breaker panels with fabricated heavy-gauge steel. The committee rejected the proposal because design, model, and testing expenses would exceed the savings, but said it would consider the idea if breaker redesign later began for other reasons. Schott resubmitted in September 1963. The January 1964 rejection added a need for nonmagnetic materials and referred to an independent cost-reduction study involving partly nonmagnetic panels. (Id. at 283–284.)

Schott alleged that the company nevertheless used his recommendations, probably in 1964. Its May 1965 response acknowledged application of the same basic idea in a redesign but maintained that the earlier rejection was justified and that the change resulted from independent action without knowledge of his suggestion. That asserted independent origin was the company’s position, not an adjudicated fact. Schott sought an accounting of savings and initially damages equal to twenty percent of them. After dismissal with leave to amend, he pleaded separate contract and unjust-enrichment counts. The trial court dismissed again, reasoning that the committee’s rejection controlled, without separately addressing the new restitution count. (Id. at 284–286.)

## Why the majority rejected contractual recovery

The majority did not need to classify the program as a unilateral offer or an invitation for employees to make offers. Either way, Schott accepted the program’s terms giving the committee final authority. If the company offered a unilateral contract, it requested a suggestion its committee in good faith would deem acceptable, rather than merely the physical act of submitting any suggestion. That specified performance had not occurred. If Schott’s submission instead constituted the offer, a good-faith committee rejection prevented acceptance. The pleaded first count therefore failed to establish an express contract, rather than establishing a contract under which no award happened to be due. (Id. at 287–289.)

Important limits appeared in the notes. Schott did not allege fraud or bad faith that would release him from the finality clause, challenge the reality of his assent, or seek judicial revision of an adhesion contract. The court also did not decide whether discretion over the award amount made damages insufficiently definite. Its resolution did not depend on retaining the unilateral–bilateral distinction as a general rule. (Id. at 285–288; nn.1, 3–4.)

## Why restitution could still proceed

The majority distinguished formal adoption by the committee from practical appropriation by the company’s operating personnel. Formal adoption mattered to the express-contract count; actual use of Schott’s idea could matter to restitution. Although unjust enrichment was unavailable where an express agreement governed the relationship, the majority had found no such completed contract here. An unsuccessful attempt to contract did not itself foreclose an obligation imposed by law. Quasi-contract could operate without assent and even despite a defendant’s contrary intention. (Id. at 289–291.)

A demurrer could defeat the claim only if the pleaded facts left no doubt that recovery was legally impossible. The exhibits connected an invitation for suggestions, a promise to reconsider during future redesign, and subsequent use of the same basic idea. Schott additionally alleged resulting savings. At this stage, the court could not characterize his submission conclusively as gratuitous or officious. It did not decide that any benefit was unjustly retained; it held that the allegations allowed that possibility to be tested. (Id. at 291–292; 286 n.2.)

Westinghouse relied on Thomas v. R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co., requiring proof of a concrete and novel idea. Thomas concerned failure of proof at trial, including prior company testing and earlier submission of the same idea. Schott was still at the pleading stage. His written proposal and allegation of a valuable idea permitted the necessary inference of concreteness and novelty, leaving him to prove those matters at trial. The majority expressly disclaimed any inference about the claim’s ultimate validity or provability. (Id. at 292–294.)

## Separate positions

Roberts, joined by O’Brien, would permit a contract claim if actual use resulted from Schott’s submission. Acceptance by conduct could matter despite a formal rejection; the allegation of appropriation supplied that possible causal connection. But Schott had submitted under the company’s established procedure and, in their view, could not obtain restitution outside its limits. Allowing a rejected suggestion potentially to yield more than $15,000 while an accepted suggestion remained subject to committee discretion distorted the agreed program. Their concurrence’s sole note made contract classification immaterial to this alternative analysis. (Id. at 294–295 & concurrence note.)

Bell opposed the unjust-enrichment doctrine and considered the written terms conclusive of the parties’ rights. He would not permit recovery contrary to those restrictions, particularly without fraud or deceit. That was a dissent, not a limitation adopted by the majority. (Id. at 296.)
