# McInnes v. California

**943 F.2d 1088 (9th Cir. 1991)** — August 28, 1991. Judge Fletcher wrote for a panel with Judges Norris and Trott; there are no separate writings. Civil Procedure. Assigned: CivPro PDF 695, printed 1381–1382, McInnes account and complete quotation in Note 2.

Theresa McInnes’s employment dispute involved two California agencies and successive proceedings. She joined the Department of Justice in 1978 and later complained of sex discrimination and harassment. A June 1984 settlement provided compensation, leave credits, retirement contributions, and partial disability status for gastrointestinal problems. It also required help placing her in a training-and-development assignment as a Senior Special Investigator with the Department of Consumer Affairs, including peace-officer training. After that assignment and probation, she could apply for employment or transfer back to Justice. The agreement did not immediately appoint her as a Special Agent; footnote 1 reproduces the conditional sequence and good-faith application terms. (1090 and n.1.)

At Consumer Affairs, supervisor Reuben Dennis harassed her and characterized her as emotionally incapable. The State Personnel Board’s later findings contrasted his behavior with another inspector’s favorable assessment. After McInnes complained to officials and showed them a draft complaint addressed to the board, the department abandoned its investigation of Dennis, scrutinized her attendance, and terminated her training assignment during a health-related absence. These facts were recounted through the board record; the appellate court was not conducting a new discrimination trial. (1090–1091.)

McInnes formally complained to the board in August 1984. An executive officer initially ruled against her, but an administrative law judge conducted evidentiary hearings and found sex discrimination and retaliatory termination. The board adopted that ruling in September 1986. It ordered cessation of discrimination, reporting of complaints, training for managers, restoration of leave, medical-expense reimbursement, $10,000 compensation, and a renewed assignment on the earlier settlement’s terms. It lacked authority to award attorney’s fees. After disputes over proposed assignments, McInnes sought immediate placement as a Special Agent without another training assignment. The board denied that modification in October 1987: Justice considered it better than the original settlement promised, and Consumer Affairs said it had offered qualifying assignments she rejected. Neither side sought state judicial review of either board ruling. (1091.)

Meanwhile, McInnes had filed with the EEOC after waiting more than sixty days from her state complaint. The EEOC largely adopted the favorable administrative findings, attempted conciliation, and issued a right-to-sue notice in October 1987. She sued in January 1988 under Title VII, seeking injunctive relief, back pay, fees and costs, and restoration to the position and benefits she would have received without discrimination. The district court initially denied dismissal without prejudice but said the board’s determinations were binding; it later granted summary judgment to defendants on that basis. The Ninth Circuit reviewed summary judgment and availability of issue preclusion de novo. This case raised issue preclusion, not claim preclusion, as note 2 emphasizes. (1092 and n.2.)

The central distinction was between **state judicial judgments** and **unreviewed administrative decisions**. Section 1738 requires federal courts to give state judgments the force they have in the rendering state. Kremer applied that statute after state judicial affirmance of an agency ruling, but its note 7 distinguished unreviewed administrative decisions. University of Tennessee v. Elliott made the Title VII distinction explicit: §1738 did not govern an unreviewed agency determination, and Congress’s statutory design prevented creation of a federal common-law preclusion rule for such Title VII claims. That differed from Elliott’s treatment of certain administrative factfinding in §1983 litigation. McInnes’s note 4 expressly preserves the distinction between issue preclusion and the unresolved extension to claim preclusion under §1983. (1092–1094 and nn.3–5.)

Defendants tried to characterize the State Personnel Board as a court of limited jurisdiction. The court rejected that premise both under California descriptions of the entity and, more importantly, federal interpretation of §1738. State law helps identify an entity’s attributes, and its characterization deserves some deference, but federal law ultimately determines whether it qualifies as a court under a federal statute. A state constitutional basis, hearings, subpoenas, and other quasi-judicial powers do not by themselves create a judicial court. The board combined adjudication with civil-service administration, enforcement, investigations, classifications, and probation rules. Its specialized bureaucratic organization and review through writ proceedings resembled an administrative agency. Preclusive force under state law likewise did not transform it into a court for §1738. (1094–1095.)

Nor did the board’s sophistication and independence distinguish Elliott. The rule did not turn on whether the agency was a single official or a formal institution. Earlier Ninth Circuit cases cited by defendants involved administrative decisions actually reviewed by courts. The possibility that either side **could have** obtained review was insufficient: reviewability was not actual judicial review. McInnes also remained entitled to invoke Title VII after winning some administrative relief when she considered the remedy or enforcement incomplete. The court therefore reversed the issue-preclusion disposition rather than treating the favorable board decision as exhausting every potential federal remedy. (1095–1096.)

The board’s findings were not irrelevant. They were probative and could be considered as evidence, but they did not conclusively bind the federal court through administrative issue preclusion. The district court should independently consider appropriate Title VII remedies, including attorney’s fees the board could not award. The panel did not prescribe a specific new appointment, damages total, or fee award. Note 7 requires the district court to examine a separate stipulation concerning board findings: the parties disagreed whether it also accepted the adequacy of remedies. Declining administrative preclusion did not decide that contractual or litigation stipulation’s effect. (1096 and n.7.)

The panel also rejected affirmance based on the district court’s purported alternative determination that a de novo record review showed complete relief. The district court had held no evidentiary hearing and supplied no findings or legal conclusions reflecting plenary consideration. Without an adequate record, the appellate court could not meaningfully review that alternative ground. This was a refusal to affirm an unsupported disposition, not an appellate finding establishing all additional relief McInnes claimed. (1096 n.6.)

The judgment was **reversed and remanded for further proceedings consistent with the opinion**. The assigned passage accurately emphasizes the federal meaning of court and the insufficiency of quasi-judicial powers. Its surrounding hypothetical about a state-created Personnel Court is an editorial question, not a ruling that labels alone either establish or defeat court status. The complete judicial report and all seven notes were read, together with the whole assigned passage and original scan. Original reporter images were not independently collated. The historical Title VII holding does not establish a universal rule against administrative preclusion under other statutes or certify current law.
