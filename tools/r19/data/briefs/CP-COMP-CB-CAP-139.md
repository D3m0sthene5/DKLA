# Skelly Oil Co. v. Phillips Petroleum Co.

**339 U.S. 667 (1950) — Supreme Court of the United States, June 5, 1950.** Justice Frankfurter wrote the Court’s opinion. Chief Justice Vinson, joined by Justice Burton, agreed with dismissal as to two defendants but dissented from the remand concerning the third. Justice Black would affirm the court of appeals throughout; Justice Douglas did not participate. Assigned account: *CivPro.pdf*, physical pages 190–191, printed pages 335–336, “Note on the Declaratory Judgment Act and Artful Pleading.”

## Contracts, certificate, and attempted termination

Michigan-Wisconsin Pipe Line Company sought a Federal Power Commission certificate under the Natural Gas Act to construct and operate a pipeline carrying Texas natural gas to Michigan and Wisconsin. Adequate reserves were a prerequisite. Phillips Petroleum agreed to supply gas that it produced or purchased in the Hugoton Field. To obtain those supplies, Phillips contracted with Skelly Oil, Stanolind, and Magnolia. Each supplier could terminate by written notice after December 1, 1946, but before issuance of the contemplated federal certificate, if Michigan-Wisconsin had not secured it by the contractual October 1 deadline. **669.**

On November 30, the Commission ordered that a certificate be issued subject to conditions. Those included state and local authorizations, financing approval, an acceptable rate schedule, and protection of an existing pipeline company’s service interests. A supplemental order would address some matters, and Michigan-Wisconsin would have time afterward to accept the certificate. The order also postponed its deemed issuance date for calculating rehearing time. News of the action appeared November 30, but the order’s actual contents became public December 2. That day, each supplier gave Phillips a termination notice, asserting that the required certificate had not been received. **669–670.**

Phillips and Michigan-Wisconsin sued in the Northern District of Oklahoma for a declaration that a certificate had issued before the notices and that the contracts remained binding. Michigan-Wisconsin was dropped as a plaintiff. The district court rejected jurisdictional objections and held the contracts effectively unterminated. The Tenth Circuit affirmed, treating the Commission’s November 30 action as satisfying the contractual certificate condition. The Supreme Court reviewed both jurisdiction and the remaining merits question. **670–671, 675.**

## Jurisdictional holding

The Declaratory Judgment Act enlarges the remedies available in federal court; it does not supply a new source of subject-matter jurisdiction. A party seeking a declaration must satisfy an independent jurisdictional grant. In this case, Phillips’s contract claim did not arise under federal law merely because it anticipated a defense involving a federal certificate. The cases against Skelly and Stanolind, for which diversity could not sustain jurisdiction, had to be dismissed. Magnolia’s different citizenship supplied diversity, so its case remained within federal jurisdiction. **671–674.**

## Why a declaration could not change the claim’s source

Before declaratory relief became available, Phillips could seek damages or specific performance only if a federal right or diversity supported federal jurisdiction. Congress allowed a court to recognize rights without immediately enforcing them, but it did not implicitly repeal the jurisdictional limits that already governed those remedies. **671–672.**

If Phillips sued to enforce these contracts, its claim would arise under state contract law. A federal issue concerning the certificate would enter in anticipation of the suppliers’ defense. Under the well-pleaded-complaint cases, a plaintiff’s own claim must present the federal question without allegations added to anticipate or avoid a defense. The likelihood that federal law would matter somewhere in the dispute did not make it the source of Phillips’s right. **672.**

The Court rejected the suggestion that this was empty technicality. Allowing a declaration to federalize a state-created right whenever the plaintiff anticipated a federal defense would substantially expand the lower federal docket. It would also require federal courts to decide uncertain local law, or await state decisions, in litigation Congress had not placed within their jurisdiction. That practical expansion would distort the Act’s limited remedial purpose. The Court’s reference to “artful pleading” concerns this effort to manufacture federal-question jurisdiction through anticipation of a defense. **673–674.**

## Magnolia’s separate position and the unsettled contract issue

Magnolia was a Texas corporation, while Phillips was incorporated in Delaware. That diversity sustained jurisdiction. Magnolia had also qualified to do business in Oklahoma and appointed a service agent. The lower courts rejected its argument that this controversy fell outside that consent, and the Supreme Court did not reexamine their application of local law. Under the then-applied *Neirbo* doctrine, venue was properly laid. Whether Oklahoma’s own courts offered an equivalent declaratory remedy did not limit the federal court’s remedy. **674.**

Jurisdiction did not, however, settle whether Magnolia’s December 2 notice terminated the contract. The Tenth Circuit relied heavily on the Commission’s present-tense announcement that a certificate was issued, the statute’s allowance for conditional certificates, and its view that the delayed rehearing date concerned only a supplemental order. **675–676.**

The Supreme Court found that treatment insufficient. A certificate defining legal rights could not simply remain undisclosed inside the agency. The Commission’s own rules distinguished adopting an order from issuing it through delivery or publication, although its terminology in other proceedings was not entirely consistent. Nor did the order’s rehearing provision clearly apply only to the supplemental order. The Court refused to declare a single uniform meaning of certificate “issuance” for every statutory context. The district court’s finding that the Commission agreed on the document November 30 did not resolve the legal effect of that action. **676–677 & n.1.**

The question had regulatory implications beyond the parties’ private interests, yet the courts had not benefited from the Commission’s experience. Under § 2106, further proceedings could illuminate those issues. Moreover, contractual words copied from a statute need not invariably carry precisely the statute’s meaning: the parties’ agreement might assign the certificate language an independent scope. The remand preserved both inquiries rather than deciding which date definitively governed the contract. **678–679 & nn.2–3.**

## Separate views and exact disposition

**Vinson, joined by Burton,** agreed with the jurisdictional dismissal as to Skelly and Stanolind, while expressing doubt that the controversy contained a federal question at all. He disagreed with reopening Magnolia’s merits. The Commission’s practice distinguished issuance of a certificate from issuance of the full order for rehearing purposes. Its later order described November 30 as the certificate’s issuance date. The district court found that the secretary prepared the exact order during the meeting and immediately announced its adoption and certificate issuance by telegram and press release. In Vinson’s view, postponing rehearing time did not suspend the certificate itself, and the contract’s reference to agency action should follow that effective date. He would affirm as to Magnolia. **679–683 & dissent nn.1–2.**

**Black** separately recorded that he agreed with the court of appeals and would affirm its judgment; the report supplies no additional reasoning. **679.**

The Court **reversed as to Skelly and Stanolind with directions to dismiss**, but **vacated and remanded as to Magnolia** for further proceedings. Thus, it neither dismissed every defendant nor finally held Magnolia’s termination effective. **679.**
