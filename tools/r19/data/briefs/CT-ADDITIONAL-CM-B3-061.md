# Conley v. Guerrero
443 N.J. Super. 62, 127 A.3d 705 (App. Div. 2015) | Contracts

## Court, issue, and result

On November 5, 2015, the New Jersey Superior Court, Appellate Division, affirmed summary judgment dismissing buyers Michael Conley and Katie Maurer's action to enforce a condominium sale. Judge Ostrer wrote for a panel listing Judges Messano, Ostrer and Tassini; no separate writing or numerical vote is reproduced. Seller Mona Guerrero's counsel timely disapproved the agreement by email and fax, although the standard review clause prescribed certified mail, telegram or personal delivery for notice to the realtor. On the undisputed actual-notice facts, substantial compliance and avoidance of disproportionate forfeiture made her termination effective. The panel expressly declined to establish a general rule that email satisfied the prescribed delivery provision. (443 N.J. Super. at 62–63, 68–73.)

## Transaction and competing offers

The buyers signed a broker-prepared contract on January 12, 2014 for Guerrero's Bernards Township condominium at $292,000 and paid a $1,000 deposit. Guerrero signed two days later; the executed agreement was delivered January 15. Its standard three-day attorney-review clause allowed either party's attorney to disapprove, required notice to the realtor and other party, and prescribed certified mail, telegram or personal delivery for the realtor's notice. Because of a three-day weekend, the review period extended through January 21. Weichert was identified as a disclosed dual agent. (Id. at 64–65 & nn. 1–2.)

After execution, other offers arrived. The buyers knew this, sent Guerrero a personal note expressing their desire for the home, and increased their offer to $298,000 around January 15. Brian Kraminitz and Michele Tanzi offered $307,500 and signed a separate agreement with Guerrero on January 16. Their attorneys negotiated modifications and regarded that agreement as binding January 20. These negotiations supplied the context but did not themselves establish that the first buyers had abandoned their contract. (Id. at 64–66.)

On January 16, the realtor asked Guerrero's lawyer to transmit disapproval of the first agreement. On January 20, a lawyer in his office sent the buyers' counsel a letter by email and fax, declaring the seller's termination and authorizing release of the deposit; the realtor received a copy by email. Receipt by counsel and agent was undisputed, and the buyers did not deny notice. On January 23, their counsel nevertheless asserted that the contract had become binding because no notice had arrived in an authorized form during review. The buyers sought specific performance and other relief against Guerrero and the competing purchasers. (Id. at 65–66 & n. 2.)

## Trial ruling and arguments

The trial judge denied temporary injunctive relief, then decided cross-motions for summary judgment because the facts were undisputed. He dismissed the buyers' complaint. The clause specified delivery methods to the broker, not the other party. Counsel had actually reviewed the agreements, satisfying the review period's protective purpose, and everyone had timely actual notice. The judge regarded the deviation as minor and emphasized substance rather than form in equity. (Id. at 66–67.)

The buyers demanded strict compliance. The seller, other respondents and State Bar Association as amicus argued substantial compliance and satisfaction of the notice provision's purpose. The panel reviewed summary judgment de novo and agreed that the disapproval was effective. (Id. at 67–68.)

## Distinct notice rights

The standard attorney-review provision originated in the court-approved settlement between the bar and realtors. The Supreme Court had added notice to the contracting party to an original proposal requiring notice only to the real-estate professional. But only the realtor's notice was subject to the specified delivery methods. Any actual notice sufficed for the buyers themselves. The panel inferred that the prescribed methods protected real-estate professionals' interests, whereas attorney review protected contracting parties from being bound by broker-prepared documents without legal advice. Those interests need not coincide. (Id. at 68–69.)

The buyers' own notice right was satisfied. They instead sought to enforce a realtor delivery right about which the realtor did not complain. The panel assumed, without deciding, that the buyers could enforce that right, and held that substantial compliance defeated their demand anyway. This alternative reasoning should not be converted into a holding that buyers categorically lack standing to enforce the prescribed forms. (Id. at 69.)

## Substantial compliance and forfeiture

The court applied the principle allowing excuse of a condition's nonoccurrence to avoid disproportionate forfeiture unless occurrence was material to the agreed exchange. Its balancing considered the extent of lost rights, the importance of the risk protected against, and how much protection would be lost by excusing exact compliance. Here strict enforcement would forfeit Guerrero's right to disapprove during review and enter a different agreement, while the buyers already had the timely actual notice the delivery rule sought to ensure. (Id. at 69–70.)

The prescribed procedure was not a material, individually negotiated exchange term: the court-approved settlement imposed it on these parties, even though they could not simply waive the standard review protections. That origin justified more liberal substantial-compliance treatment than a condition expressing an arm's-length bargain. The panel did not hold that all imposed conditions were optional; it applied forfeiture balancing where the provision's practical notice purpose was undisputedly fulfilled. (Id. at 68–70.)

## Earlier cases and the limited email holding

Peterson's demand for strict adherence concerned delivery of an executed agreement to a broker rather than the seller, which failed to achieve the purpose of seller delivery. Here the buyers and realtor had actual notice; litigation disputed its legal effect, not receipt. Kutzin involved no actual disapproval within review, rather than a timely disapproval whose method deviated from the prescribed form. Romano involved prior attorney approval during review, which had not occurred here; Gaglia concerned a party trying to profit from its own counsel's defective disapproval. Those distinctions prevented the precedents from compelling enforcement of this sale. (Id. at 66–67, 70–72.)

The panel refused the amicus request to revise the standard clause for modern communications. Certified mail, telegram and third-person delivery provided independent proof and generally reliable means of delivery; the record did not establish that telegram services were unavailable. Whether email or fax generally fulfilled those functions was left for others. Its affirmance was confined to these facts, with undisputed timely notice to buyers, attorney and agent. The complaint remained dismissed, with no specific-performance order. (Id. at 72–73.)
