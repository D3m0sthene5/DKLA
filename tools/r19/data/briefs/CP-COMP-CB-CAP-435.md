# Baltimore & Carolina Line, Inc. v. Redman

**295 U.S. 654 (1935) — Supreme Court of the United States, June 3, 1935.** Justice Van Devanter delivered the Court’s opinion. The report contains no separate opinion or numerical vote tally. Assigned source: *CivPro.pdf*, physical pages 571–572, own-caption excerpt printed 1125–1127, with all three attached editorial notes through printed 1128.

The Court held that the Seventh Amendment permitted judgment for a defendant after a plaintiff’s jury verdict when the trial judge had expressly reserved the defendant’s sufficiency motions and taken the verdict subject to their resolution. That procedure preserved an established common-law division between legal rulings and factual determinations. The Court modified the appellate mandate to require dismissal on the merits, and affirmed the judgment as modified. It did not simply order another trial. 295 U.S. at 659–661.

## Facts and procedural path

Redman sued Baltimore & Carolina Line in a federal court in New York for personal injuries allegedly caused by the company’s negligence. The Court’s opinion describes neither the accident’s mechanics nor the amount of damages; its concern is how the trial court handled the legal sufficiency of the evidence. After all evidence had been presented, the defendant moved both to dismiss the complaint and for a directed verdict, arguing that the proof could not support a verdict for Redman. The judge reserved decision on both motions and submitted the case to the jury expressly subject to the reserved rulings. Neither party objected. The jury returned a plaintiff’s verdict; the judge then found the evidence sufficient, denied the motions, and entered judgment on that verdict. Id. at 656.

The Second Circuit disagreed about sufficiency and reversed. The defendant asked for dismissal of the complaint, but the appellate court directed a new trial because it understood *Slocum v. New York Life Insurance Co.*, 228 U.S. 364 (1913), to prohibit contrary judgment after a jury verdict. The Supreme Court granted the defendant’s petition concerning that remedy and denied Redman’s separate petition challenging the insufficiency determination. Consequently, the question before the Court was what judgment should follow the appellate ruling, rather than a fresh examination of the underlying negligence evidence. Id. at 656 & nn.1–3, 659.

## Issue and positions

Did the Seventh Amendment require a new trial after the appellate court held the evidence legally insufficient, or could the court direct judgment for the defendant because the jury’s verdict had been received subject to an express reservation?

The defendant sought dismissal on the merits. The appellate court’s contrary position rested on its reading of *Slocum*: once a jury had decided for the plaintiff, an appellate court could correct the legal error only by setting the verdict aside and directing a new trial. Redman’s unsuccessful sufficiency petition is recorded, but the opinion does not furnish a detailed account of his separate merits arguments. It would therefore be inaccurate to invent particular testimony or an independent Supreme Court holding about the accident. Id. at 656, 661.

## Governing constitutional distinction

The Seventh Amendment preserves the jury-trial right that existed under English common law when the Amendment was adopted. Its Reexamination Clause also protects that right against enlargement of judicial power over facts already tried by a jury. The Court nevertheless distinguished the substance of the right from procedural form. In the absence of express or implied consent to a different allocation, legal questions belong to the judge and factual questions belong to the jury under judicial instructions. Legal sufficiency was a question for the court. Id. at 656–657, 659.

That distinction does not mean that every verdict may automatically be replaced by contrary judgment. The operative question was whether this particular manner of reserving a legal issue and receiving a conditional verdict belonged to the common-law procedures that the Amendment preserved. The Court answered yes. Id. at 659–660.

## Reasoning: reservation changes the constitutional posture

In *Slocum*, the trial court had denied a directed-verdict request without reserving any issue and received the plaintiff’s verdict unconditionally. The appellate court later held the evidence insufficient and directed judgment for the defendant under a state statute. The Supreme Court treated that action as an impermissible displacement of the plaintiff’s unwaived right to have factual issues resolved by a jury. A state statute could not authorize a procedure inconsistent with the Seventh Amendment; setting aside the verdict for legal error and ordering a new trial was permissible in the circumstances there. Id. at 657–658.

Here the judge had expressly reserved the two motions before receiving the verdict. The verdict therefore remained subject to the court’s legal decision. Because neither litigant objected to this arrangement, the Court regarded the reservation and submission as having their tacit consent. The trial judge’s eventual rejection of the motions did not erase the reservation or turn the verdict into the unconditional verdict involved in *Slocum*. Id. at 658–659.

Common-law courts had long reserved legal questions while taking verdicts subject to later rulings. Reservation carried authority to make the disposition required by the eventual legal conclusion, including a nonsuit or judgment for the party against whom the jury had found. The extended fifth footnote supplies early English decisions, legal treatises, and later examples involving conditional verdicts and nonsuits. Its examples establish the procedure’s historical existence; they are not findings about Redman’s accident. Id. at 659–660 & n.5.

The Court acknowledged uncertainty about the practice’s precise origin and theoretical foundation. The suggested advantages were more considered legal rulings, fewer retrials, and broad acceptance by litigants. Its constitutional point did not depend on proving one exact historical explanation: the practice was established when the Amendment was adopted. Earlier decisions of the Supreme Court had also recognized conditional verdicts, including a case in which it directed judgment for the defendant after a plaintiff’s conditional verdict. Id. at 660–661 & nn.6–7.

New York’s statute embodied the relevant common-law practice, and the trial judge had followed it and the state court’s approved application. The statute reinforced the procedural route; it did not independently override the federal Constitution. With the insufficiency ruling in place, the proper result was dismissal on the merits, equivalent to judgment for the defendant on a directed verdict. Id. at 661 & n.8.

The Court expressly confronted broad passages in *Slocum* that supported the Second Circuit’s reading. Those passages exceeded the circumstances actually decided there and were qualified by *Redman*. Thus the opinion both distinguished *Slocum* and limited its broader language; it did not announce that the entire earlier decision was overruled. Id. at 661.

## Exact disposition and assigned significance

The Supreme Court modified the Second Circuit’s judgment by replacing its new-trial direction with a direction for dismissal on the merits, then affirmed as modified. The defendant thereby obtained the requested final disposition rather than another opportunity to try the same case. Id. at 661.
