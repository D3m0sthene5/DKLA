Parties
David Roth was a first-year assistant professor at Wisconsin State University–Oshkosh and the plaintiff
seeking reasons and a hearing concerning nonrenewal. The university authorities, including the Board of
Regents, were defendants and Supreme Court petitioners. They defended their decision not to offer another
appointment and denied Roth’s allegation that the reason was retaliation for criticism of the administration.
The Supreme Court reviewed the procedural due process ruling, not a finding resolving that disputed motive.
408 U.S. at 566–569 & n.5.
The challenged action was expiration and nonrenewal of an annual appointment. Roth completed the term the
university had promised. His case therefore differed from dismissal during an existing term or discharge from a
tenured position, both of which would involve a different asserted source and duration of job security. Id. at
566–568, 576–578.
Material Facts
Roth’s appointment began September 1, 1968, and ended June 30, 1969. The formal notice of appointment
functioned as the equivalent of an employment contract even though there was no separate contract instrument.
Its terms did not promise renewal or continued employment unless cause existed. Roth was in his first teaching
position and had not acquired tenure under Wisconsin law. Id. at 566 & n.1, 578.
The governing statute provided permanent employment after four years of continuous service, subject to
efficiency and good behavior, and protected permanent teachers against discharge without cause and specified
procedures. A nontenured teacher had some opportunity for review of dismissal during the year, but university
rules expressly distinguished dismissal from nonretention for the following year. They required notice by
February 1 while providing that reasons and review need not be supplied for nonretention. Id. at 566–568 &
nn.2–4.
Before February 1, 1969, the university president notified Roth that he would not be rehired for 1969–1970. No
reasons or hearing accompanied the decision. On the record presented, the university made no charge of
dishonesty, immorality, or comparable misconduct damaging his standing, and imposed no bar preventing him
from applying for other university employment. These absences were central to the majority’s liberty analysis.
Id. at 568, 573–575.
Roth alleged that his criticism of the administration caused the decision. The university disputed that
allegation, and the district court reserved the factual question for trial. Douglas’s dissent described controversy
over the administration’s suspension of students and Roth’s criticism, but the majority did not find that

Procedural History
Roth sued in federal district court, asserting a substantive free-speech objection and a procedural due process
objection. The district court granted him partial summary judgment on procedure and ordered reasons and a
hearing. It left the true reason for nonrenewal undecided. The Seventh Circuit affirmed the partial judgment,
with one judge dissenting. The university authorities obtained certiorari. Id. at 568–569 & n.5.
The Supreme Court confined its review to the claimed constitutional right to reasons and a hearing on this
record. It reversed and remanded because Roth had not established a deprivation of protected liberty or
property sufficient to support summary judgment in his favor. The decision did not finally dispose of the
distinct claim that the university had retaliated against protected speech. Id. at 569, 574–575, 578–579.
Issue
Did nonrenewal of this one-year, nontenured appointment deprive Roth of liberty or property protected by the
Fourteenth Amendment, requiring reasons and an opportunity to be heard? Could the importance of continued
employment itself establish that threshold interest, or did Roth need a protected entitlement or a distinct
impairment of liberty? Id. at 569–578.
Rule and Holding
Procedural due process applies when government deprives a person of protected liberty or property. At the
threshold, the inquiry concerns the nature of the interest, rather than simply balancing its importance against
the government’s convenience. A property interest requires a legitimate claim of entitlement grounded in
independent rules or understandings, such as state law, rather than desire, need, or unilateral expectation. Such
interests can arise from statutes, express terms, or implied assurances; formal tenure is not the only possible
source. Id. at 570–572, 576–578.
Roth’s appointment secured employment only through June 30, 1969, and no statute, policy, rule, or
established understanding in this record secured renewal. Nonrenewal without damaging charges or a
restriction foreclosing other employment also did not establish a liberty deprivation. He therefore was not
entitled to summary judgment requiring reasons and a hearing. The ruling did not authorize retaliation for
protected speech, decide that allegation, or deny that different nonrenewal circumstances could implicate
liberty or property. Id. at 573–579 & nn.12–16.
Judgment
The Court reversed the Seventh Circuit’s judgment and remanded for further proceedings consistent with its
opinion. Roth lost the partial summary judgment on his procedural claim. The Court did not award damages,
order reinstatement, or find a constitutionally valid motive for the university’s decision; the underlying
free-speech dispute remained unresolved at this stage. Id. at 568–569 n.5, 574–575, 579.
Reasoning
The district court had balanced Roth’s substantial concern in reemployment against the university’s interest in
deciding summarily. Stewart considered that approach premature. Balancing can help determine the form of
required process after a protected interest is identified. It cannot replace the initial showing that government
has deprived the claimant of something within the constitutional categories of liberty or property. Roth’s
understandable need for continued employment did not alone answer that question. Id. at 570–571.

The Court simultaneously rejected the earlier categorical distinction between governmental rights and
privileges. Public benefits and employment can be protected even when they are not traditional tangible
property, and liberty extends beyond freedom from physical restraint. The issue was therefore not whether
public employment can ever receive constitutional protection. It was which aspect of Roth’s interest was
secured, for what period, and whether nonrenewal had consequences falling within protected liberty. Id. at
571–572 & nn.9–11.
The liberty analysis examined what the State had done in connection with nonrenewal. An accusation seriously
injuring reputation, such as dishonesty or immorality, could require an opportunity to refute the charge. A
restriction barring the individual from a broader range of employment could likewise implicate occupational
liberty. Neither circumstance was established here. The Court distinguished loss of one position from a
state-imposed obstacle to pursuing other work. Id. at 573–575.
That distinction was tied to the record. The lower courts assumed nonretention would significantly damage
Roth’s later career prospects, but the majority found no evidentiary support for the proposed foreclosure of
opportunity. It also explained that being somewhat less attractive to another employer because of one
nonrenewal would not itself establish the relevant deprivation. The decision did not require a court to ignore
actual governmental blacklisting or sufficiently damaging charges when supported by the facts. Id. at 574 n.13.
The purpose of a hearing also depends on the protected interest. When the deprivation concerns a damaging
accusation, a hearing allows the person to clear his name. Successfully doing so does not necessarily create a
substantive right to the position: the employer may remain free to decline renewal for another reason. This
qualification prevents a liberty-based procedural remedy from becoming an automatic grant of tenure. Id. at
573 n.12.
The property analysis turned to existing sources of entitlement. Welfare recipients may have claims grounded
in eligibility standards; tenured employees may rely on tenure provisions; employees dismissed midterm may
rely on the term’s promise; and even an employee without formal tenure may have an implied assurance of
continued service. These examples showed why the label attached to the position was not decisive. The
operative question was whether a rule or understanding actually supported a claim to the disputed benefit. Id. at
576–577.
A claimant need not win the eventual merits dispute before receiving process. The Court explained that
Goldberg’s recipients had an entitlement framework supplied by statute even though a hearing was needed to
decide whether they met its conditions. By contrast, Roth lacked a comparable rule securing renewal at all. His
appointment created a protected interest for the stated year but no promise concerning the next year, and no
independent source supplied the missing continuation right. Id. at 577–578.
Roth suggested that most annual appointees were in practice rehired. The Court found no lower-court
determination that such practice established a binding common law of reemployment, and specifically referred
to Perry’s recognition that sufficiently established institutional understandings could matter. Frequent renewal
and legally supported entitlement were thus separate questions. The Court did not hold that only a written
tenure clause can ever generate property. Id. at 578 n.16; Perry, 408 U.S. at 601–603.
The First Amendment allegation remained a separate claim requiring factual development. The majority
declined to infer a procedural-hearing entitlement merely from a background of controversy and an unproven
retaliation allegation. It distinguished direct governmental restraints on expression from the interest in a
teaching job standing alone. This reasoning left the substantive prohibition on retaliation intact while rejecting
the lower courts’ prophylactic procedural rule on the present record. Roth, 408 U.S. at 574–575 & n.14.

Finally, the Court separated the constitutional minimum from institutional policy. It expressed no conclusion
about whether colleges should voluntarily give reasons or hearings to nontenured faculty. The lack of a federal
constitutional requirement on these facts therefore did not amount to a finding that unexplained nonrenewal
was sound university administration. Id. at 578–579.
Separate Opinions
Chief Justice Burger’s shared concurrence emphasized that an employment entitlement could arise under state
law from an express or implied contract. He would have federal courts abstain when uncertain state contract
law controlled whether the claimed entitlement existed, allowing state courts to resolve that antecedent
question. This suggested approach to future unclear-state-law cases was his concurrence, rather than an
additional abstention disposition ordered by the Roth majority. Id. at 603–604 (Burger, C.J., concurring in Roth
and Perry).
Justice Douglas would have affirmed. He emphasized academic freedom, the risk that nonrenewal could
conceal retaliation, and the practical effect of losing a teaching position on later employment. In his view,
notice of reasons and an opportunity to respond were important safeguards against pretext; an individual should
not have to begin a full lawsuit simply to discover why the government acted. He treated nonrenewal’s
practical consequences as comparable to dismissal and considered the interest in continued teaching sufficient
to require process. Id. at 579–586 (Douglas, J., dissenting).
Douglas also recognized that determining whether speech was protected and whether stated reasons were
genuine often required careful factual examination. He approved the lower courts’ approach allowing academic
procedures to occur while the federal court retained ultimate responsibility for constitutional questions. His
dissent accordingly combined a broader due process theory with concern that procedural secrecy could
undermine substantive speech protection. He did not purport to find the university’s disputed motive after a
trial that had never occurred. Id. at 582–587 & n.2/2.
Justice Marshall agreed with the majority’s general framework but interpreted both liberty and property more
broadly. He argued that governmental employers must act fairly rather than arbitrarily and that a person
seeking public employment was entitled to it unless the government could provide a reason for denial. In his
view, that interest required explanation and an opportunity to contest disputed facts even without an
independently established promise of renewal. This was a proposed broader conception of constitutional
entitlement, not the rule adopted by the Court. Id. at 587–590 (Marshall, J., dissenting).
Marshall rejected the prediction that reasons and hearings would necessarily cripple public administration.
Reasons should already exist for legitimate decisions; suitable summary procedures could address large
numbers of requests. He further argued that procedural regularity can expose innocent errors and discourage
arbitrary decisions even when it cannot eliminate every deliberate abuse. The value of procedure, in this
account, extended to improving governmental decisionmaking and public confidence. Id. at 590–592.
Justice Brennan, joined by Douglas, adopted Marshall’s conclusion that both teachers were entitled to reasons
and an opportunity to respond. He would have affirmed Roth’s favorable summary judgment and modified
Perry’s remand to direct corresponding procedural relief. His short opinion also agreed with Perry’s separate
First Amendment holding. Justice Powell did not participate in either decision. Id. at 604; Perry, 408 U.S. at
594, 603.
