# Weintraub v. Krobatsch
64 N.J. 445, 317 A.2d 68 (1974) | Contracts

## Holding and disposition
The New Jersey Supreme Court reversed summary judgments requiring home buyers to forfeit their $4,250 deposit to the seller and pay a $2,550 broker's commission. Their allegations of a seller's deliberate concealment or nondisclosure of a substantial, latent cockroach infestation entitled them to a full trial on equitable rescission. The court rejected a categorical rule immunizing a knowing seller merely because no affirmative representation was made. It did not itself find knowledge or fraud, rescind the contract, or finally award a returned deposit. Acting Chief Justice Jacobs wrote the March 19, 1974 opinion; Jacobs, Hall, Sullivan, Pashman, Clifford, and Judge Conford voted for reversal and remand, with none opposed. (445–446, 449–450, 455–457.)

## Contract and disputed discovery
Mrs. Weintraub owned a six-year-old house in Englishtown and listed it with the Serafin Agency. The Krobatsches inspected it while the lights were on and found it suitable. Their June 30, 1971 contract set a $42,500 price and recited inspection, satisfaction with physical condition, absence of representations, and no seller responsibility for present or future condition. They deposited $4,250 with the broker in escrow. At their request the seller had the house fumigated. A subsequent fire caused damage, but they were prepared to address that by adjustment at closing. These facts were recounted on review of summary judgment, with doubts and favorable inferences resolved for the buyers. (446–447.)

On an August evening before closing, the buyers entered the vacant house and reported seeing cockroaches everywhere upon turning on the lights. Their lawyer immediately notified the seller that they considered the infestation, despite recent extermination, to render the house unfit for habitation and sought rescission. An exterminator later confirmed infestation throughout the house, saying that two treatments separated by twenty-one days could eliminate it for a relatively modest charge, but carpeting would have to be removed to treat all affected areas. The relatively modest treatment estimate did not conclusively resolve the infestation's materiality. (447–448, 455–456.)

The buyers claimed the seller knew of the problem and kept every light illuminated during earlier inspections because the insects were nocturnal. Their lawyer argued that a resident could not have lived amid such an infestation without knowing. The seller denied knowledge. The appellate court accepted the buyers' allegation only for purposes of defeating summary judgment; it left actual knowledge and concealment for the trial judge. The buyers acknowledged that their fraudulent-nondisclosure theory would fail if the seller genuinely lacked knowledge. (448–450, 455.)

## Proceedings and parties' positions
The seller rejected rescission and sued, initially seeking specific performance but later limiting her claim to the deposit as damages. The broker counterclaimed for its commission. The Law Division rejected the buyers' summary-judgment motion, granted the seller judgment for $4,250, and directed release of the escrow to satisfy it, while reserving the broker's claim for trial. The Appellate Division affirmed the seller's judgment and additionally awarded the broker $2,550 on the premise that the buyers had defaulted by refusing to close. No oral testimony had been taken; the record consisted of meager pleadings and conclusory affidavits. (446, 448–449.)

Before the Supreme Court, the buyers sought a trial on fraud entitling them to rescind, which if established would negate liability to seller and broker and support return of their deposit. The seller argued that, even assuming knowledge, an arm's-length seller had no duty to disclose. She invoked the rule illustrated by Swinton, where bare nondisclosure of termites did not support the purchaser's damages pleading. (449–450.)

## Disclosure, inspection language, and equitable justification
The court drew on New Jersey authority recognizing that silence can be fraudulent where a party is bound in conscience and duty to disclose. It distinguished an authority involving no proven or inferable seller knowledge and explicitly rejected Swinton's categorical approach as inconsistent with its own sense of justice and fair dealing. Its discussion of possible developments in Massachusetts law was not a holding that Swinton had been overruled there. The governing holding was New Jersey's decision to allow this particular equitable claim to be tried. (449–451.)

A series of persuasive authorities concerned concealed termites, filled ground, nighttime absence of water, and latent defects affecting a broker's duties. Together they supported restricting caveat emptor where a transaction participant knowingly withheld a material latent condition. The court treated this contract's inspection and present-condition language as comparable to clauses that had not barred claims of fraudulent concealment. Its own builder-vendor and landlord cases reinforced the move toward fair dealing, including a duty to reveal a known material latent condition unobservable by the other party. The opinion did not convert sellers into insurers of all unknown defects, or dispense with materiality and knowledge. (451–456.)

Two questions remained for trial. First, the buyers needed to establish extensive infestation, seller awareness, and deliberate concealment or nondisclosure because revealing the condition could defeat the transaction. Second, even if that conduct were proved, the judge had to decide whether its significance justified rescission. Minor conditions ordinarily disregarded by reasonable participants would not warrant intervention. Cockroaches did not necessarily cause structural injury like termites, but the alleged magnitude and repulsiveness could support equitable relief. The buyers' readiness to accommodate intervening fire damage, contrasted with their immediate response to infestation, informed that assessment without conclusively deciding it. Both factual and legal disputes were to be resolved by the trial judge because the matter was primarily equitable. (455–456.)

## Exact result and assigned comparison
The judgment entered in the Appellate Division was reversed and the case remanded for a full trial. There was no separate writing. The six-person voting list must be taken from this opinion's final page, rather than the adjoining prior case's seven votes on its opening report page. (457.)
