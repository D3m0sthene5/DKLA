# Wood v. Boynton
64 Wis. 265, 25 N.W. 42 (1885) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
Justice Taylor delivered the Wisconsin Supreme Court’s October 13, 1885 decision affirming judgment for the jewelry-business defendants. Wood sued to recover possession of an uncut diamond, alleging value of $1,000. After hearing all the evidence, the circuit judge directed a defense verdict. Her motion for a new trial was denied, and she appealed the judgment. The Supreme Court held that the sale remained effective: her tender of the price and interest did not restore ownership or establish a right to recover possession. No dissent, separate writing, or numerical division is reported. (265, 268, 270, 273.)

## What the parties said and knew
Wood possessed a small, straw-colored stone, approximately the size and shape of a canary bird’s egg, with one end somewhat pointed. She did not know its character or value. In September or October, while having jewelry repaired, she asked Samuel B. Boynton what it was. She told him someone had suggested a topaz, and he said it might be. Asked its value, he said he did not know but would offer one dollar to keep it as a specimen. She initially declined because it was attractive. (268–269.)

Around December 28, 1883, needing money, she returned and said she had brought the topaz. Boynton asked what he had offered; she answered one dollar, which he paid. Later the stone proved to be a rough diamond worth approximately $700. This identified value differs from the $1,000 alleged in her action. She tendered the one dollar with ten cents interest and demanded the stone; the defendants refused. (268–269.)

The Court emphasized that this account came from Wood’s own testimony and that she supplied no other witness to the transaction. Boynton’s version did not materially differ or provide more favorable evidence for her. He testified that he had never seen an uncut diamond, that cut diamonds were quite different, and that he neither knew nor thought the stone was a diamond when he bought it. The Court found no evidence supporting concealed superior knowledge. Jewelry dealing alone did not supply that missing proof in this record. (269–272.)

## Arguments about fraud and mistake
Wood argued that the enormous disparity between price and admitted value was evidence of fraud to be considered with the surrounding circumstances. Her counsel also characterized the error as a vital mistake about the kind or species of the thing, analogous to a sale by description of an article differing in kind from that described. The defense distinguished mistakes concerning the actual subject sold from uncertainty about qualities or value and argued that the available means of inquiry were shared. These were the reported arguments, not additional factfindings about either party’s expertise. (265–267.)

The Court framed the controlling issue as title. Sale and delivery of the particular stone for one dollar had passed ownership to the buyers. Tender and demand could restore it only if Wood had a legal basis to undo the sale. For an action at law to recover the article, the Court identified fraud inducing the transaction or a mistake in delivery such that the article delivered was not the article sold. It explained that the latter situation was not strictly rescission at all: title to the wrongly delivered article had never passed through the actual bargain. (270.)

## Identity of the article versus an unexpectedly valuable bargain
There was no substitution or mistaken delivery. Wood displayed the same stone that the defendants bought and received when they paid. Both sides were ignorant of its intrinsic character and value. Her suggestion that it might be topaz was tentative, not an established contractual warranty of a known classification; Boynton’s matching uncertainty did not amount to an assurance that induced her to sell. She had kept the stone for some time and made inquiries, then chose to sell without further investigation. Discovering that the transaction was financially unfavorable did not alone permit repudiation. (270–271.)

The Court tested its reasoning with the reverse case: suppose the owner honestly said she had been told the stone was a diamond, disclaimed personal knowledge, and received $500, but it proved to be an inexpensive topaz. The buyer could not undo that bargain merely for mistake or breach of a nonexistent warranty. Fraud would require proof that the seller falsely described what she had been told or knew the diamond claim was false. This hypothetical illustrated the distinction between an uncertain belief and a misleading assertion of knowledge; its figures were not findings about Wood’s transaction. (271.)

Nor did the price disparity establish the missing fraud. The value was open to both parties’ investigation, neither knew it, and the evidence indicated that both regarded the price as adequate. Later discovery of hundreds of times greater value did not show that Boynton had wrongfully procured the sale. The Court expressly reserved whether inadequacy might have some influence in a different equitable action to avoid a sale. Its decided issue was whether this evidence established fraud or mistake sufficient to rescind and recover the stone at law. (272–273.)

The final affirmance left the directed defense verdict intact. It was not an order to return the diamond upon repayment, a damages award to Wood, or a finding that Boynton deliberately exploited knowledge that he concealed. The Court acknowledged her unfortunate bargain but found no legally sufficient ground for the particular relief sought. (273.)
