# Students for Fair Admissions, Inc. v. President and Fellows of Harvard College

**600 U.S. 181 (2023), decided June 29, 2023. Supreme Court of the United States.** No. 20–1199, together with **Students for Fair Admissions, Inc. v. University of North Carolina**, No. 21–707. This one published opinion resolves two distinct judgments: First Circuit review in Harvard's case and certiorari before judgment in UNC's. Assigned material: November continuation casebook, PDF 133, printed 1190, a developed editorial account of associational standing. Pinpoints use each writing's original slip pages, rather than an inferred continuous reporter-page conversion.

**Judgments and participation.** The Court reversed both the First Circuit judgment upholding Harvard's admissions program and the Middle District of North Carolina judgment upholding UNC's program. Chief Justice Roberts wrote for six Justices: himself, Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, and Barrett. Sotomayor and Kagan dissented in both cases. Jackson participated in UNC, joining Sotomayor's dissent as applied there and writing a UNC-specific dissent joined by Sotomayor and Kagan; she took no part in Harvard. Accordingly, the votes were **6–2 for Harvard and 6–3 for UNC**. Thomas, Gorsuch (joined by Thomas), and Kavanaugh wrote three concurrences. This brief preserves both docket identities; publication in one instrument does not make them one underlying judgment. (Court 40; syllabus 8; separate-writing headings.)

## The admissions systems and litigation

Harvard, a private college receiving federal funds, used several stages of competitive undergraduate admissions. First readers scored academic, extracurricular, athletic, school-support, personal, and overall qualities. Race could affect the overall rating. Regional subcommittees recommended applicants to a full committee of approximately forty members, which discussed racial composition and voted individually on applications. Tentative admissions were then reduced through a final process called the lop. The lop list identified legacy status, recruited-athlete status, financial-aid eligibility, and race. The trial court found that race provided a determinative tip for a significant share of admitted African-American and Hispanic applicants. (Court 2–3.)

UNC, a public university, initially had admissions readers rate multiple dimensions and recommend admission or denial. Race was one factor in their review and could provide a substantial plus in individual cases. Most initial recommendations were provisionally final, but a school-group review committee examined every initial decision and could also consider race. The parties disagreed about the characterization and significance of these racial considerations; the Court and dissents drew different inferences from the trial findings and statistics. (Court 3–5 & n.1; Sotomayor 28–35 & nn.24–29; Jackson 16–21 & nn.76–94.)

Students for Fair Admissions, a nonprofit founded in 2014 to defend civil rights and equal protection, sued both institutions in November 2014. Its Harvard challenge invoked Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964; its UNC challenge included the Fourteenth Amendment. The Harvard trial lasted fifteen days with thirty witnesses; UNC's trial lasted eight days. Both district courts upheld the programs under the Court's admissions precedents. The First Circuit affirmed Harvard's judgment. The Supreme Court granted ordinary certiorari in Harvard and certiorari before judgment in UNC, so there was no Fourth Circuit merits judgment to reverse. (Court 6; Sotomayor 27 & nn.22–23.)

The schools defended individualized, holistic review and the educational benefits of diversity, maintaining that available race-neutral alternatives would not adequately preserve their missions. SFFA sought abandonment of the precedents permitting race-conscious admissions and alternatively disputed narrow tailoring. The dissent stressed the trial findings rejecting its proposed alternatives. The Court ultimately invalidated the programs under strict scrutiny, restrictions against racial negatives and stereotypes, and the requirement that racial preferences end. (Court 22–34; Sotomayor 27–35.)

## Assigned holding: associational standing

Before reaching admissions law, the Court addressed UNC's argument that SFFA was not a genuine membership organization when it filed suit. Article III requires an injury in fact fairly traceable to the defendant and likely redressable by the requested judgment. An organization can assert its own injury or represent its members. Under **Hunt**, representative standing requires that members could sue in their own right, that the interests are germane to the organization's purpose, and that neither the claim nor requested relief requires the individual members' participation. Respondents did not contest that SFFA satisfied that three-part test. Their objection was an additional proposed prerequisite: members must control and fund the organization. (Court 7–8.)

The Court rejected that interpretation of Hunt. Hunt involved a state apple commission that had no conventional members. Its growers' and dealers' control, service, and financing demonstrated that they were effectively members, allowing a nontraditional entity to represent them. That analysis supplied a functional substitute for membership; it did not impose further governance requirements on every voluntary membership association. SFFA had identifiable voluntary members and represented them in good faith. At filing, the Harvard litigation involved an incorporated nonprofit with forty-seven voluntary members. The UNC record identified four rejected applicants who had joined, supported SFFA's mission, received updates, and had opportunities to provide input. The Court therefore found no need for additional scrutiny into the organization's operation. (Court 8–9.)

The assignment accurately emphasizes this distinction. The result does not dispense with Hunt's three conditions, authorize any advocate to sue without an injured member, or decide the separate question of standing based on an organization's own injury. It resolves the proposed control-and-funding restriction on a genuine voluntary membership organization. The surrounding assigned discussions of Brown Group, Flyers Rights, and American Physicians concern other decisions and are context rather than additional SFFA holdings.

## Merits: why these programs failed

Roberts traced the Equal Protection Clause's development through Reconstruction, segregation, and Brown, and emphasized equal legal treatment of persons regardless of race. Racial classifications receive strict scrutiny: they must serve a compelling interest and be necessary through narrow tailoring. The Court discussed specific remediation of identified unconstitutional or unlawful discrimination and prevention of serious imminent prison violence as recognized compelling interests outside the higher-education cases. This opinion does not purport to decide every possible racial classification in every setting. (Court 10–16 & n.3.)

The Court evaluated Harvard under equal-protection standards because existing precedent treated discrimination violating the Clause by a federally funded institution as violating Title VI. No party asked it to reconsider that linkage. Harvard was therefore not treated as a public institution directly subject to the Fourteenth Amendment merely because it accepted funds. Gorsuch's independent statutory analysis was a concurrence, not the Court's chosen ground. (Court 6 n.2; Gorsuch 19–25.)

Roberts reviewed Bakke's fractured decision and Powell's permitted diversity rationale, then Grutter's approval of individualized consideration subject to constraints. He treated three conditions as indispensable: strict scrutiny, no racial stereotyping or negative use of race, and a meaningful end point. The Court found the current programs failed each. (Court 16–22.)

First, the universities' objectives—such as leadership, knowledge, citizenship, empathy, and exchange of ideas—were commendable but insufficiently focused and measurable for meaningful strict-scrutiny review. Courts could not determine what degree of racial preference was necessary or when the asserted benefits had been attained. The racial categories were also overbroad, undefined, or underinclusive, weakening the connection between classifications and educational goals. Academic deference could not replace judicial examination of that connection. (Court 22–26 & nn.4–5.)

Second, the Court rejected the assertion that race only provided a positive factor without disadvantaging anyone. Admissions to a finite class were zero-sum: a racial advantage could reduce other applicants' opportunities. It also viewed preferences grounded in race itself as relying on assumptions about the perspectives associated with racial groups, contrary to the individualized dignity protected by equal protection and Grutter's prohibition on stereotyping. The dissent disputed both the legal inference and its portrayal of the actual holistic systems; those criticisms must remain attributed rather than treated as agreed facts. (Court 27–30 & n.6; Sotomayor 43–50; Jackson 16–21.)

Third, the Court found no meaningful termination point. Representation-based measures risked racial balancing; the educational-benefit objectives were too indefinite to establish completion. Grutter's expectation about twenty-five years did not license unlawful preferences until 2028, and periodic review did not justify continuing indefinitely. Harvard's comparison of racial composition across years, illustrated by the chart on Court page 31 and defended differently by the dissent, figured in this discussion. Neither university identified an actual end date. (Court 30–34 & n.7; Sotomayor 34–35 & n.29, 53–56 & n.37.)

Finally, the Court allowed consideration of an applicant's account of how race affected their individual life, including discrimination or inspiration, when tied to qualities such as courage, leadership, or particular abilities. It prohibited using essays or other devices simply to re-create the unlawful racial-preference system. This is neither a categorical ban on mentioning race nor an unrestricted permission to award preferences whenever an essay mentions it. (Court 39–40.)

Military academies were expressly **not decided**: none was a party, and the Court acknowledged potentially distinct interests. The reservation is not an affirmative holding that their programs are lawful. The Court also noted that neither university defended its present admissions policy as a remedy for its own past discrimination. (Court 22 n.4, 36 n.8.)

## Concurring and dissenting reasoning

**Thomas.** Joining the Court fully, Thomas offered an originalist account of equal citizenship and a colorblind constitutional rule. His extended discussion examined Reconstruction legislation, historical educational segregation, the statutory argument, and remedial classifications. He rejected using universities' asserted benefits as sufficient justification for differential racial treatment and criticized deference to alleged discriminators. He also argued that preferences could produce academic mismatch, stigma, resentment, and reduced individuality; these were his arguments, contested at length by Sotomayor, rather than independent empirical findings adopted throughout by the Court. Thomas regarded Grutter as effectively overruled and defended race-neutral routes to educational opportunity. (Thomas 1–32, 39–58 & nn.1–12; Sotomayor 56–61.)

**Gorsuch, joined by Thomas.** Gorsuch read Title VI as independently prohibiting a federal-funds recipient from intentionally treating a similarly situated person worse even partly because of race. Using textual and but-for-causation reasoning associated with Bostock, he rejected benign motive, other contributing factors, or supposed group-wide benefit as defenses. His opinion reviewed the systems' categories and preferential practices, but he stressed that statutory liability did not require resolving every dispute about their magnitude or motivation. He questioned treating the differently worded Title VI and Equal Protection Clause as interchangeable and criticized the interpretive moves in Bakke. Roberts instead retained the established equal-protection linkage without reconsideration. (Gorsuch 1–25 & nn.1–9; Court 6 n.2.)

**Kavanaugh.** Kavanaugh joined the Court and separately emphasized the temporary character of permissible racial classifications. He read Grutter's twenty-five-year expectation as an important temporal limit, viewing the approaching college class of 2028 as relevant and noting that neither school defended a definite later termination. He acknowledged continuing discrimination and the availability of race-neutral responses to its consequences. His temporal explanation should not obscure the Court's separate conclusion that these particular programs already failed substantive limits. (Kavanaugh 1–8 & n.1.)

**Sotomayor, joined by Kagan and by Jackson only for UNC.** Sotomayor understood Reconstruction, Brown, and the admissions precedents as permitting limited race-conscious measures to achieve genuine equality in a society still shaped by segregation. She emphasized academic freedom, educational benefits, trial findings, and the practical failure of SFFA's proposed alternatives. She argued that the Court silently displaced settled precedent without the justification and reliance analysis stare decisis required. In her view, requiring greater measurement while condemning numerical attention created an impossible framework; the treatment of individual essays largely described what holistic review already did. She rejected the majority's portrayal of the programs as simple racial preferences, disputed its statistical and stereotyping inferences, criticized the uncertain temporal requirements, and warned about reduced educational diversity and professional pipelines. (Sotomayor 1–69, all forty-two numbered notes and participation asterisk.)

**Jackson, joined by Sotomayor and Kagan, UNC only.** Jackson supplemented the principal dissent with an account of how law and policy created and transmitted racial disparities in wealth, education, housing, and health. Her John-and-James applicants were explanatory hypotheticals, not actual litigants. She described UNC's forty-criteria holistic process as assessing individual circumstances, with race disclosed voluntarily and no automatic racial plus or quota, and disagreed with the majority's reading of its findings. She argued that recognizing race-linked opportunity differences could produce fairer assessments of merit and benefits beyond the campus; mandated race blindness would perpetuate disparities. Her lengthy historical and empirical footnotes support her dissent's account and are not separate Court findings. (Jackson 1–29, all 106 numbered notes and participation asterisk.)
