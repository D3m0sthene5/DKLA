# Maxton Builders, Inc. v. Lo Galbo
68 N.Y.2d 373, 502 N.E.2d 184, 509 N.Y.S.2d 507 (1986) | Contracts

## Decision and judgment
The New York Court of Appeals upheld the seller's right to recover a traditional 10% down payment after the buyers defaulted on a real-estate contract. It first held that the buyers had failed to exercise their limited cancellation right in the specified manner and time. It then declined to abandon the longstanding New York rule permitting the seller to retain such a payment following an unexcused default. Chief Judge Wachtler wrote the November 18, 1986 opinion; Judges Meyer, Simons, Kaye, Alexander, Titone, and Hancock, Jr., concurred. The court affirmed, with costs, the Appellate Division order granting the seller summary judgment for the down payment. (68 N.Y.2d 373, 375–378, 381–382.)

## Purchase, tax rider, and attempted cancellation
The Lo Galbos contracted on August 3, 1983, to buy a newly built house from Maxton for $210,000. They tendered a $21,000 check as the escrow down payment. A handwritten rider requested by the buyers allowed them to cancel if real-estate taxes based on a full assessment at that price exceeded $3,500, provided they gave the seller written notice within three days; cancellation would entail return of the escrow funds. On August 4, Cynthia Lo Galbo and Maxton's president visited the assessor's office and obtained an estimate above $3,500. (Id. at 376.)

The buyers' lawyer orally informed the seller's lawyer that the buyers wished to cancel. A certified cancellation letter was mailed on Friday, August 5, but the seller's lawyer received it August 9. The buyers also stopped payment on the deposit check. Maxton sued on September 20 for the amount of the down payment. It later sold the house on December 20 for the same $210,000 price, but paid a $12,000 brokerage fee for the resale. It alleged that the breach also caused mortgage-related carrying charges, interest, taxes, insurance, and other losses exceeding the deposit, although it elected to seek the deposit rather than a separately calculated damages award. (Id. at 376–377.)

## Lower courts and competing theories
Both sides sought summary judgment. The buyers maintained that the tax rider entitled them to cancel and that they had properly done so. Maxton asserted that cancellation did not satisfy the rider and that the buyers' default entitled it to the down payment under Lawrence v. Miller. Special Term deemed cancellation ineffective but denied Maxton summary judgment on recovery, reasoning that the size of the payment and the successful resale raised a factual issue whether claiming the deposit imposed a penalty. The Appellate Division modified and granted Maxton summary judgment for the deposit, applying New York's rule even when the seller resold at the same or a higher price. The buyers appealed on both cancellation and damages. A separately dismissed cross complaint was no longer at issue, as the first footnote expressly states. (Id. at 375–377 & n. 1.)

## Written receipt was required within the cancellation period
The buyers argued that, because the contract did not expressly make time of the essence, reasonable notice sufficed. In their view, mailing written notice and communicating orally within three days accomplished that. The court rejected the argument: when an agreement requires written notice within a stated period, the writing must actually arrive within that period. The parties had bargained for a limited right to cancel; the buyers did not exercise it as agreed. The late writing therefore left the contract in force, making the buyers' refusal to perform a breach. The tax estimate's satisfying the rider's substantive threshold did not independently cancel the sale. (Id. at 377–378.)

## Restitution criticism and the distinction for traditional down payments
The buyers alternatively sought to limit Maxton to actual damages and asked the court to reconsider Lawrence v. Miller. Wachtler acknowledged criticism of the broad common-law rule denying restitution to parties in default. As applied to substantial or nearly completed performance, that rule could make forfeiture grow as the breach became smaller. The modern approach instead generally allowed recovery of the net benefit exceeding the injured party's loss, with the defaulting claimant bearing the burden of proof. (Id. at 378–380.)

But the court distinguished the reform of that general rule from the particular practice concerning real-estate down payments. The modern Restatement itself recognized liquidated damages and understandings or usages providing for retention of money upon breach, with an illustration involving a 10% first installment. The court reviewed developments allowing restitution in other fields, and New York's unsuccessful legislative proposal to change the real-estate rule. That proposed reform would itself have permitted retention of a reasonable percentage; the second footnote explained the commission's concerns about proving losses, excluding some injuries from legally recoverable damages, and encouraging contract breaking. These observations supplied context rather than an enacted alternative rule governing this sale. (Id. at 379–381 & n. 2.)

## Why the court retained the New York rule
The court gave particular weight to stability where settled rules structure contractual rights and reliance. Although restitution for defaulting parties had advantages generally, the court found it no better or more workable for real-estate down payments near 10% than New York's established usage. If the contract expressly allowed retention, such a modest amount would probably survive liquidated-damages analysis given uncertainty about actual loss. Even without such a clause, a defaulting buyer under a proposed net-benefit approach would face a difficult burden showing that the payment exceeded the seller's damages. The court thought changing the rule would often extend litigation without saving anyone from real financial loss. (Id. at 381–382.)

Here the buyers made no effort to show that actual losses were below those the seller alleged or that they had conferred a net benefit. The court also regarded real-estate sales as generally negotiated at arm's length and treated the bargaining table as the place to alter the established consequences, absent a real risk of overreaching. It therefore affirmed the award of the $21,000 down payment even though payment on the tendered check had been stopped and the property had resold for the same price. This was not an adjudicated itemization proving that every claimed carrying cost equaled a particular figure. The decisive ground was the retained down-payment rule and the buyers' ineffective cancellation. (Id. at 376–378, 381–382.)

## Limits and reading scope
The third footnote expressly reserved installment payments beyond a 10% down payment; the court did not declare a right to retain every payment of any size.
