# Farmworker Justice Fund, Inc. v. Brock; Farmworkers Justice Fund, Inc. v. Brock
811 F.2d 613 (D.C. Cir. 1987), vacated as moot, 817 F.2d 890 (D.C. Cir. 1987) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Long regulatory history and the challenged decision

In September 1972, El Congreso petitioned the Labor Secretary for field-sanitation requirements covering drinking water, handwashing facilities, and toilets. A December 1973 suit challenged inaction. An advisory committee approved a standard in December 1974, and the district court ordered further action in 1975. OSHA proposed a rule in April 1976 acknowledging health hazards from inadequate sanitation, yet did not finalize it. Earlier appellate decisions recognized the Secretary's discretion to order priorities while requiring a timetable and rejecting indefinite disappearance of an initiated standard. The 1982 settlement then required a good-faith effort to complete the process within thirty-one months, with further delay subject to explanation and court approval. These earlier proceedings are background to the distinct 1987 judgments, not additional judgments silently certified by this brief. (811 F.2d at 614–617.)

OSHA reopened the inquiry in 1983 and proposed a revised standard in March 1984 covering farms employing at least eleven workers in hand labor. Congressional appropriations restrictions barred OSHA spending on farms with ten or fewer employees and no temporary labor camp. A granted extension brought the process to April 1985, when the Secretary decided against a federal standard, invoking priorities and federalism. Secretary Brock later reversed that position on October 21, 1985, finding unacceptable health risks and a need for further regulation, but allowed states eighteen months to provide adequate protection. If their response was inadequate, OSHA would issue its standard within another six months. The petition challenged this conditional two-year postponement. (617–619; 627–628 n. 16.)

The majority treated the October decision as materially different from the April refusal. In its view, Brock had already concluded that protection required regulation regardless of competing priorities; the remaining question concerned delaying that protection in hope of state action. Williams disputed that reading, considering the commitment conditional and resource allocation still central. That disagreement frames much of the case. (623–624 & n. 11; Williams opinion, 638–640.)

## February 6, 1987: review was available

Chief Judge Wald wrote the merits opinion, joined by Senior District Judge Will; Judge Williams concurred on reviewability and dissented on the merits and remedy. All three agreed that Heckler v. Chaney did not foreclose review of this decision. Will separately concurred, and Williams supplied a full partial concurrence and dissent. (613–614, 633–635.)

The majority distinguished the bounds of statutory authority from discretionary judgment within those bounds. Courts must examine whether an agency relied on considerations Congress permitted; even a rational policy could be unlawful if the statute prohibited that basis. Review for abuse of discretion, by contrast, remained narrow and could not substitute judicial judgment for agency expertise. Chaney's protection of many nonenforcement decisions reflected the absence of manageable standards and expertise-intensive choices; it did not immunize agency action contrary to law. (619–623 & nn. 5–9.)

The majority did not adopt a general presumption for every conditional withdrawal of a proposed rule. This decision followed completed rulemaking and promised a rule if a stated condition failed. The court proceeded to ask whether the OSH Act provided manageable standards for reviewing the particular postponement. Settlement-enforcement questions were unnecessary to the majority's statutory resolution; the current proceeding was separately docketed from the older district-court action. (619 n. 4; 623 n. 10.)

## Impermissible rationales and permissible consideration of state protection

The majority understood the preference for state action partly to reflect a general conception of appropriate federal-state roles. Congress had already made that institutional choice in the OSH Act. The statute contemplated federal leadership and a specific mechanism for state responsibility under § 18, requiring approved plans at least as effective as federal standards. The Secretary could not withhold a needed standard simply because he preferred a different allocation of governmental responsibility. Statutory text and legislative history supported that limit. (624–628.)

The small-farm exclusion supplied no additional authority to withhold protection from workers OSHA could regulate. State standards might cover small farms, but the October decision measured adequate state action against the proposed federal standard, which covered larger farms; it did not require states to protect the otherwise excluded workers. The Secretary could not gamble with covered workers' protection to encourage regulation of people Congress had placed outside his direct jurisdiction. The majority gave the agency the benefit of ambiguity concerning preemption and assumed the narrower, legally correct understanding that federal large-farm regulation would not eliminate state protection of small farms. It still found the asserted rationale inadequate. (627–630 & nn. 16–22.)

Existing or genuinely imminent equivalent state protection, however, was a permissible consideration in determining need for a federal rule. Faced with uncertainty about Congress's intent, the majority accepted that reasonable interpretation under then-governing Chevron principles. A short postponement could be justified if the agency reasonably expected virtually all covered workers soon to receive equivalent state protection. This was different from an abstract preference for state government or speculative leverage involving excluded workers. (630–631 & n. 23.)

## Why the majority rejected this further delay

The Act's nationwide protective purpose, emphasis on speed when health was at stake, and requirement of explained agency determinations supplied review standards. The majority did not declare one hundred percent coverage an absolute threshold, but considered near-comprehensive coverage important where the Secretary acknowledged unacceptable risks. After more than a decade of proposed standards and acknowledged need, a further two-year postponement required support the court did not find. (631–632 & nn. 24–26.)

About twenty-five percent of workers within OSHA's relevant jurisdiction remained unprotected. Thirty-seven states lacked standards, and only two were developing them. Five new state standards over several prior years did not support expecting sufficiently broad action in eighteen months. Subsequent reports that twelve states would not regulate corroborated the concern, but the majority expressly rested its decision on the original record's inadequacy, not hindsight. It also distinguished percentages of all farmworkers from percentages within OSHA's jurisdiction: incorporating excluded small-farm workers distorted the asserted marginal gain from federal protection. (632–633 & n. 27.)

The February judgment **ordered the Secretary to issue the federal field-sanitation standard within thirty days from issuance of the mandate**, relying on APA § 706(1)'s authority to compel unlawfully withheld or unreasonably delayed action. The deadline therefore was tied to the mandate, not automatically to the date of the opinion. (633.)

## Will's concurrence and Williams's different approach

Will fully joined the majority and offered breach of the 1982 settlement as an additional ground. The bargain promised either a standard or a final refusal permitting prompt review. In his view, the October reversal of the April refusal again left the workers without either, mooted review of the earlier refusal, and prolonged the delay. He regarded this as affirmative maneuvering to defer legal obligations, not merely passive inaction. That contractual rationale was his separate position; the majority had not resolved settlement issues. (633–634.)

Williams agreed that rulemaking refusals differed from ad hoc nonenforcement and were reviewable, but emphasized Chaney's caution about reallocating scarce resources. He would review a stated legal basis while giving substantial deference to speculative policy comparisons. He did not think the Secretary had relied on an impermissible general federalism preference: the small-farm restriction, local agricultural variation, possible state coverage, and freeing inspectors for other serious hazards provided specific reasons for this strategy. The majority's view that priorities had already been resolved overlooked the commitment's conditional nature. (634–640.)

Williams did identify a legal error: he read the October decision as retaining the erroneous premise that a federal large-farm standard would preempt state small-farm standards. Congress's small-farm restriction could not sensibly strip states of their ability to protect those workers merely because OSHA regulated larger farms. He therefore **would remand for reconsideration with a correct preemption understanding**, rather than compel immediate promulgation. (640–641.)

He otherwise rejected the majority's finding of unreasonable delay and its compulsory-rule remedy. Predicting state response and comparing benefits from immediate farm regulation against broader enforcement priorities required expertise, and the record showed some potential for meaningful state action. Even an abuse finding should lead to reconsideration or accelerated proceedings, not a court's commitment of enforcement resources to a specific rule. He also rejected Will's settlement theory: the April determination met the extended timetable, the workers themselves requested reconsideration, and the agreement about timing did not authorize forcing the draft into law. His nine notes elaborate statutory restrictions, agency reasoning, resource comparisons, and the state-plan mechanism. (641–644; Williams nn. 1–9 at 635–641.)

## May 7, 1987: a distinct mootness vacatur

The Secretary issued field-sanitation standards and notified the court by an April 28 letter. Because the mandate had not issued, the same panel's May 7 order **vacated as moot the February opinion and judgment, Will's concurrence, and Williams's partial concurrence/dissent**. It also dismissed the panel rehearing petition as moot, terminated the case on the docket, and directed issuance of a certified copy of the order instead of a formal mandate. There was no separate writing or merits reconsideration in that order. (817 F.2d at 890.)

Thus the reported thirty-day command and the accompanying merits analyses were vacated before the mandate issued. They remain historically significant reasoning, with the vacatur attached; the order did not affirm either side's merits position. The assigned account's description of withdrawal as moot should be read with the actual order's express coverage of the judgment and all separate writings.
