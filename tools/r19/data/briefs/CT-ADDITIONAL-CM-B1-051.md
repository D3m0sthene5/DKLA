# International Filter Co. v. Conroe Gin, Ice & Light Co. — intermediate appeal
269 S.W. 210 (Tex. Civ. App.—Beaumont Jan. 30, 1925), rehearing denied Feb. 11, 1925 | Contracts

## Decision and setting

Justice Walker wrote the intermediate appellate opinion affirming a judgment that International Filter take nothing in its breach-of-contract suit. The court treated Conroe’s signed order as an offer to purchase a filter and International Filter’s president’s subsequent approval as an acceptance, but held that the acceptance had not been communicated before Conroe withdrew its offer. This is the earlier judgment discussed in the casebook’s Expressions of Acceptance note, not the later Commission of Appeals recommendation and Supreme Court reversal reproduced as the principal case. The original intermediate report identifies the Beaumont Court of Civil Appeals of Texas; the note’s “Ky. App.” parenthetical is erroneous. The published report contains one judicial opinion and no recorded dissent or numerical vote. Its caption records denial of rehearing and an editorial star records that a writ of error was granted April 8, 1925. (269 S.W. at 210–211, 214–216; C&M physical PDF265–266, printed220–221.)

## Transaction and correspondence

On February 10, 1920, Conroe’s manager, Henry Thompson, signed a proposal solicited by International Filter’s representative, W. W. Waterman. It concerned a Junior No.2 steel-tank water softener and filter, shipped from Chicago, to purify water of the character shown by a sample Conroe would supply. Although the document was expressed as the manufacturer’s proposal, it said it became a contract when accepted by the purchaser and approved by an executive officer at International Filter’s Chicago office. It also made the proposal subject to change without notice unless promptly accepted. Thompson’s notation requested shipment by March10. On February13, International Filter president P. N. Engel placed his dated approval on the paper. The February14 letter signed by M. B. Johnson acknowledged and thanked Conroe for its order, repeated the apparatus and shipment specifications, and asked Conroe promptly to ship a water sample for analysis. (Id. at 211.)

Conroe wrote on February28 that the late season and a possible capacity increase made it wish to cancel for the time being. International Filter replied March2 that the tank had been ordered and built and that its unusual small steel-tank configuration made cancellation unacceptable. Conroe reiterated on March4 that it could not accept the filter that season. The March9 response said the company had stopped work in compliance with Conroe’s request but would bill its costs, including selling expenses, unless Conroe instructed shipment. On April6, the manufacturer asserted that the softener had been practically completed when stopped, demanded the overdue $615 first installment, invoked acceleration of the remaining price, and threatened collection and storage expenses. Those letters stated International Filter’s position; the trial court did not accept its description of the work’s completion at the time of cancellation. The machine remained with International Filter and had never been shipped when the case was tried. (Id. at 211–214.)

## Evidence, trial findings, and procedure

Engel testified that the apparatus had no practical market apart from a purchaser with matching operating conditions. He compared it to prescription glasses assembled from standard parts for a particular customer. In a later deposition, he estimated $25 scrap value and a maximum $300 value if a suitable purchaser could be found with little adaptation expense. Thompson disputed the apparatus’s extraordinary character: a separate water-treatment unit suitable for an ice plant of roughly ten to forty tons could serve many plants, whether its tank was wood or steel. This disagreement mattered to the trial court’s alternative damages analysis. (Id. at 212–213.)

Thompson also testified that Waterman had drawn his attention to the prompt-acceptance clause, and that he told Waterman Conroe needed an immediate definite answer because the purchase was a rush order late in the season. Following a bench trial, Judge J. M. Combs found that no effective contract existed before cancellation. The February14 acknowledgment was the only pre-cancellation communication, and there was no evidence that Johnson was an executive officer or had been represented as one. The court found neither completed nor substantially completed preparation for shipment at cancellation, and no sufficient proof of the apparatus’s condition or reasonable value at that time. It rejected Conroe’s jurisdictional objection because the transaction contemplated interstate commerce, notwithstanding International Filter’s lack of Texas business authorization. It separately concluded that the damages proof would be inadequate even if a contract existed. International Filter appealed the take-nothing judgment. (Id. at 211, 213–214.)

## Issues and governing approach

The main questions were who made the operative offer, whether Engel’s approval accepted it, whether notice of that approval was necessary, and whether the February14 letter supplied either acceptance or notice. International Filter first described the purchaser as offeror in its appellate brief, then corrected its position in a supplemental brief to argue that its own proposal was the offer and Conroe’s signature the acceptance. The court determined the instrument’s legal function from the reserved home-office approval rather than its formal introductory wording. Conroe was proposing terms on which it would buy; the parties understood that the manufacturer still had to accept. (Id. at 214.)

The court distinguished an offer requesting an act from one requesting a promise. Its stated general rule required communication for the latter, while acknowledging that an offeror could dispense with notice through the terms or form of its offer. International Filter relied on that exception: the clause made the contract effective upon home-office approval. Conroe relied on the reservation of prompt acceptance, its ability to change the proposal without notice, and its insistence on an immediate answer. (Id. at 214–215.)

## Holding and reasoning

Engel’s approval was itself an acceptance. The appellate court expressly rejected the trial court’s contrary conclusion on that point: the familiar commercial endorsement communicated approval just as effectively as the word “accepted.” The defect lay in uncommunicated acceptance, not an inability of brief or informal words to indicate assent. (Id. at 214.)

The court read both clauses together. It reasoned that Conroe’s reserved ability to change its proposal would otherwise become meaningless unless it remained available until Conroe was notified of the manufacturer’s Chicago approval. If that construction was not compelled by the instrument, the provisions were at least ambiguous and should be construed against International Filter, which supplied the language. Thompson’s uncontradicted account of the immediate-answer discussion was admissible either because it restated the notification requirement or because it explained the ambiguity and the parties’ understanding. It did not impermissibly contradict a clear integrated writing. (Id. at 215.)

The February14 letter neither accepted the offer nor notified Conroe of Engel’s completed acceptance. It thanked Conroe for an order and sought the still-needed sample without declaring that the manufacturer had approved. The court relied on decisions distinguishing acknowledgment and a promise of attention from assent, including Courtney Shoe’s rejection of a similar order acknowledgment as an acceptance. Requesting the sample was especially important: International Filter retained the privilege to examine the water before agreeing to furnish a suitable machine, and the request did not indicate that the company had waived that privilege. The letter was consequently consistent with considering an unfinished proposal rather than communicating a binding approval. (Id. at 215–216.)

## Exact disposition and later-stage distinction

The court affirmed the trial judgment in all respects because no contract had been completed under its analysis. It did not reach International Filter’s appellate attacks on the alternative damages ground, explaining that they could not undermine the formation ground supporting affirmance. The disposition therefore does not establish an appellate holding that the $25/$300 valuation testimony was necessarily insufficient in a valid-contract case. (Id. at 216.)

The assigned principal case’s later disposition reverses this intermediate judgment and remands to the Court of Civil Appeals to address previously unconsidered questions. Its reasoning rejects the intermediate court’s notification construction and additionally treats the February14 letter as sufficient notice if notice were necessary. This brief preserves the intermediate court’s historical reasoning and result; those propositions should not be presented as the final resolution of the litigation or as a certification of current contract law. The later stage remains covered by the separate principal-case brief. (C&M physical PDF265, printed220; principal citation277 S.W.631.)
