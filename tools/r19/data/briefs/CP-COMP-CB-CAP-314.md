# Southern Construction Co., Inc. v. Pickard

**371 U.S. 57 (1962) · Supreme Court of the United States · November 5, 1962 · Civil Procedure**

**Assigned account:** CivPro.pdf, physical pages 383–384, printed pages 733–734: complete Note 4, including the Court's quoted rationale and the final editorial question before Note 5.

**Decision in one sentence.** Where federal law required related construction claims to be litigated in two different districts, Rule 13(a) did not require a common counterclaim to be asserted in whichever action received the first responsive pleading. 371 U.S. at 60–61.

## Facts and procedural path

Southern Construction was the federal government's prime contractor for rehabilitation of barracks at Fort Campbell, Tennessee, and Fort Benning, Georgia. Three contracts covered the Georgia work and one the Tennessee work. Southern furnished the Miller Act's performance and payment bonds, with Continental Casualty as surety. Samuel J. Pickard, doing business as Pickard Engineering, was the plumbing and heating subcontractor on both projects; Atlas Supply was his principal supplier. Id. at 57–58.

Pickard's men left the Tennessee work before completion in December 1955 and shortly afterward left the Georgia work. Atlas asserted unpaid accounts of $34,520 on Tennessee and $104,000 on Georgia. Following an August 1956 conference, Southern paid Atlas **$35,000 for a complete release covering both projects**. The Court's first note explains Southern's secondary liability to subcontractor suppliers under the Miller Act. Id. at 58 & n.1.

In December 1956 Pickard initially sued Southern and Continental in Georgia for amounts allegedly due on both projects. Southern answered and counterclaimed in January 1957, asserting expenditures exceeding the contract price and including the $35,000 Atlas payment. But the Miller Act required an action to be brought in the district where its contract was performed and nowhere else. In April 1957 Pickard therefore brought the Tennessee action and amended the Georgia complaint to remove the Tennessee claim. Id. at 58–59.

The Georgia action went to trial in 1959. The Tennessee district court later found that Southern had dropped the Atlas payment from its Georgia counterclaim before that trial. The Georgia case still lacked final judgment when the Supreme Court decided this case: Southern had obtained a new trial on its counterclaim in September 1961. Id. at 59. Thus this was not simply an attempt to evade a completed adverse judgment on the same payment.

In Tennessee Southern initially included the payment in an affirmative counterclaim. Pickard responded that it was barred by res judicata. Southern later waived affirmative relief and sought only a **$34,520 credit** against Pickard's Tennessee demand—the amount Atlas originally attributed to that project. The district court allowed that credit and found Pickard entitled to no recovery. The Sixth Circuit reversed on the credit issue. Id.

The appellate court treated the unallocated $35,000 settlement as a potential compulsory counterclaim in either action. It reasoned that when Southern filed the Georgia responsive pleading, the payment was not yet the subject of another pending action. It was therefore compulsory in Georgia and could not later be asserted in Tennessee. The Supreme Court granted review of Rule 13(a)'s application to these unusual circumstances. Id. at 59–60 & n.2.

## Issue and reasoning

**Did the first responsive pleading conclusively select the permissible forum for the common payment claim?** The Court accepted **for purposes of decision** the lower court's determination that the payment had not been allocated between the projects and could have been asserted in either case. It did not independently decide how the settlement should ultimately be allocated. Id. at 60.

The historical rule quoted in the second note required a counterclaim arising from the same transaction or occurrence as the opposing claim, subject to its stated qualifications and pending-action exception. Rather than ignoring that requirement, the Court asked what it was designed to accomplish: avoiding multiple actions and resolving common disputes in one suit. It particularly targeted a defendant who omitted a counterclaim and then began a second affirmative action using that omitted claim as its complaint. Id. at 59–60 & n.2.

That rationale did not fit this record. Pickard himself first tried to combine the project claims. **Federal law compelled their fragmentation** into Georgia and Tennessee cases. Southern then faced two actions brought against it, with a settlement relevant to both. Asserting the settlement in Tennessee did not create the needless additional action the rule sought to prevent. The contractor had a reason to regard the credit as particularly connected to the Tennessee project, where the requested $34,520 corresponded to Atlas's asserted Tennessee balance. Id. at 60–61.

The Court therefore rejected the mechanical proposition that the first responsive pleading must determine where the common counterclaim could be used. Its holding expressly rested on these circumstances. It did not create a general option to ignore compulsory counterclaims whenever a defendant prefers another forum or finds separate litigation convenient. Id. at 61.

## Judgment and an important limit

The Supreme Court **reversed the Sixth Circuit judgment insofar as it concerned this counterclaim and remanded to that court for further proceedings consistent with the opinion**. The ruling was per curiam; the report contains no separate judicial writing and does not identify a numerical vote. Id. at 61. This was a limited reversal, not a final adjudication of every disputed contractual amount in both projects.

The third note supplies the limiting principle that the textbook quotation omits. After certiorari, Southern amended its Georgia counterclaim to include the same $35,000 payment, but further Georgia proceedings awaited this decision. The Court explicitly stated that **once the counterclaim was adjudicated in one action, it could not be reasserted in the other**. Id. at 61 n.3. Permission to select a proper pending forum therefore did not authorize a double recovery or repeated adjudication after the claim had been resolved.

## How to use the assigned account

The textbook compresses Southern's litigation choice into electing to assert the counterclaim in the second, Tennessee suit. The complete opinion shows that it had originally included the payment in Georgia, dropped it before trial, used it in Tennessee, and later restored it in Georgia while proceedings there remained pending. Id. at 58–59, 61 n.3. Those details matter because the decision addresses compelled parallel litigation rather than an ordinary failure ever to plead a claim.

The account's final question asks whether the exception should be limited to claims that substantive law requires to be split across districts. That is an editorial question about the decision's reach, not a Supreme Court holding extending the exception to all parallel cases. The decision answers the presented Miller Act circumstances and preserves the adjudication bar in note 3.
