CT-CB-0192 | Owen v. Tunison
Owen v. Tunison
131 Me. 42, 158 A. 926 (1932)
Citation, court, and assigned scope
The Supreme Judicial Court of Maine, sitting as the Law Court, decided Owen on February 8,
1932. Justice Barnes wrote the reported opinion. The assigned passage is at printed pages
192–194 of the Contracts casebook. The full report is short and supplies the procedural
posture and precise sequence of correspondence; it contains no separately reported
concurrence or dissent.
Parties and requested relief
W. H. Owen was the prospective purchaser of the Bradley block and lot in Bucksport, Maine.
R. G. Tunison owned the property and resided in Newark, New Jersey, although the response
at issue apparently was written while he was in Cannes, France. Owen sought damages for
Tunison's alleged refusal to complete an agreed sale. He asserted that he remained ready
and willing to pay the price. The action was not a completed conveyance dispute: whether the
communications ever created a sale contract was the threshold issue. 131 Me. at 42–43.
Material facts and communication sequence
On October 23, 1929, Owen wrote to ask whether Tunison would sell specified store property
on Main Street for $6,000. The letter identified the property by its location between a
drugstore and grocery store. It thus presented an identified proposed purchase rather than
an abstract inquiry about real-estate values. The court's final paragraph calls it an offer of
$6,000, but the outcome does not depend on whether that opening inquiry itself supplied
every element of an offer. Id. at 43–44.
Tunison's reply, apparently written November 12, reached Owen on December 5. It explained
that several thousand dollars had been spent on improvements, that the upper floors had
been converted into apartments with baths, and that the building had been put in first-class
condition. The operative sentence said it would not be possible for him to sell unless he
received $16,000 cash. The sentence stated a condition below which a sale would be
unacceptable. It did not expressly say that he would sell to Owen upon Owen's assent to that
amount. Id. at 43.
Owen immediately sent a message purporting to accept the supposed $16,000 offer and
directing Tunison to send the deed to Eastern Trust and Banking Company in Bangor.
Tunison received that message in France. Four days later Owen learned that Tunison did not
wish to sell. Owen filed his damages action on January 14 of the following year. The report
therefore distinguishes the date the response apparently was written from the date it was
received; describing the response simply as a December 5 offer obscures both the timing and
the contested legal characterization. Id. at 43–44.

Procedural history and decision posture
The case came to the Law Court on report, with judgment to be rendered as the law and
admissible evidence required. The opinion does not describe an appellate reversal of a
damages verdict or a dismissal on the pleadings. Its task was to determine the legal effect of
the established correspondence and direct the appropriate judgment. Owen's allegations of
agreement and loss could not substitute for a communication that actually constituted an
offer. Id. at 42, 44.
Issues
Did an owner's statement that he could not sell unless he received $16,000 cash amount to a
present offer capable of acceptance, when made in response to a prospective buyer's $6,000
inquiry? If it did not, could the buyer's message calling the earlier statement an offer,
agreeing to the stated amount, and requesting a deed itself create the asserted contract?
Rule and holding
No sale contract arose. An expression of the minimum terms on which a person might
consider a transaction is not necessarily a proposal empowering the recipient to conclude
that transaction by assent. Tunison's communication could reasonably serve to open further
negotiations; the court held it was not an offer to sell. Owen's purported acceptance
therefore had no existing offer to accept. Id. at 44.
The holding concerns the existence of an offer, not the adequacy of the price, the sufficiency
of consideration, or whether an accepted real-estate agreement could support damages. The
court expressly assumed that a seller who makes a written offer that is accepted may owe
damages for refusing to convey, then held that the antecedent offer was missing here. Id.
Actual judgment
Judgment was directed for Tunison, the defendant. The court did not award damages, order
conveyance, or remand for valuation of Owen's loss. Id.
Reasoning
A necessary price condition did not establish a sufficient condition for agreement
The difference between saying that a sale is impossible below a certain price and promising
to sell at that price drives the decision. The former eliminates some possible bargains. It does
not necessarily establish that price is the only remaining matter or that assent to it will bind
the owner. Tunison connected the $16,000 figure to expenditures and improvements, which
explained his rejection of the much lower suggested price without necessarily committing
him to a sale. Id. at 43–44.
This is an interpretation of the actual communication in context. The court did not announce
that an offer must contain the formula “I offer,” or that every sentence using “unless” is
legally incapable of constituting an offer. Its conclusion was that these particular words did
not express a proposal to sell. The classification cannot be resolved merely by finding a
definite dollar amount and identifiable land: definiteness of possible terms and willingness to
be bound are distinct questions.

The buyer could not determine the legal character of the owner's letter by labeling
it
Owen's telegram showed that he wished to close at $16,000. It did not establish that Tunison
had previously granted him that power. Calling a communication an offer in a later response
cannot change its objective meaning when made. The court accordingly required a proposal
of sale before treating the telegram as an acceptance capable of concluding an agreement.
Id. at 44.
The timing of Tunison's refusal does not change the result. Because the court found no offer,
it had no need to decide whether a revocation arrived before a valid acceptance or whether a
binding contract already existed by the time Tunison declined. Turning Owen into a
mailbox-rule or revocation case would skip the logically prior formation question.
The precedents reinforced the distinction between negotiation and commitment
The court relied on a Maine decision treating a statement that the owner would not consider
less than a specified amount as something other than an outright offer. It also discussed a
seed seller's statement of the price he wanted, which could function as general commercial
information rather than a promise to supply everyone who responded. The analogies showed
that a price statement can be meaningful and deliberate while still leaving agreement to a
later step. Id.
Those precedents were not treated as a universal exclusion of quotations from contract
formation. They supplied comparisons for reading Tunison's language. The next assigned
case, Fairmount Glass, demonstrates why context and an invitation to immediate acceptance
can produce the opposite characterization. That comparison is a study synthesis across
cases, not an additional holding announced by the Owen court.
Separate opinions
The report contains one opinion by Justice Barnes and no separately reported concurrence or
dissent. No numerical vote breakdown is supplied in the text read; one should not invent one
from the absence of separate writings.
Study use and limits
The practical question is whether a reasonable recipient has been given the power to finish
the bargain, rather than merely information useful for proposing a bargain. A statement that
a seller “will sell to you for $16,000, payable on these terms” would provide materially
different evidence from the sentence in Owen. The casebook's suggested redraft is an
analytical variation, not an alternative fact found by the court.
Do not confuse an unsuccessful contract theory with a general entitlement to recover the
value of disappointed expectations. The opinion rejects the predicate contractual obligation;
it does not reach a damages calculation. Nor does the full report resolve modern
electronic-contract issues, promissory-estoppel theories, or every possible reliance claim.
The case is most useful as a disciplined first step in formation analysis: identify the offer
before applying rules about acceptance.
