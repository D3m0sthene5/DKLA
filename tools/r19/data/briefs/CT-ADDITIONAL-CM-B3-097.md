# C.L. Maddox, Inc. v. Coalfield Services, Inc.
51 F.3d 76 (7th Cir. 1995) | Contracts

## The mine subcontract and disputed stoppage

Maddox’s January 18, 1991 contract with Zeigler/Old Ben Coal required demolition of an underground loading facility, removal of its steel, fabrication of a replacement, and installation. Maddox retained fabrication and sought to subcontract the rest. At a February site meeting, Coalfield’s president offered to do the job for $230,000 in three weeks if his crew could work around the clock. Confident from similar projects, he did not inspect the facility underground. On March 19 Coalfield sent a written proposal specifying that price, the conditional three-week schedule, and biweekly payments based on progress. Maddox requested a noncompetition clause, which Coalfield added by return fax. Maddox said it would sign the amended proposal but never did. Work began March 20. (51 F.3d at 77.)

Coalfield’s progress was slower than expected. Repeated requests that Maddox sign the proposal received no response. Early April 8 Coalfield brought its crew, tools, and equipment to the surface. Hours later it faxed a demand for acceptance of the proposed contract and payment of an enclosed $103,500 invoice, representing its asserted forty-five-percent completion. Maddox apologized for its silence, questioned that percentage, but offered to accept it in good faith with ten-percent retention. The offer appeared conditioned on Coalfield signing an enclosed acceptance letter, and there was evidence of that condition in a telephone conversation. (Id. at 78.)

That letter extended the schedule to four weeks because the crew had not been permitted to work Sundays as contemplated, but also added $1,000 liquidated damages for each day beyond the new deadline. Coalfield expected another five or six weeks of work, creating potential exposure of about $35,000 under the new clause. It rejected the letter, demanded the invoice payment and its own proposed terms except the completion date, and said that even if Maddox accepted, work would restart April 16. Maddox required resumption by April 9. Coalfield never resumed; Maddox hired someone else to complete the job in time for its prime contract. (Id.)

## Procedural changes and the appeal

In this diversity action governed by Illinois contract law, Maddox sought its added completion costs. Coalfield counterclaimed for $103,500. Both moved for summary judgment; Coalfield initially maintained that no contract existed. The district judge first found a contract from the substantial performance and held Coalfield liable for abandoning it, directing Maddox to document damages. The judge later reconsidered who had breached. With the parties’ consent, a magistrate judge then tried that issue without a jury, found Maddox responsible, and awarded Coalfield $103,500. In calculating that award, the magistrate considered himself bound by the district judge’s supposed forty-five-percent completion finding. Maddox appealed. (Id. at 78–79.)

Chief Judge Posner wrote the March 22, 1995 opinion for a panel also comprising Ripple and District Judge Norgle, sitting by designation. The report contains no separate writing or numbered substantive footnotes; its designation note identifies Norgle’s district. The court sustained Maddox’s liability but reversed the damages determination and remanded. (Id. at 76–77 & designation note, 82.)

## An existing contract without the signature

Neither party still disputed contractual existence on appeal, but the court explained why the unsigned paper did not defeat it. The presidents had agreed on the job, price, and schedule, and Coalfield had performed a substantial portion. Its partial performance took the oral agreement outside the statute of frauds under the authority the court applied. Maddox’s promise to sign implied assent to the stated terms and helped fill gaps in the oral agreement. Both permitting and doing substantial work were inconsistent with an intention that no binding obligations arise until signature. Neither party developed an argument that essential terms were missing. (Id. at 79.)

The existence of that agreement nevertheless did not eliminate Coalfield’s concern about payment or make its request for written confirmation meaningless. It faced increasing expense under an incompletely specified oral arrangement, without a response from a customer who might later deny having orally accepted the written terms. Finishing the whole project would leave it seeking $230,000 through litigation with substantially reduced practical leverage. (Id. at 80–81.)

## Why suspension was justified, though the magistrate’s reasons were insufficient

The appellate court rejected the magistrate’s reliance on unpaid progress payments as an already completed breach justifying the initial stoppage. Coalfield sent its first invoice only after stopping, and progress-based payment could hardly be expected before it reported the progress. Nor could the liquidated-damages demand independently supply an earlier justification: it also followed the stoppage. Coalfield’s argument that it merely paused for a few hours was unpersuasive because its later fax proposed no return for eight days, suggesting a substantial interruption in a time-sensitive project. Without an excuse, that interruption could constitute breach. (Id. at 79–80.)

The court instead found an excuse in Maddox’s repeated silence in response to reasonable confirmation requests. For about three weeks Maddox neither signed, proposed different terms, nor responded to repeated requests concerning a proposal that the record showed contained no unusual, coercive, or one-sided terms. Coalfield was entitled, in these circumstances, to interrupt performance rather than accumulate costs with no assurance of payment. The subsequent liquidated-damages proposal corroborated the reasonableness of its earlier suspicions. The court did not hold that requesting that clause was itself a breach, and it did not permit later events retroactively to create a justification absent when work stopped. They served as evidence of the reasonableness of the already existing concern. (Id. at 80–81.)

This service contract was not governed by the Uniform Commercial Code. Posner drew instead on the assurance principle of UCC §2-609 and its generalization in Restatement (Second) of Contracts §251. He expressly acknowledged that the Restatement was not law and that the court could find no Illinois decisions discussing that section; the federal court considered the principle sound and predicted Illinois courts would regard it likewise. Coalfield’s requests to confirm the agreed proposal were treated as reasonable requests for assurances, or sufficiently equivalent in these circumstances. Unanswered requests, the incomplete terms, growing expense, and lack of offsetting payments together supported protective suspension and a demand for payment for work done. This is a federal prediction about the particular Illinois-law dispute, rather than direct application of Article 2 to services. (Id. at 80–81.)

The usual requirement of a clear, unequivocal statement of intention to breach did not foreclose that result. The court explained that the formula addresses repudiation based on a statement; this claim arose from silence in response to the equivalent of assurance requests. It also rejected the argument that an already existing contract made confirmation unnecessary, because anticipatory-breach remedies themselves presuppose a contract. Coalfield need not complete a costly project and then face a possible failure-to-mitigate defense when reasonable grounds already supported cutting its losses. (Id. at 81.)

## Completion deadline and damages remand

Maddox also suggested that Coalfield had underestimated the job and used the unsigned proposal as a pretext to abandon an unprofitable undertaking. The court recognized that this was potentially a meaningful argument, but found it insufficiently developed to justify reversal. The record did not establish whether three weeks was a firm requirement or an estimate, and any firm promise depended on seven-day work that had not been allowed. The court did not adjudicate impossibility, impracticability, or frustration defenses; it said it did not know what such excuses might have been available. (Id. at 81–82.)

The $103,500 award nevertheless could not stand. Forty-five-percent completion had been an assumption based on Coalfield’s concession during its unsuccessful effort to deny a contract, rather than a litigated finding binding Maddox. Maddox’s April 8 response was some evidence, but could reflect a compromise designed to obtain new terms or a mistake. The court also considered the “mend the hold” limitation on inconsistent contractual litigation positions, but Maddox had not used the concession to establish contractual existence in a way that triggered that concern. The amount of performance had not actually been determined. Thus the court affirmed that Maddox alone was the contract breaker, reversed the damages determination, and remanded for Coalfield’s damages to be redetermined. It did not affirm a final $103,500 entitlement. (Id. at 79, 82.)
