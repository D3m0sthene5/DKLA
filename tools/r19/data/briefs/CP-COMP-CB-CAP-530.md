# Inwood Laboratories, Inc. v. Ives Laboratories, Inc.

**456 U.S. 844 (1982) — Supreme Court of the United States, June 1, 1982.** Justice O’Connor wrote for six Justices. Justice White, joined by Justice Marshall, and Justice Rehnquist separately concurred in the result. All nine agreed to reverse and remand. The companion proceeding was *Darby Drug Co. v. Ives Laboratories*. Civil Procedure: Rule 52(a), appellate reweighing of facts, and the difference between factual and legal error. Assigned source: *CivPro.pdf*, physical pages 634–635, the own-caption account beginning at printed 1255 and ending at printed 1256.

## Commercial facts and the claims

Ives Laboratories held a patent on cyclandelate, a prescription vasodilator used in long-term treatment of peripheral and cerebral vascular diseases. It sold the drug under the registered trademark CYCLOSPASMOL. The drug was a white powder inside gelatin capsules: a blue capsule for the 200-milligram dose and a blue-and-red capsule for the 400-milligram dose, each bearing an Ives identifying imprint. Ives originally chose the colors arbitrarily. Its patent expired in 1972. (846–847.)

Generic manufacturers then sold cyclandelate in capsules matching those colors. The manufacturers initially used no identifying imprint; after litigation began, Premo and Inwood added their own marks. Ives conceded that the generic drugs and its product were bioequivalent and had the same bioavailability. Copying the drug after patent expiration was therefore distinct from falsely identifying its manufacturer. (847–848, nn.2–5.)

Ives promoted its product to prescribing physicians, particularly encouraging instructions against generic substitution. The generic companies principally distributed catalogs to wholesalers, hospitals, and pharmacies. Those catalogs truthfully described their drugs as equivalent or comparable, sometimes listed prices, and sometimes mentioned the capsule colors. Bulk containers correctly identified the generic manufacturer. Pharmacists then transferred the capsules into their own dispensing bottles and supplied the bottle labels seen by consumers. That last step created the opportunity for mislabeled generics. (847–849.)

Ives sued manufacturers and wholesalers under §§32 and 43(a) of the Lanham Act and New York unfair-competition law. Its §32 theory was contributory infringement: identical capsules and promotional materials allegedly encouraged pharmacists to dispense generics under Ives’ trademark. Ives did not contend that the suppliers themselves placed the Ives trademark on their products. Its §43(a) theory challenged false designation of origin through copying the colors and marketing equivalent products; Ives argued that the colors were nonfunctional and identified its product’s source. It sought an injunction against matching capsules and damages. (849–851, nn.6–11.)

Note 8 separates two pharmacist practices. A pharmacist could disregard a prescription forbidding substitution and falsely label the generic drug; alternatively, a pharmacist authorized to substitute could still mislabel the generic as CYCLOSPASMOL. The latter distinction matters because substitution was not inherently unlawful. Note 4 describes New York’s prescription and labeling requirements. (847–850, nn.4, 8.)

## Proceedings and competing views of the evidence

The district court initially denied a preliminary injunction. The Second Circuit affirmed and explained the contributory-infringement standard for the merits trial: liability could arise from a suggestion, even implicit, to mislabel the generic product, or from continued supply to retailers known or reasonably understood to be engaging in infringement. After a bench trial, the district court found neither ground established and entered judgment for the suppliers. (851–852.)

The trial court found that the catalogs could not fairly be read as urging infringement. Instances of mislabeling were too infrequent to support an inference of implied encouragement; the mislabeling that occurred reflected misunderstanding of state substitution law rather than deliberate passing off. It also rejected the §43(a) claim. Matching colors had functional uses: assisting patients with identification and continuity, helping in emergencies, and reducing confusion in dispensing. Ives had not established that the colors primarily identified the product’s source. (852–853.)

The Second Circuit reversed on §32 without expressly finding the district court’s findings clearly erroneous. It thought the evidence showed a pattern of illegal substitution and mislabeling, drew its own conclusions about the suppliers’ anticipated misconduct, and considered the explanations for the matching colors unpersuasive. It did not reach the other statutory and state claims. The Supreme Court reviewed that judgment. (853, 855–858.)

## Contributory infringement and the governing review standard

The Court recognized that trademark liability can extend beyond the person directly applying another’s mark. A manufacturer or distributor is contributorily responsible when it intentionally induces infringement or continues supplying a product to someone it knows or has reason to know is infringing. Mere manufacture of an imitative product did not, by itself, resolve those required factual showings. Note 13 rejects a watered-down rule based simply on reasonably anticipating misconduct. The majority read the appellate court as professing the correct legal standard but misapplying factual review; White read its reasoning differently. (853–855, n.13.)

Under Rule 52(a), appellate courts must accept findings unless review of the full evidence leaves a definite and firm conviction that a mistake occurred. The trial judge’s opportunity to evaluate credibility and weigh evidence supports that restraint. A reviewing court may not replace a permissible trial-court interpretation simply because it would weigh the same evidence differently or infer a more suspicious purpose. (855–857.)

The qualification reproduced in assigned note 15 is essential: findings based on a mistaken understanding of governing legal principles do not receive protection on that basis. Here, however, the trial court used the correct test already supplied by the appellate court. The majority treated the disagreement as evidentiary reweighing, not correction of a demonstrated legal mistake. (855, n.15.)

## Why the appellate reversal failed

Note 16 gives the underlying test-shopping evidence. Of 83 pharmacists receiving prescriptions permitting substitution, 48 dispensed the brand and 35 a generic. Ten of those 35 used CYCLOSPASMOL on the label, but five also included a form of “generic,” nine informed the purchaser about substitution, and only one charged the brand price. Different emphases could support different inferences; the district court’s conclusion that the evidence did not establish supplier encouragement was not unreasonable as a matter of law. (855–856, n.16.)

The Court examined the other appellate reasons individually. The assumption that imitative products would lead substantial numbers of pharmacists to illicit profit lacked record support. The trial judge’s conclusion about confusion over state law rested on multiple instances of pharmacists misunderstanding permissible substitution, not merely a casual exchange. Later indictments cited by the appellate court were not convictions and involved information unavailable when the district court decided. Even if relevant evidence had been improperly omitted, the appellate court should ordinarily remand for the trial court to consider it rather than make new findings itself. (856–857, nn.17–19.)

Finally, the district court’s functionality finding provided a legitimate reason for the matching colors. The Second Circuit could not simply disregard it or deem it unpersuasive. A legal challenge required identifying legal error; a factual challenge required applying clear-error review. Functionality was most directly related to §43(a), but also informed whether identical colors evidenced improper encouragement under §32. (857–858, n.20.)

## The separate opinions

**White, joined by Marshall, concurred on a different principal ground.** He objected that the broad clear-error application question was not presented in the petitions, although note 1 acknowledges that one petition did raise Rule 52 concerning functionality. He would reverse because the Second Circuit changed the contributory-infringement standard from intentional encouragement or knowledgeable continued supply to foreseeable wrongdoing by unidentified pharmacists. Generic competition and lawful substitution should not be burdened merely because some unknown retailers might misbehave. (859–862, White nn.1–3.)

White also viewed functionality as a complete affirmative defense to a contributory claim **predicated solely on reproducing a functional attribute**. Once the patent expired, that feature was no longer the originator’s exclusive property. His final note explicitly limits the proposition: copying a functional feature would not protect active inducement of infringement or reproduction of additional nonfunctional features. He likewise recognized functionality as a defense under §43(a), while agreeing that the remaining claims needed appellate review. This broader defense analysis belongs to the concurrence, not an undifferentiated nine-Justice holding. (862–864, White nn.4–6.)

**Rehnquist separately concurred.** He agreed that the appellate court erred in rejecting findings without identifying clear error, but preferred that the court of appeals determine whether the findings actually were clearly erroneous. He stressed its greater experience in routine factual review and questioned the Supreme Court’s performing that assessment itself. He also understood the majority’s restriction as applying where the appellate court had not found clear error; it did not extinguish the authority to reach contrary findings after properly determining that trial findings were clearly erroneous. (864.)

## Exact disposition and assigned emphasis

The Court **reversed the judgment finding a §32 violation and remanded to the Second Circuit**. The §43(a) and state unfair-competition claims, which the appellate court had not addressed, required independent review. The judgment did not finally dispose of every claim in the action or declare all copying and marketing practices immune from liability. Note 12 also identifies a trial finding concerning knowledgeable continued supply that the appellate court had not discussed and the Supreme Court did not address independently. (852, n.12; 858–859.)
