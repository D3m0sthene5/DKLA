# Duk v. MGM Grand Hotel, Inc.

**CP-122 · Civil Procedure · 320 F.3d 1052 (9th Cir. 2003) · March 3, 2003; amended on denial of rehearing April 17, 2003 · Hawkins, joined by D.W. Nelson and Wardlaw**

The court distinguished a jury’s permissible reconsideration of inconsistent answers from an improper compromise designed to reach a predetermined recovery. It upheld resubmission to the still-available jury and reinstated that jury’s internally consistent revised verdict, holding that the trial court had improperly ordered a new trial merely because the fault percentages changed.

## Facts and sequence of proceedings

Fernando Duk and his wife stopped at MGM’s Las Vegas hotel after a promotional trip for their lighting business. Duk drank heavily while gambling and became disruptive in a restaurant. Security personnel asked him to leave, then detained him after a citizen’s arrest when he refused. His wife told security he was an insulin-dependent diabetic. While detained, he complained of lung pain; he presented evidence that he also complained of chest pain and alleged that security failed to convey that complaint to the responding paramedics. They checked his blood sugar and lungs, but not his heart rate or blood pressure. Police took him to jail. After release the next morning, he went to a hospital and learned he had suffered a heart attack. Severe damage ultimately required a heart transplant in 1995. He sought medical expenses, pain and suffering, and economic losses from inability to work. 320 F.3d at 1054–1055.

The first trial in 1998 used a Rule 49(a) special verdict. Under Nevada’s comparative-negligence scheme, Duk could recover only if his negligence was 50% or less. The form instructed the jury to stop and return the verdict if it assigned him more than 50%; otherwise it should determine damages. The jury initially assigned Duk 65% and MGM 35%, yet continued to award $3.3 million. Before announcing that verdict, the judge identified the inconsistency and told the jury to continue deliberating. Twenty minutes later it returned a second verdict assigning Duk 49% and MGM 51%, with the damages figure unchanged. Id. at 1055.

The judge then granted MGM a new trial because the percentages differed between verdicts. Before retrial, MGM added the ambulance provider and police/detention entities as third-party defendants. Duk settled with the ambulance provider for $50,000 and the police/detention parties for a combined $10,000; the court found the settlements in good faith. The second trial produced a defense verdict. Duk challenged the new-trial grant; MGM cross-appealed resubmission, asserted an improper damages component, and challenged the settlements. Id. at 1055–1056.

## Resubmission issue and rule

Although Rule 49(b) expressly authorizes further consideration of general verdicts accompanied by interrogatories, Rule 49(a) did not expressly address resubmitting special verdicts. Circuit precedent permitted it because the rule did not prohibit it. Asking the still-present factfinder to clarify inconsistent answers promoted fairness and efficiency, provided the judge did not steer the jury toward a particular outcome. Id. at 1056–1058.

MGM relied on *Floyd v. Laws*, which treated an answer given in violation of a stop instruction as surplusage. But Floyd’s jury had already been discharged. That situation offered no opportunity for jury clarification. Duk’s jury remained available, so Floyd did not require the court to discard the damages answer and enter judgment for MGM. The court held that resubmission remained within discretion even with a stop instruction. It considered contrary Fourth Circuit authority but followed the view permitting the original jury to correct its own mistakes. The mere possibility of a compromise did not defeat the presumption that jurors would perform their duties properly. Id. at 1056–1058 & nn. 2–5.

## Why the new-trial order was improper

The Seventh Amendment required an attempt to reconcile jury findings on a reasonable view of the evidence before disregarding them. This obligation also applied to an apparent conflict between an original verdict and a verdict returned after resubmission. Resubmission entails renewed deliberation, not just correction of copying mistakes; jurors may reconsider their answers in light of the instructions. A difference between successive verdicts can therefore reflect the very clarification the judge requested. Id. at 1058–1060 & n. 6.

The second verdict was internally consistent. The judge’s stated reason for rejecting it was manipulation of fault percentages, without considering a lawful change of mind. The court distinguished *Riley v. K Mart*: there the jury changed both liability percentages and total damages in a manner preserving essentially the same net recovery, demonstrating an obvious effort to achieve a predetermined result. Duk’s jury left total damages unchanged. A legitimate reconsideration of fault remained plausible. Because the discrepancy could be explained consistently with law and evidence, granting a new trial was an abuse of discretion. Id. at 1059–1060.

## Additional holdings and exact disposition

MGM’s damages challenge failed. Lost earnings were properly submitted, both parties presented evidence, and no defect in the damages instructions was suggested. The court found no basis to disturb the award. It also upheld the good-faith settlement determinations for lack of abuse of discretion. Under the Nevada statute discussed in the opinion, good-faith settlements released settling defendants from further contribution to the nonsettling defendant, requiring consideration of fairness and overall appropriateness. Id. at 1060–1061.

The court affirmed in part and reversed in part, ordering reinstatement of the first jury’s second verdict and entry of judgment for Duk with interest calculated by the district court. Each party bore its own appellate costs. The $3.3 million is the unchanged total-damages finding; the disposition calls for judgment consistent with the revised fault allocation, not a finding that the unreduced figure itself is the final amount payable. Challenges to errors at the second trial were unnecessary to discuss because ordering that trial was improper. Id. at 1061 & n. 1. Hawkins wrote for the three-member panel without a separate opinion. The complete reported amended opinion contains six judicial notes, all considered here. Its historical lesson is that a court should preserve a reasonably supportable jury correction rather than equate every changed answer with illicit compromise.
