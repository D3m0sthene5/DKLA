# Northern Mariana Islands v. Canadian Imperial Bank of Commerce — certification

**693 F.3d 274 (2d Cir. 2012) — September 5, 2012, amended September 6, 2012.** Per curiam panel of Circuit Judges Cabranes, Straub, and Hall. This is the certification decision, distinct from the New York answer at 21 N.Y.3d 55 and the final federal disposition at 717 F.3d 266. The shared assigned account is CivPro physical page 610, printed page 1205, Note 3.

## Dispute and procedural posture

The Commonwealth sought to enforce tax judgments against the Millards by reaching accounts held at a foreign subsidiary of Canadian Imperial Bank of Commerce. It argued that the parent bank’s ownership and practical influence over the subsidiary could support a turnover order under New York CPLR §5225(b). The district court denied the turnover motion while maintaining interim protection against movement of the accounts. This background is described fully in the later state answer, 21 N.Y.3d at 58–59; the present short opinion refers readers to the district court’s April 12, 2012 opinion rather than repeating the facts.

The Commonwealth appealed. The question was not simply whether a New York court had personal jurisdiction over the parent. Section 5225(b) speaks of a third party’s possession or custody of a judgment debtor’s assets, and the parent might lack those attributes even if a subsidiary held the money. Whether the statutory terms extended to that relationship was unresolved state law. (275.)

## Why the court certified rather than decided the merits

The Second Circuit adopted the district court’s explanation that the appeal turned on unresolved interpretation of §5225(b). It considered the New York Court of Appeals better positioned to interpret the provision within New York’s overall statutory scheme, including the legislature’s intent and whether the same language in §5225(a) should have the same meaning. Certification allowed the state’s highest court to resolve that state-law question before the federal court finished the appeal. The decision does not itself establish that practical control suffices or fails. (275.)

Under New York Court of Appeals Rule 500.27 and Second Circuit Local Rule 27.2, the panel certified two questions. First, can a court issue a §5225(b) turnover order to an entity that lacks actual possession or custody of the debtor’s assets when its subsidiary might have possession or custody? Second, if so, what factual considerations determine whether issuing the order is permissible? The second question was expressly conditional; it asked for relevant considerations only if the first answer allowed that kind of order. (275.)

The panel also invited the state court to expand or alter the questions. Their formulation therefore did not purport to constrain the state court’s analysis. The federal opinion supplies neither a parent-subsidiary control test nor an independent assessment of the particular ownership evidence. Those merits matters remained for the certification process and resumed appeal. (275.)

## Operative order and separate stages

The panel **certified the questions** and ordered its clerk to transmit the opinion, briefs, appendix, and record to the New York Court of Appeals. The parties were to bear that court’s certification fees and costs equally. The same federal panel would resume consideration after the state court disposed of the certification. The district court’s stay would remain effective at least until the panel disposed of the returned case. (275–276.)

References to an appendix and record here are transmission instructions, not appendices reproduced as part of this short judicial opinion. The opinion contains no judicial footnotes or separate writings, and no numerical vote is reported.

The textbook correctly identifies certification as the first stage but also compresses the later answer and final result into the same paragraph. The prohibition on this turnover order belongs to the later decisions, not to this certification judgment. Studying the stages separately shows how an unsettled state-law issue can pause a federal appeal while interim relief preserves the practical value of the eventual ruling.
