# Goodisson v. Nunn
4 T.R. 761, 100 Eng. Rep. 1288 (K.B. 1792) | Contracts

## Court, pleading posture, and result
The reproduced report dates the King’s Bench decision June 19, 1792. Lord Kenyon, Chief Justice, and Justices Buller and Grose each delivered reasons supporting judgment for the defendant purchaser. The seller’s debt action sought the £21 stipulated for the buyer’s failure to complete the transaction. After the purchaser obtained production of the agreement and pleaded the seller’s nonperformance, the seller demurred to the three special pleas. The court rejected the contention that the buyer owed the sum independently of the seller’s conveyance or offer to convey. This was a determination on the pleadings, not a damages verdict following findings that either party had performed. (Preserved Williston reprint, 33–37.)

## Reciprocal agreement and the special pleas
The seller undertook to convey a copyhold estate on or before September 2; the purchaser undertook to pay £210 by that date. Each party would owe the other £21 upon failure to carry out the specified obligation. The instrument also addressed taking up the copyhold for the buyer or his wife, the seller’s taking it up for himself, and sharing associated expenses. Its conveyancing language was imperfectly drawn, as the report’s note expressly acknowledges. (Reprint 33.)

The buyer first pleaded non est factum, contesting the instrument, and then three additional defenses: that the seller did not convey by the appointed date through the reasonably required means; that he had not conveyed by that date or subsequently; and that he had no interest in the premises enabling him to convey when the articles were made. The seller’s general demurrer addressed those last three defenses. The deciding reasons concentrated on the seller’s failure to convey or offer performance and the dependency of the parties’ undertakings; they did not separately adjudicate title evidence under the fourth plea. (33–34.)

## Seller’s arguments for independent obligations
Wood argued for the seller that each covenant was general and independent, with one promise supplying consideration for the other promise rather than actual performance being a condition to the buyer’s obligation. He contrasted the language with an express undertaking to pay upon conveyance. Relying on older authorities, including Pordage v. Cole and Blackwell v. Nash, he maintained that the purchaser’s remedy for a failure to convey lay in a separate action against the seller rather than withholding the stipulated payment. (34–35.)

Asked about later decisions requiring performance or an offer where acts were due together, counsel proposed another distinction: the buyer supposedly had to choose what conveyance was wanted and whether title would go to him or his wife. If the buyer owed the first act, the seller argued, the contemporaneous-performance cases would not govern. These were counsel’s constructions, not findings that the buyer had actually obstructed a conveyance offered by a prepared seller. (35.)

## Kenyon: substance of the exchange controls dependency
Kenyon regarded the case as clear in both strict law and justice. The seller had neither conveyed nor offered to do so, nor taken a step toward conveyance, yet claimed the contractual penalty. The reciprocal acts were to occur together: the seller would receive the price on conveyance, and the buyer would receive the estate on payment. Kenyon illustrated the unreasonable credit risk of requiring one party to part with the whole consideration and rely on a separate suit against a possibly insolvent counterparty. The hypothetical price of £40,000 was an illustration, not the transaction’s actual £210 price. (35.)

He accepted the principle common to the older authorities that dependent covenants required the plaintiff to perform or offer performance. He rejected their application of that principle as if the mere exchange of promises necessarily made the duties independent. In particular, interpreting consideration as only a promise to transfer could not justify ignoring the practical exchange of land and money. The seller had to plead performance or an offer to perform before maintaining this action. Kenyon treated correction of misapplied principles, supported by later precedents, as a judicial duty. (35–36.)

## Buller and Grose: contemporaneous acts and no forced personal credit
Buller likewise reasoned that the seller’s conveyance and buyer’s payment were, by the nature of the sale, acts to be done together. He invoked Mansfield’s explanation that the essence of such an agreement could be that neither side was to give the other personal credit. He described later cases involving an offered assignment and the other party’s refusal as consistent with recovery by a party who had done all it could to perform. Those precedents did not dispense with the present seller’s performance or offer. (36.)

Buller also answered the proposed buyer-first distinction. The instrument required Goodisson first to take up the copyhold for himself: he had to be admitted before surrendering it to the buyer. The option whether the ultimate conveyance would be to the buyer or his wife therefore did not justify this seller’s complete inaction. Grose supported examining whether, in reality, the seller had to act first or the performances were concurrent, rather than resolving dependency solely through technical wording. He considered the later precedents consonant with good sense and justice and joined judgment for the purchaser. (36–37.)

## Exact disposition and limits of the assigned account
The report ends with judgment for the defendant. It adds that Gibbs, who would have argued for the defendant, mentioned Duke of St. Albans v. Shore and Boone v. Eyre; that brief reporter statement is distinct from an additional judicial opinion. The judgment did not award the purchaser the reciprocal £21 or resolve the enforceability of either stipulated sum under a modern penalty doctrine. The report calls the claimed sum a penalty; the assigned text calls it liquidated damages, but the holding turns on dependent performance, not that classification. (37.)

The textbook’s account on printed page 835 uses the case to introduce concurrent conditions and quotes the performance-or-offer pleading requirement. The complete reproduced report adds the demurrer, the conveyancing arguments, and the individual explanations of Kenyon, Buller, and Grose. The subsequent discussion of modern UCC tender rules is the editors’ comparison, not the law applied by King’s Bench in 1792.
