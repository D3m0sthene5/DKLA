# Circle B Enterprises, Inc. v. Steinke
1998 ND 164, 584 N.W.2d 97 (1998) | Contracts

## Court and result
The North Dakota Supreme Court’s September 15, 1998 decision modified Circle B’s judgment from $20,161.01 to $17,078.72 and affirmed it as modified. Justice Meschke wrote for Chief Justice Vande Walle and Justices Maring and Sandstrom. Justice Neumann agreed with almost all the reasoning but dissented from allowing the daily assessment to exceed the unpaid contract balance. He would have reduced the judgment further, to $11,412.14. The disagreement concerned the contractual cap, not whether the stipulated remedy could be valid despite being called a penalty. (97, 99, 102; ¶¶1, 18–21.)

## Restoration agreement, delays, and trial judgment
In 1990, Circle B’s president, Jerald Bugliosi, orally arranged for Jim Steinke, operating as Heritage Corvette, to restore the company’s 1961 Corvette. After restoration was not timely completed, they executed an October 26, 1994 agreement requiring completion by April 21, 1995. It recited payments of $13,815, including a $1,600 hard top and a $2,161.01 credit for unfinished work. It identified $14,094.43 in remaining work and, after payment credits, set the balance to be paid at $10,333.42. (99; ¶2.)

The writing made time essential. If Steinke failed to complete and deliver by the deadline, $100 per day would be assessed and deducted from the $10,333.42 balance, subject to adjustments for added items. It further permitted Circle B to engage another contractor, making the third party’s charges Steinke’s responsibility. Upon restoration as agreed, Circle B would pay the stated balance. These connected provisions mattered both to the damages calculation and to whether Steinke could claim payment for unfinished performance. (99; ¶2.)

Steinke missed the April deadline. Circle B sued in July 1995 for enforcement and possession. The parties provisionally extended completion to September 27, but he also missed that date; the car was returned September 28. Circle B paid a different company $9,251.13 to finish. The trial court found breach of all the agreements, credited Steinke with $11,653.99 in work already paid for, and required return of the $2,161.01 excess credit. Although it found breach damages difficult to ascertain at formation, it declined the daily remedy: it viewed that provision only as a reduction of a final payment that had never become due because Steinke did not finish. It instead awarded $18,000 for six years’ lost promotional use of the car at $3,000 annually. (99; ¶¶3–4.)

Steinke challenged the loss-of-use award as unsupported by pleading and proof. He also claimed $3,756.64 of work after the written agreement, sought payment in contract or unjust enrichment, and claimed the $1,118.29 difference between the agreed remaining balance and the substitute contractor’s charge. (99–100; ¶5.)

## Failure of substantial performance and the restitution question
Contract recovery required substantial performance: good-faith performance subject only to relatively unimportant, unintentional deviations arising from mistake or inadvertence. Although the trial judge made no express substantial-performance finding, the figures supported a legal conclusion that this condition was absent. The agreement recognized $10,053.99 of earlier work; the trial finding of $11,653.99 total effectively allowed only another $1,600. Even Steinke’s larger asserted later contribution represented less than one-third of the remaining $14,094.43 obligation. Such deficiencies could not be called unimportant. He therefore had no contractual entitlement to payment for work after October 26, 1994. (100; ¶¶6–7.)

Failure to substantially perform did not itself always eliminate restitution. A breaching contractor could sometimes recover the reasonable value of a conferred benefit outside the contract. The Court discussed Restatement section 374, including the qualification that parties may integrate part performance into a liquidated-damages arrangement that precludes restitution. That possibility required examining the whole writing rather than treating unjust enrichment as an automatic entitlement to a further credit. (100; ¶¶8–9.)

## Validity of the agreed daily remedy
North Dakota’s statute generally invalidated contractual penalties, while permitting anticipated compensation where actual breach damages would be impracticable or extremely difficult to fix. The parties’ label was not conclusive. The party seeking enforcement carried the burden of establishing difficulty of measurement at formation, a reasonable effort to estimate damages, and a stipulated amount reasonably related to probable damage rather than disproportionate to anticipated harm. Those foundational matters were factual. (100–101; ¶¶10–11.)

The Court treated those considerations together, consistent with precedents favoring liquidated damages outside adhesion contracts. Difficulty of estimation had greater importance where negotiations were minimal and less where bona fide bargaining had occurred; a reasonable estimation effort did not invariably require direct face-to-face negotiation about the figure. Here Steinke participated in negotiating, reviewed the writing, and proposed changes. The agreement was negotiated for prompt completion rather than imposed as an adhesion contract. Bugliosi’s characterization of the clause as an incentive or motivational tool did not by itself make it invalid. The finding that damages were difficult to ascertain was not clearly erroneous, and the Court accepted the remedy as a reasonable endeavor in these circumstances. (101; ¶¶12–13.)

The trial court consequently erred in substituting its own six-year advertising-loss measure for the enforceable agreed remedy. The Supreme Court’s decision did not establish that $100 daily is invariably reasonable, or that negotiation alone validates any penalty; it applied the statutory foundation and the actual record of this agreement. (101; ¶¶11–14.)

## Majority’s calculation and Neumann’s competing construction
The majority counted 160 days from the April deadline until return, yielding $16,000. It first offset the $10,333.42 unpaid balance, reducing that payment to zero, then imposed the remaining $5,666.58 on Steinke. Reading the next sentence as an additional obligation, it added the $9,251.13 paid for substitute completion. The Court treated the value of later part performance as integrated into this remedial agreement, so Steinke received neither separate restitution nor the difference between his promised balance and the replacement charge. (101–102; ¶¶15–17.)

The resulting agreed-remedy recovery was $14,917.71: the $5,666.58 assessment plus $9,251.13 completion expense. Adding return of the earlier $2,161.01 credit produced the final $17,078.72 judgment. The modified affirmance did not remand for proof of more advertising loss. (102; ¶¶17–18.)

Neumann read the phrase providing deduction from the balance as a cap at $10,333.42. He would extinguish that balance but not award another $5,666.58 once it was exhausted. Keeping the other recovery components, his proposed judgment was $11,412.14. This was a partial concurrence and dissent from the majority’s construction and computation, not a finding that the entire liquidated-damages agreement was unenforceable. (102; ¶¶20–21.)
