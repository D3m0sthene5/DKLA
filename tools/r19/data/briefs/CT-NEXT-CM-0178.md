# Young v. City of Chicopee
186 Mass. 518, 72 N.E. 63 (Mass. October 18, 1904) | Contracts

## Facts and arrangement
Young agreed in writing to repair a wooden highway bridge across the Connecticut River. Decayed timber and other woodwork were to be replaced with sound material securely fastened into the carriageway. Compensation for both labor and materials was a specified amount per thousand feet of lumber, measured after installation and certified by the engineers. To minimize disruption of public travel, the contract required material for at least half the contemplated repairs to be on the job before work began. Young complied by placing lumber along the bridge and riverbanks. During the work a fire, without either party's fault, destroyed the bridge and some lumber that had not yet been incorporated into it. Further performance of the repair contract became impossible. (518–519.)

## Procedure and contested claim
Young sought compensation for labor and materials. The city accepted liability for work done and materials already incorporated into the bridge, but denied liability for unused lumber destroyed at the site. The Superior Court refused the city's requested ruling excluding that loss, and a jury returned $1,312.43 for Young. The city's bill of exceptions provided for judgment on that verdict if the unused lumber was compensable; otherwise Young would receive the smaller agreed amount. Thus the appellate dispute concerned preparatory materials, rather than the contractor's entitlement to payment for incorporated work. (518–519.)

## Holding and principle
Hammond's opinion held that the city did not bear the loss of Young's unused lumber. Destruction discharged both parties from further performance of this entire repair contract, while permitting recovery for qualifying partial performance. The owner's liability depended on contract work that had become sufficiently identified with the existing structure that, absent its destruction, it would have benefited the owner in the manner contemplated by the agreement. Labor and materials actually incorporated into the bridge qualified; lumber merely positioned for later use did not. (519–520.)

## Reasons and restitution boundary
No delivery of the unused lumber to the city had occurred. Young retained title, care, and control. He could exchange it for other lumber, and the city could not lawfully appropriate it against his will if he refused to continue work. Had the lumber survived the fire, it would still have been his property. The requirement that material be on site before commencement accelerated the work; it did not transfer ownership or insure Young against losses to his inventory. The compensation formula likewise measured material actually installed, rather than material accumulated for possible use. (519–520.)

The full opinion discusses alternative explanations for partial-performance recovery: an implied undertaking by the owner that the structure would continue to exist, and an implied assumpsit treating work properly performed as done at the other's request. Without committing the outcome to a single theoretical formulation, the court limited the owner's obligation to work identified with the structure and directed toward the contract's ends. Even though the fire erased the installed work's physical benefit, the city remained liable for that qualifying performance. It did not become an insurer of all expenditures made in reliance on the contract. The plaintiff's Missouri and Illinois authorities were considered distinguishable. (520.)

## Exact disposition and source qualification
The court sustained the city's exceptions and set aside the jury verdict. Under the parties' stipulated alternative it directed entry of the smaller judgment for Young, rather than denying him any recovery. The report's procedural introduction states that alternative as $584.77, while the concluding judicial sentence, also reproduced in the textbook, states $584 damages. That internal difference is preserved here rather than silently harmonized. Hammond wrote the only reported judicial opinion; no dissent, separate writing, or numbered judicial footnote appears. (518, 520.)
