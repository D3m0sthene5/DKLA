# Parsons Steel, Inc. v. First Alabama Bank

**474 U.S. 518 (1986) — Supreme Court of the United States — January 27, 1986.** Justice Rehnquist wrote for a unanimous Court. The assigned Civil Procedure account is Note 3, printed page 1378, physical PDF page 693, following *Semtek*. The case concerns a federal court's attempt to protect its judgment after a state court has already rejected the asserted preclusive effect of that judgment.

## Parallel litigation and conflicting results

Parsons Steel, Inc., and Jim and Melba Parsons sued First Alabama Bank of Montgomery and bank officer Edward Herbert in Alabama state court in February 1979. They essentially alleged that the bank had fraudulently induced the Parsonses to permit a third person to control a subsidiary and ultimately obtain complete ownership. The subsidiary entered involuntary bankruptcy in April, and its trustee joined the state action. The parent company and the Parsonses then sued the bank in federal court in May, alleging that the same conduct violated the Bank Holding Company Act amendments. The trustee did not join the federal action. 474 U.S. at 520.

The parties conducted joint discovery. The federal liability trial occurred first: a jury favored the plaintiffs, but the District Court entered judgment notwithstanding the verdict for the bank, and the Eleventh Circuit affirmed. The bank and other state defendants then pleaded res judicata and collateral estoppel in the state action. The Alabama court rejected the res judicata defense. The state complaint was later amended to add a Uniform Commercial Code claim challenging the commercial reasonableness of the bank's foreclosure sale of the subsidiary's assets. The state jury returned a general verdict awarding a total of **$4,000,001** to the plaintiffs. Id.

Those results created the conflict. The defendants had won in federal court but lost both the preclusion defense and the later verdict in state court. They returned to the federal District Court to seek an injunction preventing further prosecution of the state action. They also apparently filed a timely state posttrial motion for a new trial or judgment notwithstanding the verdict, a procedural step the Supreme Court noted even though the appellate opinion had not mentioned it. Id. at 520–521 & n.1.

## Federal injunction and the appellate ruling

The District Court concluded that the two actions rested on the same facts and substantially the same claimed damages. In its view, the state fraud and UCC claims should have been presented as pendent claims in the federal suit, so the bank's federal judgment barred them. It enjoined further prosecution of the state proceeding to prevent that proceeding from nullifying its earlier judgment. Id. at 521.

A divided Eleventh Circuit panel affirmed the injunction in relevant part. It treated the Anti-Injunction Act's relitigation exception as modifying the Full Faith and Credit Act enough to permit the federal court to make the final preclusion determination, despite the state court's intervening rejection of that defense. It also rejected waiver from waiting until after the state trial. Separately, it remanded for consideration of whether the nonparticipating bankruptcy trustee could pursue a UCC claim based on facts different from those of the federal action. Id. at 521–522 & n.3.

Judge Hill's dissent would distinguish a federal injunction sought before the state court adjudicated preclusion from one sought after that adjudication. Once the state court had resolved the issue, he would require recognition of its ruling under § 1738. That distinction became central to the Supreme Court's analysis. Id. at 522–523.

## Issue and statutory rules

The question was whether the relitigation exception allowed the federal courts to disregard the state court's resolution of the res judicata issue. The Court held that it did not. The Full Faith and Credit Act, § 1738, requires federal courts to give a state judgment the same preclusive effect it would have in the rendering state's own courts. They cannot substitute their own preclusion rules for the state's. The Anti-Injunction Act, § 2283, ordinarily forbids federal injunctions staying state proceedings but allows, among other exceptions, injunctions necessary to protect or effectuate federal judgments. Id. at 519, 523.

Those statutes could be read consistently rather than treating the injunction exception as an implied partial repeal of § 1738. Before the state court rules on the merits of a federal-judgment preclusion defense, the relitigation exception may permit protective relief in an appropriate case. Once the state court has finally rejected the defense, however, § 1738 requires the federal court to determine the preclusive effect of that state decision under state law. Id. at 523–524.

## Why the earlier federal judgment did not authorize a second try

The Eleventh Circuit relied on Congress's 1948 response to *Toucey*, but the Supreme Court distinguished timing and procedural posture. The parties seeking injunctions in *Toucey* had approached federal court before the state courts resolved the preclusion question. Here the bank first chose to litigate that question in state court and returned to federal court only after losing. Congress's protection of federal judgments did not establish a right to disregard that intervening state adjudication. Id. at 524.

The prospect of inefficient parallel litigation did not justify the injunction either. Concurrent state and federal proceedings are part of the dual system, subject to the statutory limits on interference. Even a mistaken state preclusion ruling does not, merely because it is mistaken, justify the intrusive remedy of a federal injunction against enforcement of the state judgment. Correction ordinarily proceeds through state appeals and potential Supreme Court review. Id. at 524–525.

The controlling inquiry was therefore not simply whether the federal court believed its own first judgment ought to preclude the state claims. It had to confront the legal effect of the **second court's determination of that very defense**. That requirement preserves both the statutory full-faith-and-credit command and the limits of the relitigation exception. Id. at 525.

## Exact judgment, remand, and reservations

The Court **reversed the Eleventh Circuit and remanded for further proceedings consistent with its opinion**. It directed that Alabama preclusion law be determined and applied initially by the District Court. It did not itself decide that the state ruling necessarily was preclusive under Alabama law. If the District Court found that it was not, the court would then be best situated to consider an injunction under the general principles of equity, comity, and federalism. Absence of state-law preclusion would not automatically require an injunction. Id. at 525–526.

Footnote 4 leaves two further arguments unresolved: whether the relitigation exception can protect claims that could have been but were not actually litigated in the first federal action, and whether the bankruptcy trustee, who was not a party to that federal action, was nevertheless bound by its judgment. The Court also did not adjudicate whether the bank committed fraud or conducted a commercially unreasonable sale. Id. at 526 n.4.
