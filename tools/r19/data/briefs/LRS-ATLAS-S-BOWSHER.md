# Bowsher v. Synar
478 U.S. 714 (1986) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Court, stage, and disposition

On July 7, 1986, the Supreme Court affirmed a three-judge district court’s judgment invalidating the automatic deficit-reduction mechanism that assigned the Comptroller General binding executive responsibilities while Congress retained statutory removal authority over him. Chief Justice Burger wrote for Brennan, Powell, Rehnquist, and O’Connor. Stevens, joined by Marshall, concurred in the judgment on a different theory concerning congressional lawmaking. White and Blackmun separately dissented. The result was seven votes for affirmance and two against; the removal-based Court opinion had five votes. The Court stayed its judgment for no more than sixty days to allow Congress to implement the Act’s fallback procedures. 478 U.S. at 714, 716, 736.

## The deficit-control scheme

The President signed the Balanced Budget and Emergency Deficit Control Act of 1985, commonly called Gramm-Rudman-Hollings, on December 12, 1985. It prescribed maximum deficits for fiscal years 1986 through 1991, declining to zero in 1991. A deficit exceeding the statutory target by a specified amount would trigger generally across-the-board reductions, divided equally between defense and nondefense programs, with certain programs exempted. Id. at 717–718.

The Directors of the Office of Management and Budget and the Congressional Budget Office independently estimated the coming fiscal year’s deficit and calculated required reductions by program. They submitted a joint report to the Comptroller General under § 251. He reviewed it, exercised independent judgment, and reported his own conclusions to the President, explaining differences from the Directors’ report. Under § 252, the President had to issue a sequestration order consistent with the Comptroller General’s report and could not recalculate or modify its estimates or specified reductions. Congress could avert some or all of those reductions by enacting legislation during an intervening period. Otherwise the order took effect. Id. at 718, 732–733.

Congress also anticipated possible invalidation of the reporting procedures. Section 274(f) supplied a fallback: the Directors’ report would go to a Temporary Joint Committee on Deficit Reduction, which would report a joint resolution containing it within five days. Special rules prevented amendments. If both Houses passed the resolution and the President signed it, it would become the basis for a presidential sequestration order. This was a legislative substitute, not continued automatic operation by the Comptroller General. Id. at 718–719.

## Litigation and standing

Congressman Synar sued within hours of enactment; eleven other Members joined him. The National Treasury Employees Union filed a substantially identical challenge, and an individual union member was later added. The spending provisions suspended scheduled cost-of-living benefit increases for union members. A three-judge district court composed of Judges Scalia, Johnson, and Gasch found standing, rejected a nondelegation challenge because the Act supplied adequate standards, but held that executive functions could not be exercised by an officer subject to congressional removal. Its judgment was reported at 626 F. Supp. 1374. Direct appeals by the Comptroller General, Senate, and House leadership followed under the Act. Id. at 719–721 & nn. 1–2.

The Supreme Court found sufficient Article III and statutory standing because the individual union-member appellee would lose a scheduled benefit increase. It therefore did not decide whether the Union itself or Members of Congress had standing. Likewise, the Court did not adopt the district court’s ruling on nondelegation: once it found the structural violation, it had no occasion to consider that or the other challenges. Id. at 721, 736 n. 10.

## Congress’s removal authority and the Court’s rule

The Comptroller General was nominated by the President from a list recommended by congressional leaders and confirmed by the Senate. He headed the General Accounting Office, the institution’s historical name. The governing statute permitted removal by impeachment or a congressional joint resolution, following notice and an opportunity for a hearing, for permanent disability, inefficiency, neglect of duty, malfeasance, or a felony or conduct involving moral turpitude. A joint resolution would be presented to the President, but Congress could override a veto by two-thirds votes. The majority therefore regarded the arrangement as permitting Congress to remove the officer even against presidential opposition. Id. at 727–729 & nn. 6–7; 738.

The constitutional rule was that Congress could not reserve removal power, apart from impeachment, over an officer charged with executing the laws. Removal created a practical means of controlling execution. Presidential appointment did not overcome that continuing relationship: the officer had reason to heed the authority that could terminate his service. The majority invoked the 1789 removal debate and Myers, then explained that Humphrey’s Executor and Wiener concerned restrictions on presidential removal rather than direct congressional participation in removing executive officers. Id. at 721–726.

That distinction was express and important. The case did not decide the validity of independent agencies whose members were removable by the President for cause or whose statutes said nothing about removal. Those arrangements did not give Congress this direct removal role. The Court’s fourth footnote rejects the contention that affirmance necessarily undermined such agencies. Id. at 724–725 & n. 4.

## Why the Comptroller General was answerable to Congress

Appellants stressed the officer’s independence and the absence of any actual use of the removal procedure. The majority answered that the constitutional defect was present in the continuing relationship, without requiring Congress first to exercise the power. The removal grounds were broad enough to encompass perceived failures to carry out legislative expectations. The Court questioned whether the enumerated grounds were exclusive and whether judicial review would be available; it did not accept those possibilities as established safeguards. Nor could structural protections depend on a judicial prediction that Congress probably would not remove the officer. Id. at 727 n. 5, 728–730 & n. 8.

The office’s broader statutory and institutional history reinforced that conclusion. Congress established GAO to check spending on its behalf, repeatedly described it as part of the Legislative Branch, and directed auditing as an agent of Congress. Earlier Comptrollers General likewise characterized themselves as congressional agents independent of executive authority. The majority relied on these relationships along with removal authority, rather than treating technical expertise or professional behavior as a substitute for constitutional structure. Id. at 730–732.

## Why the reporting task was execution of law

Appellants described the Act’s calculations as mechanical or ministerial. The Court disagreed. The Comptroller General had to evaluate projected revenues and expenditures, interpret statutory requirements, decide the facts relevant to their application, and specify program-by-program reductions. His report could differ from the Directors’ report and controlled what the President had to do. Those were decisions implementing an enacted statute, not merely advice to Congress. Id. at 732–733.

Congress could set policy by legislation and subsequently change that policy through legislation. It could not retain direct control over execution through an officer answerable to its removal power. The Court analogized that arrangement to the legislative veto invalidated in Chadha: the ability to remove or threaten removal could influence how delegated law was carried out. That conclusion concerned this binding execution function, without adjudicating every other task Congress had assigned the Comptroller General. Id. at 726–727, 733–736 & n. 9.

## Remedy and the fallback

Appellants proposed severing the removal provision of the 1921 law instead of invalidating the 1985 reporting provisions. The majority declined that statutory reconstruction. It could alter an office deliberately made independent of the Executive and disrupt the balance behind numerous later assignments. More decisively, Congress had expressly supplied a fully operative fallback for invalidation of any § 251 reporting procedure. The majority treated that language as settling the appropriate response and allowed it to operate. It affirmed the district court’s judgment and order, subject to the sixty-day maximum stay. Id. at 734–736 & nn. 9–10.

## Stevens and Marshall: congressional process

Stevens disputed the majority’s inference that carefully limited removal authority necessarily made the officer subservient. Congress should be presumed to follow the statutory grounds, and no historical threat of policy-based removal was identified. Comparable for-cause authority over the FTC had been understood to preserve independence. Removal authority was evidence of the relationship, but was neither necessary nor sufficient for Stevens’s conclusion. Id. at 736–741 & nn. 1–4.

Instead, the Comptroller General’s longstanding duties made him an agent of Congress. Detailed statutes required investigations, evaluations, assistance, and reports specifically for Congress and its committees. Obligations to the Executive did not negate that primary institutional identity. The deficit-control assignment authorized consequential policy judgments binding the Nation, which Congress or its agent could exercise only through bicameral passage and presentment. Congress could delegate policy implementation to an independent agency or the Executive, but could not use its own lesser representative to bypass its prescribed lawmaking process. Whether the functions were labeled executive or legislative did not advance that inquiry; their character could vary with the institution exercising them. Id. at 741–759 & nn. 5–25.

Stevens distinguished ordinary internal or ancillary congressional functions from binding national policymaking. He also left open the legality of existing Comptroller General responsibilities that might not reach that level. For him, the fallback’s use of a joint resolution demonstrated the appropriate constitutionally prescribed process. Id. at 753–758, especially nn. 20–21.

## White: practical independence

White favored a functional inquiry into actual disruption and aggrandizement. The statute prescribed detailed criteria intended to minimize policy choices; setting spending levels was centrally a congressional function, and assigning their implementation to an independent officer took no rightful presidential discretion away. He accepted that the duties could be called executive and that Congress could not retain an executive role for itself, but rejected the premise that this limited removal power made the Comptroller General Congress’s agent. Id. at 759–765 & nn. 1–5.

A removal resolution required bicameral passage and presentment, unlike Chadha’s one-House veto. Grounds for removal, a hearing, and possible judicial review offered additional limits. Presidential approval or a difficult two-thirds override made domination implausible. White also criticized the majority’s reliance on legislative-history statements made when the earlier proposed bill contemplated a concurrent resolution without presidential participation. No Comptroller General had been threatened with or subjected to removal, and congressional reporting obligations alone did not establish control over unrelated functions. Id. at 766–775 & nn. 6–13.

White would sustain the Act. Alternatively, he agreed with Blackmun that any necessary invalidation should target the old removal provision rather than the new deficit-control mechanism. The fallback did not itself answer which of two incompatible provisions should be invalidated. Id. at 775–776 & n. 14.

## Blackmun: choosing the less disruptive remedy

Blackmun acknowledged that direct congressional participation in removing an executive officer might violate separation of powers, while rejecting the majority’s claim of practical subservience. He found it unnecessary to decide that structural question because these plaintiffs were not entitled to the requested destruction of the budget mechanism. Any incompatibility should be addressed by refusing to permit congressional removal if attempted. Id. at 776–779 & n. 1.

Courts should choose the remedy that least disrupts congressional objectives, rather than let the identity or timing of a plaintiff’s lawsuit dictate which provision falls. Blackmun drew on Glidden and the choice between extension and invalidation of discriminatory benefit statutes. The fallback showed a preference for some deficit-control mechanism over none; it did not establish a preference for preserving the old removal power over the primary mechanism. The rarely used removal provision was far less important than the new budget scheme, and its deletion would preserve the Comptroller General’s independence and many other responsibilities. Id. at 779–787 & nn. 2–5.
