# American Textile Manufacturers Institute, Inc. v. Donovan
452 U.S. 490 (1981), decided June 17, 1981 | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Occupational hazard and rulemaking

OSHA’s 1978 Cotton Dust Standard regulated exposure to airborne particles generated in processing cotton. Inhaling that dust caused byssinosis, or brown lung disease, ranging from acute breathing problems to chronic, irreversible and disabling impairment. The record contained estimates of at least 35,000 current and former workers suffering the most disabling form and approximately 100,000 suffering some form. The Court described both expert evidence and workers’ accounts; it did not treat cotton dust as a hypothetical hazard. (494–499 & nn. 6–13.)

The regulatory history moved from an older limit for total dust to measurements of respirable dust. Following recommendations, public comments, and more than fourteen days of hearings, OSHA issued the final standard after a proceeding producing roughly 105,000 record pages. Its eight-hour exposure limits were 200 micrograms per cubic meter for yarn manufacturing, 750 for slashing and weaving, and 500 for other covered processes. These were less stringent in some sectors than the proposed uniform 200 limit. OSHA rejected both the industry’s less protective alternative for yarn manufacturing and the union’s proposed 100 limit: it found the former insufficiently protective and the latter technologically infeasible. (499–504 & nn. 14–23.)

The standard primarily used engineering and work-practice controls, with four years for full compliance, subject to infeasibility provisions. Respirators were required during interim and certain other conditions, alongside monitoring, medical surveillance, training, and warnings. An employee medically unable to use any respirator had to receive an opportunity to transfer to an available job at an acceptable dust level without losing earnings or other employment rights or benefits. The industry challenged that wage guarantee, rather than the transfer opportunity itself. (502–503, 536–537 & nn. 69–71.)

## Litigation and issues

Manufacturers, their trade association, and the National Cotton Council challenged the standard directly in the D.C. Circuit under OSH Act § 6(f). Labor organizations participated and had argued below that the standard was insufficiently stringent. The circuit largely upheld the standard, although it remanded the cottonseed-oil portion for inadequate economic-feasibility support. In the consolidated Supreme Court cases, the principal disputes were whether OSHA had to balance regulatory costs against health benefits, whether the economic-feasibility findings had substantial evidentiary support, and whether OSHA could impose the wage guarantee. The technological-feasibility challenge was not pursued in the Supreme Court. (494–495 & nn. 2–5; 504 n. 24; 529 n. 53.)

## Feasibility, rather than a new agency cost-benefit balance

Section 6(b)(5) instructed the Secretary to choose the toxic-material or harmful-agent standard most adequately assuring, to the extent feasible and on the best available evidence, that workers would avoid material health impairment over a working lifetime. Industry petitioners argued that the reduction in significant risk also had to bear a reasonable relationship to the costs of achieving it. They did not insist that OSHA assign a formal dollar value to every life or injury. The Secretary and unions answered that Congress had already chosen the governing balance: maximum protective reduction of significant risk, limited by technological and economic feasibility. (505–508 & nn. 25–28.)

Justice Brennan’s majority adopted that understanding. Feasible meant capable of being done, not desirable after a separate benefit-cost comparison. Congress placed worker health above other considerations except those making the protection unachievable. A less protective standard selected because its additional health benefits were deemed not worth an achievable cost would replace Congress’s balance with OSHA’s. Other enactments expressly directed agencies to compare costs and benefits; this one did not. (508–512 & nn. 29–30.)

Section 3(8)’s general requirement that a standard be reasonably necessary or appropriate did not override the specific feasibility command for this subcategory of health standards. Otherwise an agency would choose the level indicated by cost-benefit balancing rather than the most protective feasible level, effectively nullifying § 6(b)(5). But the Court preserved meaningful limits: the standard still had to address significant risk, and a redundant, unnecessarily burdensome technique accomplishing no additional protection might violate § 3(8). It left open possible balancing for other types of standards and for choosing priorities among different hazards. (512–514 & nn. 31–32; 509 n. 29.)

The legislative history supported the distinction. Legislators worried that a literal absolute-safety command could prohibit virtually every occupation. Amendments limited the provision to toxic materials and harmful physical agents, required material impairment, and retained feasibility as a limiting condition. Congress understood that substantial compliance costs would follow and treated protection as a cost of doing business, providing assistance for small businesses rather than authorizing OSHA to substitute a different health-cost tradeoff. The majority did not claim every piece of history was unambiguous; it found the overall record consistent with the statutory text. (514–522 & nn. 33–39.)

## Economic-feasibility evidence and appellate review

Under § 6(f), agency determinations were conclusive when supported by substantial evidence on the whole record, including contrary evidence. Because Congress assigned that review to courts of appeals, the Supreme Court would intervene only if the circuit misapprehended or grossly misapplied the test. It did not ask whether it independently would have reached the same factual assessment in the first instance. (522–523.)

OSHA evaluated competing estimates rather than simply ignoring costs. It rejected an approximately $1.1 billion engineering-control estimate by the Research Triangle Institute because it included synthetic-only machinery, inadequately accounted for existing controls, and lacked current industry data. It adopted a $543 million industry engineering-control estimate, combining other costs for a total of $656.5 million, but identified reasons that estimate itself could overstate cost, including existing compliance, modernization, developing technology, and possible synthetic-machine inclusion. Petitioners objected that the adopted estimate concerned less stringent limits for some operations than OSHA ultimately imposed. The agency acknowledged imprecision and limited access to underlying confidential industry data. The Court found its reasoned use of the best available evidence sufficient under the governing appellate standard, while recognizing that an estimate for the actual final limits would have been preferable. (523–530 & nn. 40–54.)

For industry impact, OSHA relied on a different part of RTI’s work that it had not found materially flawed. RTI had assessed a more expensive, more stringent uniform limit, costing approximately $2.7 billion, and still predicted no serious threat to the textile industry as a whole. Its study considered prices, demand, financing, returns, competition, employment, energy, and other effects. OSHA found the final standard within the covered industries’ financial capability, although marginal firms might close. The Court found no gross misapplication of substantial-evidence review in accepting that judgment. It understood the agency to predict maintained long-term profitability and competitiveness, and expressly did not decide whether a standard threatening those characteristics would be feasible. (530–536 & nn. 55–68.)

## Wage guarantee and exact disposition

The wage guarantee required a different result. Whatever OSHA’s underlying authority to require such protection, its methods had to be justified by their relationship to worker health or safety. The agency’s stated goal was minimizing the transferred employee’s adverse economic impact. Litigation counsel argued that workers otherwise might hide symptoms or refuse safer transfers to avoid wage loss. The Court acknowledged that this could be a valid health rationale and that supporting evidence existed, but post hoc advocacy could not replace the agency’s own required findings and explanation. Courts were not to search the record and formulate an unarticulated rationale. A boilerplate prediction that the protection would improve respiratory measures did not cure the inadequacy of the articulated fairness rationale. (537–540 & nn. 72–74.)

Accordingly, the Court affirmed the circuit in all respects except its approval of the wage guarantee. It vacated that portion and remanded with directions for further proceedings before the Secretary. This did not declare wage guarantees categorically unauthorized when properly connected to health goals; it rejected this issuance on the rationale and findings actually supplied. (540–541.)

## Dissents and nondelegation debate

Justice Stewart would have reversed for lack of substantial evidence without resolving cost-benefit interpretation. In his view, neither rejected RTI cost figures nor an overestimate for a less stringent standard supplied a rational estimate for the standard actually adopted. Assuming one overestimate happened to equal the additional cost of stricter limits was unsupported speculation, and the circuit’s acceptance was a rare gross misapplication warranting Supreme Court intervention. (541–543.)

Justice Rehnquist, joined by Chief Justice Burger, dissented on nondelegation grounds. He viewed the feasibility language as masking Congress’s inability to decide whether OSHA should be required, allowed, or forbidden to balance health gains against costs. Unlike ordinary ambiguity or legislative compromise, that omission transferred the fundamental choice between statistical lives and industrial resources to unelected officials. The majority answered that multiple proposed constructions alone did not establish unlawful delegation. Rehnquist’s closing note clarified that his objection was Congress’s failure to make the crucial choice, not merely the existence of competing interpretations. The assigned discussion emphasizes this disagreement, especially his statement at page 546 that resolving the underlying disagreement could have prevented enactment. (543–548 & closing n. *; 541 n. 75.)

Brennan wrote for White, Marshall, Blackmun, and Stevens, producing a 5–3 judgment with Powell not participating. Stewart’s dissent and the Rehnquist–Burger dissent were distinct grounds for disagreement. The complete 59-page official report was read, including majority notes 1–75 and Rehnquist’s unnumbered closing note; Stewart had no numbered notes. The assigned passage on PDF 58–59, printed pages 100–101, was read natively and in both original scans, with the Lowi/Ely editorial commentary distinguished from the two OSHA decisions discussed there.
