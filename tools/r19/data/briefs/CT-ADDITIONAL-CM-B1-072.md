# Frantz v. Cantrell

711 N.E.2d 856 (Ind. Ct. App. Apr. 30, 1999) | Contracts | CT-ADDITIONAL-CM-B1-072

The Indiana Court of Appeals affirmed a $3,904.97 bench-trial judgment for defective shingles. A merchant's implied warranty of merchantability did not depend on a special relationship with the shingles' manufacturer. The seller's experience and representations supported merchant status, and an asserted roofing-trade practice did not exclude the warranty without evidence that the homeowner knew or should have known of it. Persistent curling and failed sealing supported breach even though the roof had not leaked. Judge Kirsch wrote, with Garrard and Najam concurring and no separate opinion. (711 N.E.2d at 856–861.)

## A paid-for roof developed persistent defects

In summer 1994, Bill Cantrell hired Joseph Frantz and Frantz Lumber to install a new shingled roof on his residence for $1,985.15. Cantrell requested good-quality asphalt rather than fiberglass shingles. Frantz selected a three-tab asphalt product it routinely sold, carrying a twenty-year manufacturer warranty. Installation was completed that summer. With no apparent initial defect, Cantrell was satisfied and paid. (Id. at 858.)

During the following winter he noticed curling edges and tabs that failed to seal. He was concerned about both the roof's integrity and its appearance. A Frantz representative inspected it, found the workmanship competent, and recognized a shingles problem he had not previously encountered. The representative said he would contact the manufacturer. Later Frantz sent information describing cold curl, which should disappear when sunlight or warmer ambient temperatures warmed the roof. It did not explain the separate failure of tabs to seal. (Id.)

Cantrell waited until summer 1995, but neither problem corrected itself. He contacted Frantz again and eventually sued for breach of implied warranty. After a bench trial, the court awarded the repair or replacement cost less a credit for the roof's value and use. Frantz appealed the existence of the warranty, the evidence of breach, and damages. The appellate court reviewed the record favorably to the trial determination rather than making a new factual assessment. (Id. at 858, 860.)

## The buyer-seller relationship supplied the warranty

Under Indiana's Article 2, merchantability is implied in a sales contract when the seller is a merchant in goods of that kind, unless properly excluded or modified. It arises by operation of law from the relationship between that seller and the buyer. Consequently, Frantz's first argument—that a special seller-manufacturer relationship was required—failed. The manufacturer's twenty-year promise did not displace the seller's independent implied obligation. The court confined its analysis to merchantability, explaining in its sole footnote that ordinary-use fitness differs from fitness for a buyer's particular purpose. The shingles were used in the ordinary way. (Id. at 858–859 & n.1.)

Frantz next denied being a merchant. Casual or isolated sales would not qualify, but that description did not fit the evidence. The lumber-company family had been affiliated with the business since 1910; its accounting statements advertised all kinds of building material. Cantrell approached it because of its local construction and roofing reputation. Its representative promptly identified an asphalt shingle it supplied and knew roofing construction, installation, and the products involved. The statutory definition included both dealing in goods of the kind and holding oneself out professionally as having relevant knowledge or skill. This record supported merchant status. (Id. at 859.)

The seller also asserted a usage of trade under § 2-316(3)(c). Cantrell's own expert had testified that roofers traditionally gave no warranty on shingles themselves. A regular trade practice can sometimes modify an implied warranty, but the applicable rule required a usage shared by the parties' trade or one of which both knew or should have known. The parties were not in the same trade, and there was no evidence that Cantrell knew or should have known of the asserted custom. The testimony therefore did not establish an effective exclusion here. The court did not hold that usage of trade can never affect warranty obligations. (Id. at 859–860.)

## Merchantability included ordinary quality and appearance

A warranty plaintiff must establish the warranty, its breach, and resulting loss proximately caused by the breach. On appeal, the court would not reweigh evidence; it accepted supported ultimate findings from the bench trial unless clearly erroneous. The trial court found that failure to seal produced curled edges, an unsightly roof, and substantial potential for early failure. (Id. at 860.)

Frantz answered that shingles serve to protect a house from the elements. None had blown off, and the roof had never leaked, so it argued that their purpose was satisfied. The court rejected that narrow account. Merchantable shingles must conform to ordinary standards and average grade, quality, and value, and must pass without objection in the trade. Tabs that fail to seal and edges that remain conspicuously curled even in warm weather need not satisfy those standards simply because no leak has yet occurred. A flat, smooth roof with properly sealed shingles was relevant to merchantability; evidence of these persistent imperfections supported the finding of breach. This was a determination on the evidence of this roof, not a rule that every minor cosmetic variation creates liability. (Id.)

## Replacement cost less the benefit received was supported

Indiana §§ 2-714(1) and 2-715 allowed reasonable compensatory measures and foreseeable incidental or consequential damages. Cantrell's witnesses explained that the faulty roof detracted from the value of the whole property, looking fifteen to twenty years old despite being only four at trial. Eliminating the problem required replacement of the entire roof, stripping two shingle layers and starting over. That consequence was reasonably foreseeable from installing these faulty shingles. (Id. at 860–861.)

The replacement estimate was $4,897.55, a usual and customary local bid. Frantz offered no contrary evidence. The trial court credited the seller for the roof's value and use by subtracting half the original price, rounded to $992.58. The resulting $3,904.97 was supported and consistent with the Code. The appellate court affirmed both liability and that award; it ordered no retrial, allocation proceeding, or remand. (Id. at 861.)

## The assigned hybrid-contract comparison has a narrower judicial foundation

C&M's account begins on physical 294, printed 249, and its final sentence continues onto physical 295, printed 250. Both complete pages were read in native text and original scans. The book treats Frantz as an implicit gravamen approach because it applies Article 2 to faulty goods within a roof-installation transaction. The actual opinion does describe the installation bargain in its facts, but it neither names a gravamen test nor expressly decides that services predominate. Its three resolved questions concern merchantability's existence, breach, and damages. The book's classification is therefore an explanatory comparison, not a broader test announced by this panel. The adjoining 2022 amendment discussion is subsequent textbook context, not part of this 1999 holding. (Id. at 858–861.)

The complete actual report, 856–861, was read in preserved judicial text and all six original scans, including the sole footnote on 859 and the final disposition on 861. The court is Indiana's intermediate appellate court, although the assignment abbreviates its parenthetical to Ind.
