# Humphrey’s Executor v. United States

Assigned excerpt: casebook pp. 184–189, September PDF pp. 114–119; adjoining notes continue on PDF p. 120. ## Parties

Humphrey’s executor sued the United States for compensation allegedly owed to William E. Humphrey after President Roosevelt’s attempted removal of him from the Federal Trade Commission. Humphrey had died before the Supreme Court decided the case. The estate sought unpaid salary, not a judicial order placing a living commissioner back in office. The United States defended the removal as authorized notwithstanding the statutory enumeration of causes or, alternatively, as an exercise of presidential power that Congress could not restrict. 295 U.S. at 618–619, 621–622, 626.

The President was the official who removed Humphrey, but the defendant in the Court of Claims action was the United States. This posture explains both the monetary dispute and the Supreme Court’s task: it answered certified legal questions necessary to the salary claim rather than reviewing a completed district-court injunction against the President. Id. at 618–619, 632 & docket-title note.

## Material Facts

In December 1931, President Hoover nominated Humphrey to succeed himself as an FTC commissioner, and the Senate confirmed him. His commission specified a seven-year term ending September 25, 1938. The FTC Act created a five-member body, limited any single political party to three members, provided staggered initial terms and seven-year successor terms, and allowed presidential removal for inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office. Commissioners could not engage in other business or employment, and the Commission chose its own chairman. Id. at 618–620.

President Roosevelt requested Humphrey’s resignation in July 1933, explaining that the Administration’s objectives would be served more effectively by personnel of his selection while disclaiming criticism of Humphrey’s personal service. In August he referred to differences concerning Commission policy and administration and his desire for confidence in the official. Humphrey declined to resign. On October 7, the President notified him of removal effective that date. The Government did not claim that any of the three statutory causes existed. Id. at 618–619, 626.

Humphrey continued to assert his membership and entitlement to the annual $10,000 salary. He died on February 14, 1934. His executor sought salary accruing from October 8, 1933, to his death. The dispute therefore involved removal during an unexpired term for disagreement over policy and administration, rather than expiration of a term, misconduct found under the statute, or a contested original appointment. Id. at 618–619.

The Commission’s statutory functions were material to the constitutional analysis. It investigated unfair methods of competition, conducted notice-and-hearing proceedings, made findings, and issued cease-and-desist orders subject to judicial enforcement and review. It also investigated and reported to Congress and could assist courts as a master in antitrust equity cases. The Court described those functions as predominantly quasi-legislative and quasi-judicial. That description was the Court’s characterization of the statutory Commission before it. Id. at 620–621, 624, 628.

## Procedural History

The executor filed the compensation claim in the Court of Claims. That court certified two questions: whether the FTC Act restricted presidential removal to the stated grounds and, if it did, whether that restriction was constitutional. The Supreme Court first construed the statute and then addressed the constitutional objection to the limitation it found. Both questions were answered affirmatively. Id. at 618–619, 632.

The reported decision does not fix a final dollar award or order reinstatement. It supplies the controlling answers to the referring court, rejecting the Government’s asserted authority to remove this commissioner for reasons outside the statute. Treating the case as an ordinary reversal of a lower-court judgment would obscure its certified-question posture. Id. at 618–619, 632.

## Issue

Did the combination of a fixed statutory term and specified removal grounds prevent the President from dismissing an FTC commissioner for policy disagreement? If Congress intended that restriction, did the constitutional executive power nevertheless require unrestricted presidential removal of a commissioner performing the functions assigned to the FTC? Id. at 619–626.

## Rule and Holding

The FTC Act restricted removal during the prescribed term to inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office. Its text, structure, and purposes established that those grounds were limitations, rather than examples added to an otherwise unrestricted removal authority. No enumerated ground was asserted here. Id. at 621–626.

The restriction was constitutional for an officer of the character involved. The Court distinguished purely executive officers from members of the Commission it characterized as performing quasi-legislative and quasi-judicial functions. Congress could secure the Commission’s independence by fixing terms and limiting removal to statutory causes. The Court expressly reserved officers falling in the uncertain area between the categories described in Myers and this case. Id. at 626–632.

## Judgment

The Court answered certified Question 1 “Yes” and certified Question 2 “Yes.” Those answers established that the statute limited removal and that the limitation was valid, so the policy-based removal asserted in the certificate could not be justified by the Government’s theories. Further disposition of the salary claim belonged to the Court of Claims. The Supreme Court’s reported judgment itself contains no reinstatement order or calculated award. Id. at 632.

## Reasoning

The statutory inquiry came first because a constitutional conflict would arise only if Congress had actually limited removal. The Government relied on Shurtleff, where a statute listed the same kinds of removal grounds but supplied no fixed term for a general appraiser. Reading that enumeration as exclusive would effectively have produced life tenure unless misconduct or another specified cause occurred. The Shurtleff Court had rejected that unusual consequence without clearer statutory direction. Id. at 621–623.

Humphrey’s position differed because Congress expressly fixed the term. Combining a definite term with removal for designated causes ordinarily indicates that the term is protected against earlier termination absent such cause, unless another provision or circumstance points the other way. The language concerning initial commissioners’ continuation in office reinforced that reading; the Court found no persuasive reason to give initial commissioners protection while denying it to successors appointed under the same scheme. Id. at 623–624.

The Commission’s design further supported the textual conclusion. Congress contemplated experienced members exercising impartial expert judgment over time. Staggered, lengthy terms, limits on partisan composition, and the Commission’s adjudicatory and advisory responsibilities served that design. Legislative reports and the purposes reflected in debates supported independence from presidential policy direction. The Court used those materials to illuminate statutory objectives and the problem Congress addressed, while acknowledging limits on using debates to define statutory words. Id. at 624–626.

Allowing at-will removal would defeat the practical assurance of tenure. An official whose position depends on another official’s pleasure has a strong reason to follow that official’s wishes even without a formal command. For the Commission Congress created, such dependence would frustrate the independent judgment that the statute sought to secure. The Court therefore rejected the idea that a fixed term merely stated the maximum possible duration while leaving unrestricted termination available. Id. at 625–626, 629.

On the constitutional question, the Government principally invoked Myers, which had sustained presidential removal of a first-class postmaster despite a statutory requirement of Senate consent. The Court confined that decision to the purely executive office actually involved. Broad statements suggesting unrestricted removal of every civil officer exceeded the issue decided there and were not controlling precedent. The Court expressly disapproved those statements to the extent inconsistent with its present conclusion, while retaining Myers’s holding concerning the executive officer before that Court. Id. at 626–628.

The FTC’s work, as the Court analyzed it, implicated different institutional responsibilities. Its application of statutory competition standards involved functions resembling legislation and adjudication; its reports aided Congress; its service as a master aided courts. It could carry out those duties without executive permission. The Court treated incidental executive functions as ancillary to those principal responsibilities, including the statutory provision permitting presidentially directed investigations, which it regarded as collateral to the Commission’s central design. Id. at 628 & n.*.

The separation-of-powers argument accordingly supported the statutory protection in the Court’s view. Congress could create an agency to help implement legislative policy and assist judicial functions with independent judgment, and tenure protection was an appropriate incident of that arrangement. Unrestricted presidential dismissal would create an indirect means of controlling functions that the Court did not classify as the President’s own executive work. The Government’s broad theory, the Court observed, would also extend to bodies such as the Interstate Commerce Commission and the legislative Court of Claims. Id. at 629–630.

Historical materials did not establish a contrary rule for this office. The 1789 discussion emphasized in Myers concerned a department whose principal officer was responsible directly to the President. The Court also identified historical recognition that officers with judicial responsibilities could present a different removal question. It relied on those distinctions rather than deriving a universal rule from any one statement about removal in the founding period. Id. at 630–631.

The conclusion remained tied to the character of the office. The Court recognized one rule for purely executive officers and another for an office such as the FTC commissionership before it, then expressly left intermediate cases for later decision. Its judgment thus did not resolve every mixed-function agency, every possible statutory formulation of cause, or the procedures needed to adjudicate a genuinely disputed allegation of inefficiency or neglect. Id. at 631–632.

## Separate Opinions

No separately filed concurrence or dissent appears in this report. Justice Sutherland delivered the Court’s opinion. A statement at 632 records that Justice McReynolds agreed that both certified questions should be answered affirmatively and refers to his separate opinion in Myers, 272 U.S. 178, for his views on presidential removal. That reference is not a new separate opinion filed in Humphrey’s Executor. The disagreements described in the reasoning concern the Government’s arguments, Shurtleff’s construction of a different statute, and the reach of Myers; they should not be presented as separate opinions filed in Humphrey’s Executor. 295 U.S. at 618–632.

## Notes

Keep statutory interpretation and constitutional validity distinct. A reader must first identify whether the particular legislation protects tenure and then ask whether that protection is permissible for the office. The Court decided both questions, using the FTC’s structure and functions in each analysis. It did not make the label independent agency, standing alone, a substitute for examining those features. Id. at 621–632.

The remedy is another important limit. This was a back-pay case brought by an executor after the commissioner’s death. It demonstrates judicial review of a removal’s legality through a compensation claim, but it does not decide the availability or proper recipient of a modern reinstatement injunction. Nor did the Court define all conduct that could count as statutory cause; the Government asserted none of the enumerated grounds. Id. at 618–619, 626, 632.

This brief reports the 1935 decision and its own express reservations. The description of the FTC as quasi-legislative and quasi-judicial belongs to that opinion’s reasoning; it is not an independent characterization of every later agency power or a certification of present removal law. Later applications and changes must be considered from their own assigned opinions. The neighboring casebook notes raise related classification and statutory-variation questions, while the holding itself remains the answer to the two questions certified here. Id. at 628–632; casebook pp. 189–190.
