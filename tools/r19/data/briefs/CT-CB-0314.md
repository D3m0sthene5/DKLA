# Meyer v. Uber Technologies, Inc.
868 F.3d 66 (2d Cir. 2017) | Contracts

## Decision and exact posture
The Second Circuit held that the Uber interface used by Spencer Meyer gave reasonably conspicuous notice of its terms and that registering objectively manifested assent under California contract law. It vacated the denial of the defendants' motions to compel arbitration and remanded for the district court to decide whether their litigation conduct waived arbitration. The appellate court settled formation on the undisputed record; it did not order a trial on formation, decide waiver, or decide the underlying price-fixing merits. Judge Chin wrote for the panel of Raggi, Chin, and Carney. No concurrence or dissent appears. Slip opinion at 2, 31–32.

## Registration facts and the disputed notice
Uber's smartphone application connected riders to third-party drivers. On October 18, 2014, Meyer registered using a Samsung Galaxy S5 running Android. He later took ten rides in several American cities and Paris. Uber's engineer submitted a declaration identifying when and how he registered from company records and screenshots of the process then in use. The material facts were undisputed. Meyer recalled entering contact and payment information but did not recall seeing the terms link and declared that he had not read the terms or arbitration clause. Slip opinion at 4–7.

The first registration screen requested name, email, telephone number, and password, with options to register through Google+ or Facebook. Meyer entered information manually and clicked Next. The second, Payment, requested credit-card details or permitted PayPal or Google Wallet. Meyer entered his credit card. To finish he clicked REGISTER in the middle of that screen. Directly below the registration controls was a statement that creating an Uber account meant agreeing to the TERMS OF SERVICE & PRIVACY POLICY. The capitalized phrase was bright blue and underlined, linking to another screen containing a button through which the terms could be opened. Id. at 5–7 & nn.1–3.

The entire payment screen could be viewed without scrolling. It had a white background, dark explanatory text, and comparatively few items: payment fields, account-registration controls, and the notice. Although the notice was small, its contrast and position connected it to the act of registration. The court evaluated the interface at the actual size of Meyer's phone, rather than assuming enlarged exhibits reproduced the user's experience. The source addenda also show different scaled versions submitted in the record and appellate briefing. Id. at 6 n.2, 24–25 & n.9.

The terms in effect, last updated in May 2013, called for binding arbitration of disputes relating to the agreement or service, subject to small-claims and specified intellectual-property exceptions. They waived jury trial and class or representative participation and selected American Arbitration Association rules. The validity of the individual-arbitration agreement depended first on whether Meyer had agreed to the terms at all. The parties did not dispute that the alleged claims would fall within the clause's scope if formation were established. Id. at 7–8 & n.4, 13–14.

## Litigation history and competing positions
In December 2015, Meyer brought a putative class action against Uber's cofounder and former chief executive Travis Kalanick, alleging that the application enabled drivers to fix prices in violation of federal and New York antitrust statutes. His amended complaint initially named Kalanick alone. The district court denied Kalanick's dismissal motion and subsequently joined Uber as a necessary party. After discovery began, both defendants sought arbitration. The court denied their motions, finding insufficient notice and assent. It did not reach alleged waiver or Kalanick's ability to enforce terms he had not personally signed. Id. at 9–10.

The defendants appealed under 9 U.S.C. § 16, which permits an interlocutory appeal from denial of a motion to compel; the underlying action was stayed. They argued that the interface reasonably communicated its contractual terms and that clicking REGISTER manifested acceptance. Meyer defended the no-contract ruling, invoked his lack of actual awareness, and argued that the defendants' litigation conduct barred arbitration even if he had agreed. He also contended that formation needed further factfinding if the denial were vacated. Id. at 10, 21–22, 29–31.

## Governing law and why review was de novo
The Federal Arbitration Act places arbitration agreements on the same footing as other contracts but does not force parties to arbitrate without agreement. State contract law decides whether the agreement exists. The court accepted the district court's application of California law, observing that California and New York rules were substantially similar for this issue. Mutual assent is objective: a court considers words or conduct in their transactional context. An apparently accepting action does not bind an unaware offeree to inconspicuous provisions in a document whose contractual character is not apparent. Slip opinion at 11–16.

Actual awareness was absent here. An offeree nevertheless can be bound if a reasonably prudent user would be on inquiry notice of the terms and unambiguously manifests assent. For websites and apps, inquiry notice depends on design and content: the existence of reasonably conspicuous contractual notice and the relationship between the indicated accepting act and those terms. Calling an interface clickwrap, browsewrap, or sign-in-wrap is descriptive, not a replacement for this assessment. Id. at 15–20.

A motion to compel uses a framework similar to summary judgment. Courts consider relevant admissible evidence and draw reasonable inferences for the resisting party. A genuine factual dispute about formation may require a trial under FAA § 4. But undisputed facts can establish or defeat formation as a matter of law. Although notice and mutual assent often involve facts, the judge here had not conducted an evidentiary trial or made findings resolving disputed evidence. The screenshots and registration evidence were undisputed. Their legal sufficiency therefore received de novo review, not the deference given trial findings. Id. at 11–14 & n.6.

## Notice: the interface mattered more than whether Meyer read it
The court first rejected the defendants' argument that a general allegation in Meyer's complaint—that account holders must agree to Uber's terms—conclusively established his personal consent. That allegation did not obviously concede his knowledge; he offered to delete it with the court's permission; and he expressly denied awareness when registering. The court construed the record in his favor and decided notice on the actual interface. Id. at 21–22.

It then adopted the perspective of a reasonably prudent smartphone user. This user need not be assumed wholly unfamiliar with apps, online enrollment, or the common meaning of blue underlined hyperlinks. Against that background, the simple screen presented reasonably conspicuous notice. The terms statement sat directly below the registration button; its dark text contrasted with the white background; the hyperlink was distinct; and no scrolling was needed to discover it. Id. at 22–25.

This differed from the cluttered Amazon page in Nicosia, which had many links, varied fonts and colors, advertisements, and a notice separated from the action button. Here the court emphasized both spatial and temporal connection: the user encountered the terms where and when registering, before accepting benefits of the account. The statement expressly connected creating an account with agreeing to the linked terms. Access by hyperlink did not itself defeat notice, and a user could choose not to read terms after reasonable notice was supplied. Id. at 25–27.

The arbitration provision's location within lengthy terms also did not defeat formation. Unlike Sgouros, where a button communicated permission for a different purpose and did not adequately signal agreement to contractual terms, this screen accurately explained the legal significance of registration. Once opened, the terms included a bold dispute-resolution heading and bold jury-waiver language. The court did not require every clause to be printed on the initial screen. Id. at 27–28.

## Assent, nonsignatory enforcement, and the remaining waiver question
Meyer did not press a button literally labeled I agree, but express assent was not necessary where the interface unambiguously connected the accepting conduct to the terms. REGISTER simultaneously created the account and accepted terms that the notice had reasonably presented. The difference between the notice's word creating and the button's word register did not obscure their relationship. His entry of payment information to establish a continuing service relationship reinforced that objective interpretation. Slip opinion at 28–30.

Further proceedings were unnecessary on formation because the engineer's declaration and contemporaneous screenshots supplied an adequate undisputed record. In footnote 11, the court also held that Kalanick could rely on the agreement despite being a nonsignatory, citing protection of employees or disclosed agents of a contracting entity. This was an appellate ruling, not an issue left wholly undecided for remand. Id. at 29–31 & n.11.

Waiver remained different. Although arbitration-related waiver ordinarily may be for the arbitrator under the cited precedents, waiver founded on participation in court litigation can be decided by the district judge. Meyer's argument concerned the defendants' defense of this very action, so the court remanded for the district court to resolve it in the first instance. The opinion did not determine whether the litigation conduct actually amounted to waiver. Id. at 31–32.

## Judgment, comparison, and checked source
The order denying arbitration was vacated, and the case was remanded to consider waiver and conduct any further proceedings consistent with the opinion. Formation was established; ultimate entitlement to compel arbitration and antitrust liability were not finally adjudicated. Id. at 32.

Read together with Kauders, the case illustrates why a general assertion that Uber registration is enforceable or unenforceable is too broad. Meyer evaluated a particular Android interface under California law, with an uncluttered light screen, a REGISTER button, and closely connected blue underlined notice. Kauders applied Massachusetts law to a different three-step rider process that ended in DONE and deemphasized the legal significance of registration. This comparison concerns those historical decisions, not a certification of current Uber interfaces or current waiver law.
