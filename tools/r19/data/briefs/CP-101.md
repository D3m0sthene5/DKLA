case concerns federal privilege law and summons enforcement as decided in 1981.
Parties
Upjohn Company, a pharmaceutical manufacturer operating domestically and abroad, resisted an Internal
Revenue Service summons for materials from its counsel’s internal investigation. The United States sought
enforcement of the summons to investigate the tax consequences of questionable foreign payments. Gerard
Thomas, Upjohn’s vice president, secretary, and general counsel, supervised the investigation with outside
counsel; the documents sought included employees’ questionnaire responses and counsel’s interview notes and
memoranda. The corporate client’s claim of privilege was distinct from any personal legal representation of an
interviewed employee. 449 U.S. at 386–389, 394–395.
Material Facts
In January 1976, independent accountants auditing a foreign subsidiary discovered payments to or for foreign
government officials to obtain government business. Thomas consulted outside counsel and chairman R. T.
Parfet, Jr., and the company initiated an internal investigation. Counsel prepared a questionnaire sent under the
chairman’s signature to foreign general and area managers. It identified Thomas as general counsel, described
the possible illegality of the payments, requested detailed information, and directed that responses go to him.
Managers were instructed to treat the investigation as highly confidential. Thomas and outside counsel also
interviewed employees. These were communications intended to supply legal advice about the corporation’s
conduct, rather than a routine accounting survey simply copied to an attorney. Id. at 386–387, 394–395.
The inquiry concerned conduct within the employees’ corporate duties. Much of the relevant information was
unavailable from senior management; lower-ranking personnel had dealt with the transactions. An
accompanying worldwide policy statement emphasized legal compliance, prohibited using payments as
disguised bribes, and directed legal questions to general counsel. That statement helped show that even
interviewees who had not received the questionnaire understood the legal purpose. The responses and notes
were kept confidential within the counsel conducting the investigation. Id. at 394–395 & n.5.
On March 26, 1976, Upjohn voluntarily filed a preliminary disclosure with the Securities and Exchange
Commission and simultaneously supplied the IRS a copy. The IRS began investigating and received lists of
interviewees and questionnaire recipients. Its November 23 summons demanded investigative files concerning
payments and accounting since January 1, 1971, expressly including questionnaires and counsel’s interview
notes. Upjohn resisted those materials on attorney-client privilege and work-product grounds. The IRS
remained able to investigate the transactions directly and had interviewed approximately twenty-five of the
listed employees. Id. at 387–389, 395–396.

Thomas’s notes did more than transcribe answers. They selected important questions and responses, evaluated
their significance, connected them to other information, and identified additional inquiries. This mixed factual
and evaluative character was material to the separate work-product question. The full opinion also reports that
seven of eighty-six interviewees had already left Upjohn; the Court expressly reserved whether privilege
covered those former employees’ communications. Id. at 394 n.3, 397, 400 n.8.
Procedural History
On August 31, 1977, the United States petitioned the Western District of Michigan to enforce the summons
under 26 U.S.C. §§ 7402(b) and 7604(a). The district court adopted a magistrate’s recommendation to enforce
it. The magistrate had found waiver of privilege and had also treated the Government’s need as sufficient to
overcome work-product protection. Id. at 388–389, 399–401.
The Sixth Circuit rejected the waiver finding but restricted corporate privilege to communications from the
management group responsible for directing corporate action in response to legal advice. It remanded to
identify which interviewees belonged to that group. It also stated that work-product protection did not apply to
administrative summonses under § 7602. The Supreme Court granted certiorari to review both limitations.
Thus the dispute was not whether corporations could ever claim privilege; it concerned which corporate
communications qualified and whether the IRS summons displaced an independent protection. Id. at 385–389,
397–399.
Issue
Does federal attorney-client privilege exclude confidential, legally directed employee communications merely
because their authors lack authority to control the corporation’s response to counsel’s advice? Does the
work-product doctrine apply to an IRS summons, and may counsel’s interview notes revealing mental
processes be compelled upon the ordinary showing of substantial need and undue hardship? Id. at 389–402.
Rule and Holding
The control-group restriction cannot govern the corporate privilege question. The communications here were
protected: employees supplied information within their duties to authorized corporate counsel, at
management’s direction, to obtain legal advice, with awareness of that purpose and under confidentiality
instructions that were observed. Protection covers the communications; it does not immunize the underlying
facts from direct inquiry. The Court resolved these circumstances without prescribing an exhaustive formula
for every corporate investigation. Id. at 394–397.
Work-product protection applies in IRS summons-enforcement proceedings. Materials reflecting counsel’s
mental processes demand stronger protection than ordinary factual work product. The magistrate’s
substantial-need-and-undue-hardship analysis was insufficient for the interview notes at issue. The Court
reserved whether such mental-process material is absolutely protected and required a substantially stronger
showing than the Government had made. Id. at 397–402.
Judgment
The Supreme Court reversed the Sixth Circuit and remanded for proceedings consistent with its opinion. The
privilege holding protected the questionnaire responses and interview notes insofar as they disclosed the
covered employee communications. The remaining work-product issues required further proceedings under the
more protective standard. The Court did not decide Upjohn’s tax liability, approve the foreign payments, or
hold every record in the company’s investigative files privileged. Id. at 397, 401–402.

Reasoning
Federal Rule of Evidence 501 directed the Court to develop common-law privilege principles through reason
and experience. The privilege serves legal advice by encouraging complete, candid communication. Effective
advice requires both instructions delivered to decisionmakers and factual information delivered to counsel.
Restricting the latter to senior management defeats that information-gathering function in a corporation, where
employees who know what occurred often have no authority to decide the legal response. Id. at 389–392.
The Court connected that principle to the investigation. Employees below the management group had
participated in the questioned foreign transactions. Interviewing only the executives able to act on counsel’s
advice could leave counsel unable to discover the acts that created the corporation’s legal exposure.
Conversely, interviewing knowledgeable employees without confidentiality protection would discourage
candid answers. Employees outside senior management may also implement the resulting advice, so privilege
supports communication necessary for compliance in both directions. Id. at 391–394.
The legal purpose was supported by concrete circumstances. Counsel was acting in a professional legal
capacity; management authorized the inquiry; the questions concerned employees’ corporate work; the
questionnaire and compliance policy conveyed the legal significance; and the company maintained
confidentiality. Those facts tied the investigation to advice about securities, tax, foreign-law, shareholder-duty,
and potential-litigation questions. An employee’s rank alone did not resolve privilege. At the same time, the
Court did not announce that every conversation with in-house counsel is protected simply because counsel is
present. Id. at 394–397.
The Sixth Circuit feared that broader privilege would conceal corporate misconduct. The Court answered by
distinguishing facts from communications about facts. The IRS could ask an employee what payments the
employee made, to whom, and for what purpose. The employee could not make those facts undiscoverable by
reporting them to a lawyer. But compelling the employee to reproduce the confidential report to counsel, or
compelling counsel’s record of it, would expose the protected communication. Upjohn had supplied witness
lists, and the IRS had already conducted interviews. Access to counsel’s collected answers might be
convenient, but that convenience did not defeat privilege. Id. at 395–396.
The Court rejected the control-group test partly because it was unpredictably applied, while declining to
replace it with rules covering every possible investigation. This was a deliberate limitation on the decision.
Rule 501 contemplated case-specific development; the Court could identify why these communications
qualified without conclusively resolving all future boundaries. The express reservation concerning former
employees is particularly important: Burger’s broader formulation cannot be treated as the Court’s resolution
of that issue. Id. at 393–397 & n.3.
Work product supplied an additional inquiry because some notes extended beyond employee communications
to counsel’s analysis. The IRS summons statute contained no indication that Congress meant to eliminate the
established protection recognized in Hickman. Civil procedure rules applied to enforcement proceedings, and
Rule 26(b)(3) incorporated work-product safeguards. The Government conceded that the Sixth Circuit’s
categorical exclusion was incorrect, leaving the strength of protection as the contested question. Id. at 397–399.
The Government emphasized distant witnesses and restrictions Upjohn had placed on answers to questions it
considered irrelevant. Those practical difficulties addressed ordinary necessity. They did not adequately
account for what counsel’s notes revealed. Selection, phrasing, evaluation, and connections between answers
expose how counsel thinks about the investigation. Even a memorandum concerning facts can therefore reveal

Rule 26(b)(3) separately requires protection of counsel’s mental impressions, conclusions, opinions, and legal
theories when discovery of work product is otherwise ordered. The magistrate had applied the rule’s ordinary
substantial-need and undue-hardship language without giving effect to that additional protection. The Court did
not decide that no circumstances could justify disclosure of the interview materials; it held that a much stronger
showing was required and remanded the remaining claim for application of that instruction. Id. at 400–402.
Separate Opinions
Chief Justice Burger agreed with rejecting the control-group test, with protecting these communications, and
with the work-product analysis and judgment. His disagreement concerned the Court’s refusal to formulate
more explicit guidance. He would recognize protection at least when an employee or former employee, at
management’s direction, communicates with management-authorized counsel about conduct within
employment so counsel can evaluate whether it binds the corporation, assess its legal consequences, or
formulate a legal response. Id. at 402–403 (Burger, C.J., concurring in part and in the judgment).
Burger considered a clear minimum rule compatible with leaving other circumstances undecided. In his view,
acknowledging that predictable protection is necessary while declining to identify a governing standard
unnecessarily continued uncertainty. His proposed inclusion of former employees was broader than the issue
the Court actually decided, and should remain attributed to him. No Justice dissented from the disposition. Id.
at 403–404; id. at 394 n.3.
Notes
Analyze the materials separately. Confidential employee answers communicated for corporate legal advice
implicate privilege. Counsel’s selection and analysis of those answers implicate work product. The underlying
events remain discoverable through proper factual investigation. These categories overlap, but the opinion does
not make their requirements or exceptions interchangeable. Id. at 395–402.
The former-employee issue and the possibility of absolute protection for mental-process work product were
expressly reserved. The holding also arose under federal privilege law in a federal tax investigation; it should
not be presented as an automatic answer to every state-law privilege dispute. The assigned excerpt omits
Burger’s concurrence and some qualifying footnotes; the additional discussion above comes from the full
judicial opinion rather than an inference from the excerpt. Id. at 389, 394 n.3, 401–404.
