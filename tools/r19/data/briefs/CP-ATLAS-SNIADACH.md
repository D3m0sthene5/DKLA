# Sniadach v. Family Finance Corp. of Bay View

**395 U.S. 337 (1969) · Supreme Court of the United States · June 9, 1969**

## Earned wages frozen before the debt was adjudicated

Family Finance sued Christine Sniadach on a promissory note, claiming $420, and named her employer as garnishee. The employer reported $63.18 in earned but unpaid wages. It would release half as a statutory subsistence allowance and hold the remaining half subject to court order. Thus the immediate dispute concerned access to $31.59 of her wages, rather than a final judgment resolving the alleged debt. 395 U.S. at 337–338; id. at 346 (Black, J., dissenting).

Under Wisconsin’s procedure, a clerk issued the summons at the creditor’s lawyer’s request. The lawyer’s service on the employer froze the wages. The statute permitted up to ten days before service on the debtor, although Sniadach herself was served the same day as her employer. Her objection therefore was not that she lacked all notice of a lawsuit. It was that she received no notice and opportunity to contest the seizure **before** losing the unrestricted use of her wages. Id. at 338–339 & nn.1–3.

Sniadach moved to dismiss the garnishment proceedings on Fourteenth Amendment due-process grounds. The Wisconsin Supreme Court upheld the lower court’s approval of the procedure. Supreme Court review concerned the garnishment arrangement; the Court did not determine whether the debt was valid, fraudulent, or ultimately collectible. Id. at 338–339.

## Holding and exact judgment

The Court held that this prejudgment wage-garnishment procedure violated procedural due process because it deprived the wage earner of earned wages without a prior opportunity to be heard. A later opportunity to regain the money after prevailing on the debt did not cure the interim deprivation. The Court **reversed and remanded to the Wisconsin Supreme Court** for further proceedings consistent with its opinion. The report states the reversal; the official Court Journal supplies the express remand. Id. at 339–342; [1968 Term Court Journal](https://www.supremecourt.gov/pdfs/journals/scannedjournals/1968_journal.pdf), June 9, 1969, at 416, No. 130.

Justice Douglas delivered the Court’s opinion. Justice Harlan separately explained the precise basis on which he joined it. Justice Black dissented. Harlan’s concurrence and Black’s dissent, including the latter’s final addendum, are distinct from Douglas’s governing analysis. Id. at 337, 342–351.

## Why temporary deprivation counted

The majority treated the interim loss of enjoyment as a real property deprivation. Recovery following trial could release the money, but meanwhile the wage earner could not use earned funds or present a defense before they were frozen. The constitutional question concerned procedure, rather than whether the Court favored or opposed Wisconsin’s debt-collection policy. Meaningful notice had to support a real opportunity to contest the taking. Id. at 339–340.

Wages were also a specialized form of property with immediate economic consequences. Freezing them could severely burden a family’s ability to meet ordinary needs. The opinion discussed employment risks associated with garnishment, legislative investigations, collection-fee leverage, and the insufficiency of statutory allowances. A creditor could pressure a debtor who urgently needed access to wages into paying disputed demands or accepting additional charges. These observations supported the procedural concern; they were not findings that this particular creditor had imposed a fraudulent debt or that Sniadach personally experienced every hardship described. Id. at 340–342 & nn.4–9.

The Court rejected the inference that historical approval of attachment automatically validated every modern wage restraint. A procedure adequate for some property or setting might be inadequate for wages and their particular role. The opinion consequently resolved Wisconsin’s wage procedure without announcing that every conceivable prejudgment attachment was unconstitutional. Id. at 340.

## The exceptional-situation qualification

The majority recognized that summary action could satisfy due process in extraordinary situations requiring special protection of governmental or creditor interests. Neither the facts nor Wisconsin’s broad statute presented such a situation. Sniadach was a local resident readily subject to personal jurisdiction; there was no demonstrated special need to freeze wages first. The Court cited earlier emergency and specialized proceedings without defining an exhaustive exception list for all future deprivations. Id. at 339.

This qualification matters to the assigned account’s two questions: how far the rationale extends beyond wages, and what exceptional circumstances could justify postponing a hearing. The historical holding supplies a strong requirement for the procedure actually challenged while leaving those broader applications for other cases.

## Harlan’s account of the protected interest and required hearing

Harlan identified the property interest precisely as the use of the garnished wages between seizure and disposition of the underlying suit. That loss was not trivial. Same-day service, a later merits trial, and possible interim relief under uncertain circumstances did not supply the required predeprivation process. Id. at 342–343.

Outside special situations, he would require notice and a hearing directed toward establishing the underlying claim’s validity, or at least its probable validity, before the debtor lost property or unrestricted use. This formulation did not demand a completed full trial in every case; it identified the function that a prior hearing had to serve. His asterisk note recognized prior cases permitting summary administrative action to protect vital governmental interests and emphasized the absence of such justification here. Id. at 343 & n.*.

Harlan also answered Black’s methodological objection. He understood fundamental fairness through constitutional specifics and the Anglo-American legal heritage, rather than through judges’ unbounded preferences. He would not let an unexplained earlier per curiam attachment decision dilute the essential notice-and-hearing principles he drew from the Court’s cases. Id. at 342–344.

## Black’s dissent and addendum

Black viewed the majority’s hardship discussion as legislative policy judgment rather than constitutional adjudication. Wisconsin provided notice and a later opportunity to assert legal defenses, and its temporary restraint preserved property pending a debt determination. He considered the Court’s intervention an unwarranted return to open-ended due-process invalidation of state economic policy. Id. at 344–345, 348–350.

He also stressed the individual record. Sniadach had received same-day service, moved to dismiss before a merits trial, and challenged a $31.59 restraint. According to the Wisconsin court’s account, she had not alleged that she owed nothing on the note, established inability to post the available release bond, or shown that her employer had threatened dismissal. Black argued against relying on harms asserted on behalf of others and questioned whether state-law grounds limited federal review. These objections belonged to the dissent; the majority did not adopt them as reasons to sustain the procedure. Id. at 346–347.

Historical attachment precedents, including the earlier summary affirmance in *McKay*, reinforced his view that temporary restraint with notice and later adjudication was constitutionally sufficient. In his addendum, Black argued that appeals to fundamental fairness or legal heritage lacked definable limits and left judges to determine constitutional validity through their own sense of justice. Harlan expressly disputed that characterization. Id. at 348–351.
