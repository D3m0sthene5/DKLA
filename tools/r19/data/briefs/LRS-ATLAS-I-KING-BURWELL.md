# King v. Burwell

**576 U.S. 473 (2015) — Supreme Court of the United States — June 25, 2015**  
**Course:** Legislation and the Regulatory State  
**Assigned materials:** November part a, PDF 157–159, printed 937–938, and part b, PDF 34, printed 978. Physical pages 158 and 159 repeat printed 938. The first account describes King’s departure from Chevron deference; the second retains a later opinion’s footnote discussing King’s concern about agency expertise.

## Concrete facts and the plaintiffs’ claimed injury

The Affordable Care Act combined three reforms: insurers generally had to accept applicants without using their health to deny coverage or raise premiums; individuals generally had to maintain coverage or make an IRS payment; and qualifying households received refundable premium tax credits to make insurance affordable. The Court described these as interdependent responses to adverse selection. If healthy people could wait until illness to buy coverage at the same price, premiums could rise as the insured pool grew sicker, encouraging still more people to leave. The historical market experience and Massachusetts reforms supplied the majority’s explanation of the statutory design, rather than a finding that every insurance market would inevitably collapse. 576 U.S. at 478–482.

The Act also created insurance Exchanges. States could establish them, but the Secretary of Health and Human Services had to establish and operate a fallback Exchange if a State declined. Section 36B’s credit-calculation provisions referred to coverage through an Exchange “established by the State” under the specified ACA provision. The IRS issued a regulation making credits available through both State and federally operated Exchanges. At the time of the decision, Virginia used a Federal Exchange. Id. at 482–483, 484–485.

Four Virginia residents sued because they did not want to buy insurance. Their asserted injury arose from the interaction of credits and the affordability exemption, rather than from refusal to provide a benefit they sought. Without credits, their insurance cost would exceed eight percent of income, exempting them from the coverage requirement. With credits, the cost would fall below that threshold, requiring them either to obtain insurance or make the statutory payment. They argued that the IRS unlawfully extended credits beyond Exchanges actually established by a State. Id. at 483–484.

## Procedure, question, and exact judgment

The district court dismissed the suit, finding the Act unambiguously authorized credits on Federal Exchanges. The Fourth Circuit affirmed on a different interpretive approach: it found ambiguity and deferred to the IRS under Chevron. On the same day, the D.C. Circuit in Halbig reached the contrary conclusion in a separate case. The Supreme Court reviewed the Fourth Circuit judgment here; the Halbig litigation is procedural context, not another Supreme Court judgment covered by this brief. Id. at 484.

The question was whether §36B authorized premium tax credits for qualifying people obtaining insurance through Federal Exchanges. The Court held that it did and **affirmed the Fourth Circuit’s judgment**, while declining that court’s Chevron rationale. Chief Justice Roberts wrote for Kennedy, Ginsburg, Breyer, Sotomayor, and Kagan. Scalia dissented, joined by Thomas and Alito. The judgment was 6–3, with one Court opinion and one dissent, no concurrences or appendices. Id. at 476, 498–518.

## Why the Court interpreted the statute independently

The Court first asked who should resolve the disputed question. Even under the 2015 Chevron regime, ambiguity did not always imply delegation to an agency. The availability of these credits involved billions of dollars and insurance prices for millions of people, and it was central to the Act’s scheme. Congress would have spoken expressly if it meant to assign that extraordinary policy question to an agency. Delegation to the IRS was particularly unlikely because the agency lacked expertise in crafting health-insurance policy of this kind. Id. at 485–486.

That reasoning led the Court to decide the correct statutory reading itself. It did not invalidate credits simply because the issue was important, and it did not uphold them merely because the IRS had adopted one reasonable interpretation. King therefore illustrates a historical limitation on the presumed delegation underlying Chevron that nonetheless produced a result agreeing with the agency. Id. at 486, 497–498.

## Reading the words within the statutory scheme

The majority acknowledged the strength of the challengers’ ordinary-language argument. The ACA defined State to include the fifty States and the District of Columbia, not the Federal Government. But the Court examined all three parts of the tax-credit phrase—an Exchange, established by the State, under the designated statutory section—within the surrounding provisions. Id. at 486–490.

The federal-fallback provision directed HHS to establish “such Exchange,” referring back to the kind of Exchange the State was directed to create. The statute defined Exchange by reference to the same section governing State Exchanges, and the requirements for Exchange operations appeared there. Treating Federal Exchanges as outside that definition would make the Exchange-related requirements inapplicable to them. The Court also identified an analogous problem in the definition of qualified individual, which referred to residence in the State that established the Exchange: a strictly literal approach would leave Federal Exchanges without qualified individuals despite provisions governing their service to such individuals. Id. at 487–490 & nn.1–2.

Other provisions required all Exchanges to provide information about premium credits, display calculators showing insurance costs after credits, and report credit-related information to Treasury. These provisions supported the Court’s conclusion that the disputed phrase was ambiguous in context. The majority did not simply redefine the Federal Government as a State for every ACA purpose. Footnote 3 expressly left other provisions using similar language unaddressed because those provisions were not before the Court. Id. at 490–493 & n.3.

The Court rejected treating the surplusage canon as absolute. It noted inartful drafting and the unusual legislative process, while still insisting that the words be read as part of the entire statute. Its conclusion was that the text admitted a broader construction when read with the fallback and Exchange provisions, rather than that courts had a general power to disregard inconvenient statutory words. Id. at 491–492.

## Why the broader structure chose among the possible readings

After finding ambiguity, the Court considered which reading fit the interlocking reforms. Without credits, many people in Federal-Exchange States would be exempt from the coverage requirement because insurance would become unaffordable under the statutory threshold. Guaranteed issue and community rating would remain, but the incentive for healthy people to participate would weaken. The Court relied on studies predicting major premium increases and enrollment losses, and observed that the individual-market risk pool extended beyond Exchange purchasers. Those were projections cited to explain the scheme, not adjudicated events that had already happened because of this judgment. Id. at 492–495.

The challengers argued that Congress offered States an incentive to create Exchanges and expected them to accept it. The majority answered that Congress expressly created a federal fallback for States that refused; that was inconsistent with treating State refusal as a reason to undermine the fallback’s operation. Footnote 4 also responded to the dissent’s comparisons with territories and a separate long-term-care program, distinguishing uncertain territorial definitions and the smaller program from the Act’s central insurance reforms. Id. at 495–496 & n.4.

Section 36B’s internal architecture further supported the Court’s reading. Its opening provisions allowed credits to applicable taxpayers, including those within the stated income range and other conditions. The challengers’ interpretation would make that eligibility an empty promise in Federal-Exchange States through lower-level calculations yielding zero. The majority considered that an unlikely way to condition the viability of the entire reform. Footnote 5 addressed other tax credits that could similarly calculate to zero, distinguishing them from this comprehensive program. Id. at 496–497 & n.5.

The ultimate holding was that §36B allowed credits for insurance purchased through either kind of Exchange. The Court recognized the danger of turning interpretation into legislation but found the broader reading justified by the particular text, context, and structure. Id. at 497–498.

## Scalia’s dissent and the interpretive disagreement

Scalia agreed that context mattered, but argued it could clarify terms rather than rewrite them. He regarded “established by the State” as clear, reinforced by the Act’s definition of State, distinct State and federal establishment provisions, and repeated contrasts between Exchange and State-established Exchange. He thought the majority erased an operative limitation and gave the same phrase inconsistent meanings. Id. at 498–505.

He separately disputed the contextual inferences: calculators could report zero credits; outreach could explain that credits were unavailable; and general instructions could be inapplicable where their predicate did not exist. He defended the credit-calculation structure as ordinary tax drafting that could accommodate midyear moves and family members insured in different States. Id. at 505–509.

The dissent also challenged the majority’s use of purposes and market projections. In its view, Congress could have chosen State participation as an additional objective, and adverse effects could reveal a flaw in the enacted scheme rather than authorize judicial repair. Scalia argued that Congress, not the Court, should decide whether to change the law, especially where the disputed wording appeared repeatedly. His closing comparisons with earlier ACA litigation were his criticism of the Court, not additional holdings of King. Id. at 509–518.

## Assigned significance and historical boundary

The assigned account emphasizes the difference between a permissible agency interpretation and a judicially determined statutory meaning. The judgment supplied the latter, so the Court did not leave the IRS free under Chevron to choose the opposite answer merely because that answer could also be described as reasonable. The later assigned footnote again stresses the mismatch between this healthcare question and IRS expertise. Those materials illustrate the significance of King’s reasoning without converting every economically important question into a categorical ban on agency action. Assigned printed 937–938, 978.
