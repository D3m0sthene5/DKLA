# Staton v. Boeing Co.

**327 F.3d 938 (9th Cir. 2003) — United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, April 29, 2003.** Civil Procedure. Assigned: CivPro PDF page 440, printed page 852, the Staton account in Note 3. Judge Berzon wrote for herself and Judge Lay, sitting by designation; Judge Trott dissented. The panel **2–1 reversed approval of the consent decree and remanded**, while finding no abuse of discretion in the settlement class’s Rule 23(a) certification. The accompanying order withdrew the November 26, 2002 majority opinion and denied rehearing and rehearing en banc. This brief addresses the substituted 2003 opinion, not the withdrawn version or later litigation.

## Facts and the proposed exchange

African-American Boeing employees brought an employment-discrimination class action in 1998, alleging discrimination in promotion, compensation, career development, and working conditions. The class ultimately included approximately 15,000 employees across twenty-seven states, with hourly and salaried workers, supervisors and nonsupervisors, and employees from recently acquired businesses. Thirty-two named plaintiffs sought relief under Title VII, §1981, and other claims. Class counsel interviewed many employees and developed substantial informal documentation, although little formal discovery had occurred when settlement negotiations began. After negotiations starting in November 1998, the parties announced an agreement in January 1999. 327 F.3d at 946–947, 953–958.

The consent decree provided $7.3 million in monetary relief before reversions and opt-out adjustments; the actual payable total would be approximately $6.5 million. An initially identified group of 264 named plaintiffs and other participating class members, reduced to 237 after opt-outs, would receive more than half the nominal monetary award. These individually identified recipients were selected by class counsel and assigned awards ranging from $5,000 to $50,000, averaging approximately $16,500. Approximately 3,400 other members submitted claims under a points system, with average awards around $1,000. The identified group largely overlapped people who had signed individual retainer agreements and contributed toward counsel’s expenses. Id. at 944 n.1; 947–949 & 948 n.4.

Boeing also agreed to approximately $4.05 million in fees and costs, including $200,000 for objectors’ counsel, and spending associated with notice and purported injunctive benefits. Prospective provisions required various consultations, monitoring, and employment-system initiatives, but often left Boeing free to choose whether to adopt suggested changes. Some programs already existed, and the decree generally could not be enforced as a route to individual discrimination relief. The release reached broadly beyond race-discrimination claims to certain contract and tort claims. Monetary class members could opt out; the equitable class did not have that option. Id. at 945 n.2; 947–951 & nn.3–9.

Objectors challenged certification, allocation of damages, counsel fees, notice, limited discovery, and the substance of prospective relief. The district court permitted some discovery into alleged collusion and held two fairness hearings. Finding no direct collusion, emphasizing litigation risks and the advantages of compromise, it certified the settlement class and approved the decree in September 1999. Id. at 951–952.

## Certification and settlement approval are different decisions

The majority first upheld the district court’s exercise of discretion under Rule 23(a). Numerosity was undisputed. For commonality and typicality, the record went beyond a single employee’s experience extrapolated to everyone else: counsel had interviewed more than 1,300 employees and documented more than 200 experiences across locations and categories. The reported company-wide practices and broad cross-section of representatives supported the findings. The court declined to impose a categorical bar on classes mixing supervisors with nonsupervisors or employees from different facilities, unions, or acquired companies. Id. at 953–959.

This was a settlement-class determination under the then-applicable precedents, not a finding that every allegation of discrimination was proved or that trial management would necessarily be straightforward. The opinion expressly acknowledged possible difficulties if litigation continued. It also distinguished concerns over the settlement’s distribution from the asserted structural conflicts among employee categories: unfair allocation could invalidate the agreement without showing that all the identified workforce divisions required separate classes. Id. at 953–958 & nn.10–12.

Rule 23(e) separately required scrutiny of whether the entire settlement was fair, adequate, and reasonable. Judicial deference to compromise remained substantial, but absence of overt collusion did not settle the question. Counsel and selected participants could respond to incentives that disadvantaged absent members without entering a secret agreement. The judge therefore had to examine fee provisions and allocation of relief particularly carefully. Doubts about the decree’s release and largely consultative prospective relief intensified that scrutiny, although those concerns alone would not have justified reversal. Id. at 958 n.12; 959–963 & nn.13–15.

## Attorney fees: available methods must retain their safeguards

The defendants’ formally separate payment of counsel fees did not eliminate the class’s interest in their reasonableness. A defendant normally negotiates the cost of the whole package; excessive fees can be exchanged for less relief even when fees do not visibly subtract from a labeled damages fund. The district court had to review the fee award rather than accept the parties’ assertion that it was independent. Id. at 963–965.

Title VII and §1981 supplied statutory fee-shifting mechanisms. A negotiated statutory fee could be reviewed against the lodestar framework: reasonable hours times reasonable rates, with the adjustments and limits appropriate to that framework. But the parties supplied too little billing information and did not attempt that justification. Instead, they treated damages, fees, notice costs, and a valuation of prospective relief as a putative common fund, then argued that the fee was approximately 28 percent of it. Id. at 965–967 & n.17.

The court did not hold that fee-shifting statutes preclude common-fund fees. It held that either approach must preserve its own protections. Ordinarily the parties can settle the total common fund and counsel then seek a judicial award from it, leaving the remainder for the class. Alternatively, they can negotiate statutory fees with the merits and justify the negotiated amount under statutory standards. Here they fixed the fee as an indispensable settlement term but sought to justify it as a percentage of a supposed fund, limiting meaningful judicial control over the class-versus-counsel allocation. Id. at 967–972 & nn.18–22.

The qualifications in the footnotes are important: the described procedures were paradigms, not the only conceivable permissible arrangements. Equivalent safeguards could support variants. The opinion also discussed a previously approved, carefully supervised procedure using a mediator and detailed lodestar review. It did not prohibit every simultaneous discussion of fees and merits or require rejection of every negotiated fee. Id. at 970 n.20, 972 n.22.

The putative fund’s valuation had a separate flaw. Speculative, undifferentiated injunctive relief could not ordinarily inflate the denominator for percentage fees. Consultation and monitoring expenses were not necessarily accurately traceable benefits to individual members, particularly when already planned expenditures could count. Removing that estimate raised the apparent fee percentage to about 38 percent, materially above the circuit’s 25-percent benchmark. The benchmark was not an automatic maximum. Accurately measurable injunctive benefits could count in unusual circumstances; otherwise obtaining such relief could influence the appropriate percentage or support a lodestar analysis or cross-check. Reasonable notice costs could also count, but duplicative notice caused by the first notice’s defects should not. Id. at 972–975 & nn.23–24.

## Distribution and incentive awards

The approximately sixteenfold average payment difference between identified recipients and unnamed claimants lacked adequate support. General assertions that people who stepped forward necessarily had the strongest claims did not substitute for direct evidence explaining the awards. Selected nonrepresentative participants also received preferences, and the record suggested that some may have been rewarded for their individual financial relationship with counsel. One recipient, Cordell Bolder, was acknowledged not to be a class member. Id. at 975–978 & nn.25–27.

Staton does **not** ban reasonable incentive awards to named representatives. It requires individualized scrutiny of their actions, benefits to the class, time and effort, and reasonable concerns about workplace retaliation. Substantiated expenses and services may also be reimbursed appropriately. Credible allegations of actual or possible retaliation may support differential relief. But a broad preferred group cannot receive unsupported extra damages simply because its members helped counsel or took the risks any participant might face. A clearly nonmember’s purported damages payment must be removed; genuinely disputed membership can instead be part of the compromise. Id. at 976–978.

The majority’s concern was also practical: knowledgeable participants can help test whether a settlement fairly serves the whole class. Providing them special, unexplained benefits can replace that check with an incentive to maximize their own recoveries. The assigned quotation captures this reasoning; it should not be converted into a categorical holding that all additional compensation is unlawful. Id. at 977–978.

## Dissent, judgment, and source distinctions

Judge Trott would have affirmed. He stressed deferential review and the district court’s investigation finding no collusion. He regarded litigation risks, opt-out rights, counsel’s explanations of differential awards, and expert endorsements as sufficient support. Quoting declarations extensively, he considered the prospective legal assistance and employment initiatives valuable rather than illusory, and viewed the fees as a stand-alone component that did not diminish class relief. The disagreement therefore concerned both the practical value of the bargain and the degree of judicial protection required despite lack of direct misconduct. Id. at 979–986.

The exact disposition reverses approval and remands. The parties may try to justify the existing damages allocation and substantiate the fixed fees under statutory lodestar principles, or renegotiate the decree and use permissible common-fund procedures. The court cannot unilaterally force altered terms on parties whose settlement must stand or fall as a whole. Id. at 978 & n.28; 964 n.16. No judgment on Boeing’s underlying discrimination liability was entered.
