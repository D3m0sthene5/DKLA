# Reynolds v. National Football League

**584 F.2d 280 (8th Cir. 1978) — September 27, 1978 — Civil Procedure**

Chief Judge Gibson wrote for a panel with Judges Heaney and Bright; no separate opinion appears. The court affirmed approval of a $13,675,000 antitrust class settlement over objections by fifteen active players and one inactive player. It sustained certification under Rule 23(b)(1), rejected objections concerning notice and representation, and denied a motion seeking remand or a stay for judicial supervision of a separately negotiated collective bargaining agreement. (280–281, 289.)

## The dispute and the two negotiated agreements

The litigation followed *Mackey v. NFL*, which had held the Rozelle Rule unlawful under the Sherman Act’s rule-of-reason analysis. Under that rule, when a player’s contract expired and he joined another team, the new club owed compensation to the former club. If the teams could not agree, the commissioner could award players or draft choices. Mackey treated player-movement restrictions as a mandatory bargaining subject but rejected the labor exemption because the rule had not resulted from bona fide arm’s-length bargaining. The NFL withdrew its pending certiorari petition as part of the present settlement. Those were earlier-stage holdings and events, not a new merits trial in Reynolds. (281–282.)

The Players Association financially supported this action, brought by seventy-eight named players or former players on behalf of 5,706 current and former professional players. Counsel also represented the association. The NFL, the association, and class counsel simultaneously negotiated a labor agreement governing future player movement and a class settlement concerning past antitrust claims. The court recognized the practical relationship: potential antitrust liability threatened both recovery and the sport’s continuing operation. Nevertheless, evidence supported the district court’s finding that the labor agreement was not consideration for the class settlement. Settlement review concerned the damages compromise, with the labor agreement relevant as surrounding context. (281–282.)

District Judge Earl R. Larson approved the settlement. The appellate standard was abuse of discretion, giving substantial weight to his familiarity with the litigants, evidence, expense, and obstacles to success. The appellate court also ultimately found his factual findings not clearly erroneous. (282–283, 289.)

## Why mandatory-class certification was upheld

The objectors wanted Rule 23(b)(3) treatment because it would allow them to opt out. They contended that the action essentially concerned antitrust damages, which did not create incompatible standards of conduct. The court instead considered the action’s broader remedial scope: plaintiffs had sought changes to league rules as well as damages, and individual injunctions governing uniform player-movement practices could impose incompatible obligations on the NFL. By settlement, collective bargaining had removed the need for that equitable remedy, but it did not erase the action’s original scope for the certification analysis. (283–284 & nn.3–4.)

Rule 23(b)(1)(A) addressed incompatible standards for the opposing party. The league recognized the risk and had not attempted to accept or waive it. Rule 23(b)(1)(B) separately addressed practical prejudice to absent members: individual changes to common league rules would apply across the league and could dispose of or impair others’ interests. Each member had rights in a common organization and contract, and coordinated adjudication avoided unfair practical advantages. (284.)

The court said the case also appeared eligible under (b)(3) because common issues predominated and class treatment was superior. But under its historical framework, where (b)(1) and (b)(3) both applied, proceeding exclusively under (b)(1) was generally appropriate to avoid inconsistent adjudication and compromised class interests. The result was not a holding that unequal damages awards alone establish incompatible conduct, nor a current-law certification of mandatory treatment for every antitrust damages settlement. (284.)

## Notice and the demands for subclasses

The objectors argued that active and retired players, with different histories, contracts, and prospective interests, required subclasses. They claimed those differences undermined representation in negotiating the labor agreement. The panel answered that the court could not set aside that agreement in these suits; the settlement before it provided damages. Labor-related representational complaints could be addressed through an action alleging breach of the union’s duty of fair representation. The opinion noted that 453 of 478 eligible voting union members ratified the labor agreement. (284–285 & nn.5–6.)

With (b)(1) certification proper, the court applied settlement notice under then-Rule 23(e), constrained by due-process reasonableness. The manner and timing of notices for the April 25 and July 5 hearings were not seriously challenged. Their contents adequately allowed members to decide whether and how to object and afforded time to prepare. The complaint that notices did not announce judicial review of the labor agreement failed because that agreement was not before the district court for approval or disapproval. Notice did not have to announce a nonexistent opportunity in this proceeding. (285 & n.7.)

## Conflicts of interest and the covenant not to sue

All class members had an interest in past damages, while only some had a substantial economic interest in future movement rules. The panel found that this theoretical division did not establish an actual conflict by representatives or counsel. Drawing on its union-representation precedent, it distinguished antagonism affecting the subject matter and economic interests of the class from disagreements about remedies, political factionalism, or the wisdom of suit. Evidence showed any bargaining tension had been resolved in favor of damages: money otherwise available to the union pension fund had increased the class settlement fund. (285–286.)

Objectors viewed the new first-refusal/compensation arrangement as more restrictive than the Rozelle Rule. The court compared 168 players playing out options in two years under the new rule with 176 in eleven years under the old one, but refused to decide whether an optimum labor agreement had been obtained. Its discussion of competitive balance supported that restraint rather than a formal antitrust merits adjudication of every contractual term. Footnote 8 describes the offer deadlines, matching right, draft-choice compensation, hardship exception, and some unresolved interpretive questions. (286–287 & n.8.)

The covenant not to sue also did not require judicial approval of the labor agreement. Old Rozelle Rule claims could be met by res judicata arising from the class judgment. Future claims concerning the new agreement could encounter the labor exemption if bona fide bargaining requirements were satisfied, which the panel thought highly likely but expressly did not decide as a necessary question. The covenant was superfluous to the matters properly before the district court. Other disputes concerning its breadth had to use contractual machinery or applicable labor or constitutional principles. Footnote 9 preserves exceptions for grievances about breaches and previously commenced individual actions. (287–288 & n.9.)

## Exact result and assigned significance

The class sought remand or a stay based on purported new evidence that teams interpreted the labor agreement differently from the association. The panel treated this as an ordinary dispute for arbitration and labor-law channels, not a reason to supervise that agreement within the settled class action. It denied the motion and affirmed the order approving the settlement, finding no abuse of discretion in either the compromise or its complicated distribution formula. (288–289.)
