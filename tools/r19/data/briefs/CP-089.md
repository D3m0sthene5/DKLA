Hansberry v. Lee
Citation
311 U.S. 32, 61 S. Ct. 115, 85 L. Ed. 22 (Supreme Court of the United States 1940). Argued October 25,
1940; decided November 12, 1940. Opinion by Justice Stone; Justices McReynolds, Roberts, and Reed

Casebook pp. 804–808; source PDF pp. 417–419. Full judicial opinion.
Parties
Lee and other property owners sought to enforce a racially restrictive agreement against the Hansberry
purchasers and persons involved in their acquisition of Chicago property. The Hansberry parties were
defendants below and petitioners in the Supreme Court. None had been a party to, or was shown to be in
privity with a party to, the earlier enforcement action, Burke v. Kleiman. 311 U.S. 37–39; CB 804–805.
Material Facts
The agreement restricted sale, leasing, and occupancy of land by Black persons and was to become effective
only if owners of 95 percent of the relevant frontage signed. The Hansberry parties challenged satisfaction of
that condition. In Burke, the litigants had stipulated that the required percentage had signed, and the court
enforced the agreement. That litigation principally concerned whether neighborhood changes had made
enforcement inequitable, rather than actual proof of the percentage of signatories. 311 U.S. 37–40; CB
804–805.
In the later Hansberry litigation, the trial court found that only about 54 percent of the frontage was
represented by signatories. It nevertheless treated the earlier determination as conclusive. The owners’
interests were potentially conflicting: a signer could wish to enforce restrictions on others’ land while
wishing to resist restrictions on the signer’s own property. Agreement membership alone therefore did not
establish shared litigation interests. 311 U.S. 38–40, 44–45; CB 804–805, 806–807.
Procedural History
The Illinois circuit court found the earlier stipulation false and fraudulent but entered an injunction because
it considered the percentage question already adjudicated. The Illinois Supreme Court agreed that the
stipulation was untrue but rejected the finding of fraud or collusion. It affirmed on the theory that Burke had
been a representative action binding the relevant class, including the present defendants. The United States
Supreme Court granted certiorari to decide the Fourteenth Amendment objection. 311 U.S. 38–40; CB
804–805.
Issue
Could Illinois constitutionally bind absent persons to the earlier judgment upholding the restrictive
agreement when the earlier litigants did not adequately represent those persons’ substantial interest in
resisting the agreement?

Rule and Holding
A personal judgment ordinarily does not bind a person who was not a party. Proper representative litigation
can provide an exception, but its procedure must fairly protect the interests of absent persons who will be
bound. Due process was violated here because the earlier litigants could not be treated as adequate
representatives of the present defendants’ conflicting interests. 311 U.S. 40–46; CB 805–808.
A state’s characterization of a lawsuit as a class action does not conclusively establish its constitutional
power to bind absentees. The Court must examine the procedures and the actual relationships and interests
involved. The Fourteenth Amendment does not require states to adopt a particular federal class-action
procedure, but it does require adequate protection before representative preclusion may operate. 311 U.S.
40–43; CB 805–806.
Judgment
The Court reversed the Illinois Supreme Court. The earlier decree could not constitutionally foreclose these
defendants’ challenge to satisfaction of the agreement’s signature condition. The decision rests on
inadequate representation and the binding effect of the earlier judgment; it does not adjudicate a general
constitutional prohibition on judicial enforcement of racially restrictive covenants. 311 U.S. 37, 44–46; CB
804–808.
Reasoning
Class litigation developed in equity to address situations in which joining every interested person was
impracticable. It can permit an adjudication to proceed and, in appropriate circumstances, bind absent
persons. But allowing a case to proceed despite absent parties and determining whether a resulting judgment
binds those parties are distinct inquiries. The latter requires attention to the protection actually afforded their
interests. 311 U.S. 41–43; CB 805–806.
Common membership in this agreement was inadequate. Its promises were several, rather than a single joint
obligation: each signer potentially had both enforcement rights and burdens. Persons seeking performance
did not necessarily have the same interests as persons resisting performance. Treating them as one class
would permit a group pursuing one side of that conflict to determine the rights of absent persons pursuing
the other. 311 U.S. 44–45; CB 806–807.
The Burke plaintiffs sought enforcement on behalf of themselves and similarly situated owners. That
position did not represent the Hansberry parties’ interest in defeating enforcement. Nor could the earlier
defendants supply the missing representation: the pleadings did not designate a defendant class, the decree
did not purport to bind one, and the defendants’ interests in defeating the agreement were not shown to
outweigh their interests in establishing its validity. 311 U.S. 45–46; CB 807–808.
Finality alone could not answer the constitutional objection. The earlier court might have authority to decide
an issue, even erroneously, between the persons before it, but that did not establish authority to decide the
same issue conclusively for inadequately represented absentees. The present parties were entitled to contest
whether the representative exception applied to them. 311 U.S. 39–43, 45–46; CB 805–808.
The opinion leaves room for representative treatment of a genuinely common issue where the procedure
ensures aligned interests and full, fair consideration. It decides the inadequacy of this representation rather
than rejecting class litigation generally. The absence of a final fraud finding did not save the Illinois
judgment; the conflict and lack of adequate representation independently supplied the due-process defect.

Separate Opinions
Justice Stone wrote the Court’s opinion. Justices McReynolds, Roberts, and Reed concurred in the result
without a separate explanation. That concurrence notation appears in the full opinion but is omitted from the
casebook excerpt. 311 U.S. 46 (full opinion).
Notes
The examination point is representation, not simply the correctness of the first decision or the absence of
formal consent. Ask whose interests the representatives pursued, whether those interests conflicted with the
absentee’s, and whether the earlier action actually undertook to represent that absentee. A common legal
instrument can create opposing interests rather than a single adequately represented class. 311 U.S. 40–46;
CB 805–808.
Read the case alongside rules on nonparty preclusion and the constitutional limits of class adjudication. Its
narrow result permits this challenge to the covenant’s signature threshold; its broader contribution requires
meaningful protection of absentees before a representative judgment binds them. The surrounding casebook
note identifies the later, distinct substantive constitutional question about enforcing racial covenants. 311
U.S. 42–46; CB 806–808 and note 1.
