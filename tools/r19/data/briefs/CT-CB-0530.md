# Mitchill v. Lath
247 N.Y. 377, 160 N.E. 646 (N.Y. 1928) | Contracts | Integration and a purported collateral oral promise

## The icehouse and the completed purchase

In fall 1923 the Laths offered their farm for sale. Across the road, on land owned by Lieutenant Governor Lunn, stood an icehouse the Laths owned and could remove. Catherine Mitchill found it objectionable when inspecting the farm. The trial court found that the sellers promised to remove it in spring 1924 in consideration of her buying the farm. Relying on that promise, she proceeded with an $8,400 purchase for cash and a mortgage, accepted a deed, took possession, and spent substantial sums improving the farm as a summer residence. The sellers did not remove the icehouse and did not intend to. The issue was whether that promise could be enforced in equity despite its omission from the written sale contract. (247 N.Y. at 379.)

Special Term entered judgment for Mitchill in an action for specific performance of the removal promise, and the Appellate Division unanimously affirmed. The sellers obtained permission to appeal. A detail recovered from the full opinion is that the written sale agreement formally named her husband, while the oral undertaking and ultimate deed concerned her. The majority rejected an effort to use the formal distinction to escape the integration issue: she was the transaction's principal throughout, so the contract would be treated as made by her in substance. (Id. at 378, 383–384.)

## A collateral label could not settle the scope of the writing

Judge Andrews described the parol evidence rule as substantive law defining the contract, rather than merely a rule regulating trial evidence. It excludes attempts to change an integrated writing through prior oral understandings, but leaves genuinely distinct and independent collateral contracts enforceable. The difficult cases arise when a written and an oral promise share consideration. Although separate agreements with separate consideration can readily be distinct, one bargain induced by another may be too closely connected to separate legally. Calling a term collateral cannot resolve that problem, because almost any omitted promise could be restated in that form. Substance and degree of connection control. (Id. at 379–380.)

The majority identified three necessary conditions for this kind of collateral undertaking: it must be collateral in form; it must not contradict an express or implied term of the writing; and it must be something parties would not ordinarily be expected to put into that writing. The third condition asks whether the document, read with surrounding circumstances, appears to define the parties' engagements completely or whether the purported extra promise is part of the principal transaction. These are related inquiries into the reach of integration, not a rule permitting every noncontradictory oral addition. (Id. at 381.)

The icehouse promise failed the third condition. The contract detailed the buyer's price, apportioned financial charges, possible survey, the sellers' deed obligations and expenses, included personal property, a lien securing payments and title expenses, the risk of fire, and the broker's commission. Taken together, those provisions appeared to state the reciprocal obligations fully. Knowing the buyer objected to the nearby icehouse did not alter that appearance: if removal had been agreed as part of this purchase, including it in the sale agreement would have been natural. The promise was collateral in form but sufficiently close in substance that it could not be proved to enlarge the written bargain. (Id. at 381–382.)

Andrews suggested that imposing an unstated seller obligation might also contradict an implied limitation in the writing, but expressly declined to decide the second condition. The holding rests on the third. Nor did the court reject relief because Mitchill failed to persuade the trial judge that the promise had been made. It barred giving that established oral agreement legal effect beside the complete written sale agreement. The distinction is between failure of factual proof and substantive preclusion despite proof. (Id.)

## Precedents, policy, and the deed distinction

The majority defended integration as generally promoting reliable transactions despite occasional hardship. It compared cases refusing proof of landlord promises that induced written leases, and an earlier assumption that an oral barn-removal promise inducing a land purchase was improper. Other jurisdictions allowed some additional obligations, but those approaches did not control New York's treatment of this writing. (Id. at 380, 382–383.)

One qualification omitted from the casebook excerpt matters. Cases allowing collateral agreements made before delivery of a deed did not necessarily govern this dispute. A deed's fixed form can be inappropriate for additional contractual promises, potentially supporting an exception. The court was addressing a prior written sale contract, where the parties could include their reciprocal obligations, rather than treating the deed itself as necessarily recording every promise. It also distinguished a borderline decision involving a writing found to embody only part of an entire agreement. Here the sale agreement appeared complete for the transaction at issue. (Id. at 383.)

## Lehman's dissent: completeness has a subject-matter boundary

Judge Lehman, joined by Judge Crane, accepted the majority's general rule but disagreed about the writing's scope. The contract completely integrated the conveyance: what land would pass, the deed, price, and conditions of closing. But the icehouse stood on other land, and removal would occur after completion. Enforcing that promise left the sale's recorded obligations unchanged. Shared consideration connected the agreements, yet did not necessarily require integrating a later act on a different parcel into the conveyance contract. (Id. at 384–386.)

For Lehman, the second condition could not independently decide the case. There was no express term barring additional obligations; implying one would depend on the same scope question posed by the third condition. Thus the central inquiry remained whether parties ordinarily would include this promise in the writing. A document alone could not show everything it was intended to cover without comparison to the negotiations and their subject matter. Completeness as to conveyance did not mean completeness as to every associated undertaking. (Id. at 385–387.)

Lehman emphasized that the removal agreement had been established by overwhelming evidence, but did not make that evidentiary strength an exception to integration. Hardship could not justify disregarding the rule. Instead it clarified the real question: whether the parties intended the writing to render the separate promise ineffective. In his view its limited conveyance subject did not show that intention regarding later work on land not conveyed. He would affirm the lower courts. (Id. at 387–388.)

## Disposition and reading scope

The Court of Appeals reversed both the Appellate Division and Special Term judgments and dismissed the complaint, with costs in all courts. It did not remand for another trial or compel removal. Andrews wrote, joined by Chief Judge Cardozo and Judges Pound, Kellogg, and O'Brien. Lehman dissented with Crane: a five-to-two division. The decision issued February 14, 1928. (Id. at 378, 384, 388.)
