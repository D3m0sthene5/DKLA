# Taylor v. Sturgell

## Citation

Taylor v. Sturgell, 553 U.S. 880 (2008). Supreme Court of the United States. Argued April 16, 2008; decided June 12, 2008. Justice Ginsburg delivered the opinion for a unanimous Court. Assigned excerpt: Civil Procedure casebook printed pp. 1349–1356, physical PDF pp. 679–683, including all four editorial notes. Full judicial opinion: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-553/pdf/USREPORTS-553-880.pdf. References to Parts identify the Court's own divisions in the linked opinion. 

## Parties

Brent Taylor was the plaintiff in the second Freedom of Information Act action and petitioner in the Supreme Court. The Federal Aviation Administration, represented through its administrator, was the government defendant. Fairchild, the aircraft manufacturer's successor, intervened to protect its asserted interest in technical documents. Greg Herrick had brought the earlier, unsuccessful FOIA action seeking the same materials. Taylor and Herrick were friends and shared interests in antique aircraft, but they were distinct requesters asserting separate claims to disclosure. 553 U.S. at 885–890, Part I. 

The FAA and Fairchild argued that Herrick's loss barred Taylor through a doctrine called virtual representation. Their defense required a basis for binding Taylor despite his absence from Herrick's litigation. Taylor sought the documents in his own suit and challenged the preclusion ruling, not the validity of the final judgment against Herrick as between the parties to that earlier case. Id. at 889–892, Parts I–II. 

## Material Facts

Herrick owned a vintage Fairchild F-45 airplane and sought FAA-held technical drawings to assist restoration. The manufacturer had supplied those materials to the agency during certification. The agency withheld them under FOIA Exemption 4, which protects specified commercial information, including trade secrets. Herrick relied on a 1955 letter authorizing public access for replacement-parts purposes to argue that any trade-secret protection had been waived. Id. at 885–887, Part I.A. 

Herrick lost in federal district court in Wyoming. The Tenth Circuit concluded that the 1955 authorization had removed the documents' secret status, but affirmed based on Fairchild's later objection to disclosure and the arguments Herrick had preserved. It did not decide whether trade-secret protection legally could be restored or whether restoration after a FOIA request would be effective, because those arguments had not been properly presented. Taylor later sought to raise those questions. Id. at 886–889, Part I.A–B. 

Less than a month after the Tenth Circuit's judgment, Taylor made his own request for the materials. He was president of an antique-aircraft association to which Herrick belonged. Herrick had asked Taylor to help restore the plane, but they had no contract or agreement for Taylor’s participation. The record showed friendship, a shared lawyer, and the transfer of litigation documents. It did not show that Taylor controlled or financed Herrick's suit, participated in it, agreed to be bound, or even knew about it while it was being litigated. Those distinctions mattered because similarity of interests is not identical to legal representation. Id. at 887–890, Part I.B. 

When the FAA did not respond to Taylor's request, he sued in the District of Columbia. Fairchild intervened and asserted preclusion. The lower courts used the relationships between the two requesters to treat Herrick as Taylor's virtual representative, even though Herrick had not purported to sue on Taylor's behalf. Id. at 888–890, Part I.B. 

## Procedural History

The district court entered summary judgment against Taylor on preclusion grounds. The D.C. Circuit affirmed under a multi-factor virtual-representation test. Its framework required identity of interests and adequate representation, plus an additional connection such as a close relationship, substantial participation, or tactical maneuvering. It relied on the requesters' aligned objectives and close association; it did not definitively find tactical manipulation because the facts were ambiguous. Id. at 889–891, Part I.B. 

The Supreme Court rejected that broad doctrine, vacated the D.C. Circuit's judgment, and remanded. It examined recognized exceptions to the rule against nonparty preclusion and concluded that only a possible agency relationship in the second suit remained open on this record. The lower courts could determine whether Taylor was pursuing his action subject to Herrick's control as an agent for a person already bound. The Court did not decide that such an agency existed, order disclosure, or resolve Exemption 4. Id. at 903–907, Part IV. 

## Issue

May a court bind a nonparty to a prior judgment based on an equitable assessment of shared interests, friendship, common counsel, and related litigation objectives, even without a recognized representative relationship or other established exception? If the second litigant may be acting as the first litigant's agent, what must the courts examine and who bears the burden of proving preclusion? Id. at 891–893, 903–907, Parts II–IV. 

## Rule and Holding

A person ordinarily is not bound by a judgment entered in litigation to which that person was not a party. The Court rejected an expansive virtual-representation doctrine that permits nonparty preclusion through an open-ended balancing of relationships and aligned interests. Federal common law governs the preclusive effect of a federal-question judgment, subject to due process, and must operate within established grounds for binding nonparties. Id. at 891–898, Parts II–III.A. 

The Court organized those grounds into six categories: agreement to be bound; certain preexisting substantive legal relationships; adequate representation in an appropriate representative proceeding; control of the earlier litigation; a later suit pursued as an agent or proxy for a bound party; and a special statutory scheme consistent with due process. The categories were a framework for analysis rather than an asserted exhaustive code covering every possible situation. Id. at 893–895 & n.6, Part II. 

Adequate representation generally requires aligned interests and either an understanding that the first party was acting representatively or protective measures taken by the first court; some settings also require notice. Mere similarity of interests and effective advocacy do not suffice. Taylor had not been adequately represented in Herrick's suit under these standards. Whether Taylor was now acting as Herrick's agent required further consideration, and the defendants bore the burden of pleading and proving the affirmative defense. Id. at 900–901, 904–907, Parts III.B, IV. 

## Judgment

The Supreme Court vacated the D.C. Circuit's judgment and remanded for further proceedings. Virtual representation could not support the judgment entered below. The remaining inquiry concerned a possible agency relationship in Taylor's prosecution of the later suit. No determination was made that Taylor was entitled to the FAA documents or that the information was outside Exemption 4. Id. at 903–907, Part IV. 

## Reasoning

The Court began by distinguishing the finality owed to a judgment from the identity of the people it can bind. Claim and issue preclusion protect parties from repetitive litigation and inconsistent results, but those objectives do not give a court unlimited authority over people absent from the first proceeding. A person who did not receive the relevant opportunity to litigate generally retains a claim even when someone else has sought similar relief unsuccessfully. Id. at 891–893, Part II. 

The recognized exceptions depend on relationships or procedures that justify treating the absent person's interests as represented or the later litigation as an attempt by a bound party to relitigate. A successor to property, for example, occupies a legally defined relationship to an earlier owner. A properly conducted class action uses procedures designed to represent absent members. A person who actually controls another's lawsuit has already exercised a litigation opportunity through that nominal party. These grounds require more than a court's conclusion that two people cared about the same outcome. Id. at 893–895, Part II. 

The D.C. Circuit's flexible test diluted those protections. It allowed shared interests and close relationships to substitute for an actual representative arrangement. That could produce the equivalent of a class action without the certification, notice, representation, and court-supervision protections governing class proceedings. The Court rejected the proposition that a successful lawyer's efforts for one client automatically counted as representation of other people with similar objectives. Id. at 898–901, Part III.B. 

The respondents relied on general language in earlier cases about justice and equity in preclusion. The Court read that language in context. Decisions allowing nonmutual issue preclusion against a party who already litigated do not authorize binding an unrelated nonparty who never had a hearing. In particular, the flexibility discussed in Blonder-Tongue concerned whether an existing litigant had received a full and fair opportunity, not a general power to designate absent people represented after the fact. Id. at 898–900, Part III.B. 

The broader doctrine also weakened predictability. A multi-factor inquiry into friendship, common interests, lawyer identity, and tactical motives could require extensive discovery before anyone could know whether an absent person's claim survived. Established exceptions provide more determinate legal questions. The Court did not deny all factual inquiry, as its agency remand demonstrates; it rejected a diffuse fairness assessment as an independent ground of preclusion. Id. at 901, Part III.B. 

FOIA's public purpose did not make every requester the representative of all potential requesters. The statute directs an agency to make information available to the person making a qualifying request. Success produces relief for that requester, and Congress had imposed no general statutory restriction on successive suits by different requesters. The Court declined to create such a restriction through an unusually broad common-law preclusion doctrine. Id. at 902–903, Part III.C. 

Nor did the possibility of repetitive requests justify altering the ordinary rule. Precedent can resolve recurring legal issues without making all citizens parties to the first case. Litigation costs also discourage unproductive repetition. The FAA had not identified the predicted pattern of abusive suits in jurisdictions rejecting broad virtual representation. The Court therefore regarded existing devices as adequate reasons not to sacrifice the ordinary nonparty protection. Id. at 902–904, Part III.C. 

Applying the established categories, the Court found no agreement to be bound, substantive legal relationship, control of the first suit, or special statutory scheme. Adequate representation also failed: nothing showed that Herrick understood himself to represent Taylor, that Taylor knew about the case, or that the Wyoming court protected Taylor's interests. The defendants could not cure those omissions simply by describing Herrick's representation of himself as competent. Id. at 904–905, Part IV.A. 

The possible agency theory was different. A bound party cannot avoid preclusion by controlling a second suit in someone else's name. Yet a suggestion of tactical maneuvering is insufficient. The inquiry should draw on agency principles, especially whether the putative agent's conduct of the later case is subject to the bound party's control. The Court left detailed development of that issue to the lower courts rather than treating the requesters' friendship as proof of agency. Id. at 905–906, Part IV.A. 

Finally, preclusion is an affirmative defense. The fact that Taylor might possess better information about his dealings with Herrick did not reverse the ordinary burden of proof. Targeted interrogatories and depositions could address that information imbalance. The FAA and Fairchild therefore had to establish the basis for binding Taylor; Taylor was not required to disprove agency as a prerequisite to maintaining suit. Id. at 906–907, Part IV.B. 

## Separate Opinions

There were no separate opinions. Justice Ginsburg wrote for a unanimous Court. The unanimity extended to rejection of the virtual-representation doctrine, the identification of applicable nonparty principles, and the remand for the remaining agency question. It did not transform that unresolved question into a unanimous finding that Taylor acted as Herrick's agent. Id. at 884, 903–907. 

## Notes

The Court cautioned against using the word privity as a substitute for analysis. It can label the conclusion that a recognized relationship supports preclusion, but does not explain why friendship, common interests, or shared counsel should bind an absent person. The useful inquiry identifies the particular exception and the facts satisfying its safeguards. Id. at 894 n.8, 898–901. 

Taylor also separates two kinds of control. Control over Herrick's first action could have supplied one established exception, but the record showed none. Control by Herrick over Taylor's second action could establish agency or proxy litigation and remained open. Confusing those directions of control would either invent participation in the first case or prejudge the remanded issue. Id. at 895, 904–906. 

The casebook excerpt emphasizes the six categories, adequate representation, FOIA's individual remedial structure, and agency. The full opinion additionally explains the differing grounds and preservation limits in Herrick's case. Neither the prior loss nor the Supreme Court's rejection of preclusion resolves the underlying trade-secret dispute for Taylor. Id. at 885–890, 903–907.
