# Galligan v. Arovitch
421 Pa. 301, 219 A.2d 463 (Pa. 1966) | Contracts

## Court, judgment, and voting positions
The Pennsylvania Supreme Court decided the appeal on April 19, 1966. Justice Cohen wrote the opinion reversing a judgment on the pleadings entered for the defendant landlords. The judgment was reversed with a procedendo, returning the action for further proceedings rather than awarding the tenant damages or finding negligence established. Justices Eagen and Roberts concurred in the result; Roberts supplied a stated public-policy ground, while Eagen supplied none. Chief Justice Bell and Justice Jones dissented without a published explanation. Of the seven listed justices, five supported reversal and two dissented, but the different positions do not turn Cohen's expressly personal policy observations into a holding of the court. (421 Pa. at 302, 304–305.)

## Facts and procedural setting
On August 4, 1961, the plaintiff, a tenant in the defendants' apartment building, tripped on the lawn in front of the building and suffered injuries. She sued in trespass, alleging that the landlords' negligence caused her injury. The defendants pleaded new matter asserting that an exculpatory clause in her lease relieved them of liability. The trial court accepted that defense on the pleadings. The Supreme Court was therefore assessing whether the pleaded lease foreclosed her action, not reviewing a trial determination that the landlords had negligently maintained the lawn. (Id. at 302–303.)

The clause required the tenant to relieve the lessor of liability for injuries associated with the use, misuse, or abuse of elevators, hatches, openings, stairways, fire escapes, hallways, and the sidewalks surrounding the buildings. It also included language concerning defective construction, failures of utilities and equipment, weather, and other causes on the premises, and expressly referred to negligence by the lessor or its agents. The breadth of its language made the defense substantial, but the court treated the seven listed locations as important restrictions on its application to this fall. The plaintiff argued both that the lawn was not within the specified locations and that the clause was invalid as contrary to public policy. (Id. at 302–303.)

## Controlling rule and application
The court held that an instrument diminishing legal rights that would otherwise exist must be construed strictly against the party invoking it and must state the parties' intention with particularity. It also applied the rule construing a written instrument strictly against its maker. Either rule, the opinion said, was sufficient to require reversal here. Without the exculpatory clause, the plaintiff could not properly suffer an adverse judgment on the pleadings. Consequently, its claimed surrender of her right to recover for negligent injury had to fit this occurrence with the required precision. (Id. at 303–304.)

The court identified the seven locations in the clause and found that none included a lawn. A lawn and a sidewalk were different places; neither encompassed the other, and the remaining listed locations did not cover the lawn either. The court distinguished Bryans v. Gallagher, in which an injury on a driveway fell within a similarly worded provision because that driveway was the sole means of ingress and egress and had been agreed to be the sidewalk contemplated by the clause. There was no comparable agreement or identification in Galligan. The clause therefore did not support judgment against this plaintiff at the pleadings stage. (Id. at 303–304.)

That reasoning concerns the reach of the contractual defense. It does not establish that all negligence waivers are invalid, that the plaintiff had proved the underlying tort, or that she would necessarily recover after further proceedings. Nor does the opinion separately analyze each of the clause's general references to injuries or causes on the premises. Its actual resolution relies on strict construction and the absence of the lawn from the specified locations. (Id. at 303–305.)

## Adhesion, public policy, and separate positions
After explaining the sufficient grounds for reversal, Cohen expressly labeled his additional policy discussion unnecessary to the decision and his personal observations. He described standardized apartment leases as containing a provision landlords would not delete, leaving a prospective tenant unable to obtain a lease without it. In that setting, he reasoned, the tenant had no bargaining power and could either accept the landlord's document or reject the transaction. Applying doctrines developed for negotiated transactions could obscure that difference. (Id. at 304.)

Cohen did not dispute the existing rule enforcing negligence covenants made by private individuals in their personal affairs. His objection was to extending a rule premised on free bargaining to an apartment tenant who could not bargain away the exculpatory term. Roberts went further in his stated concurrence in the result: he considered the clause a contract of adhesion lacking a meeting of the minds, contrary to public policy, and without legal effect. The report gives no separate written reasoning for Eagen's concurrence or Bell and Jones's dissents. Their positions should be reported as recorded, rather than supplied with inferred arguments. (Id. at 304–305.)

## Assigned account and source scope
The assigned note at printed page 660 accurately uses Galligan to illustrate strict construction of an exculpatory provision: the lawn was absent from the lease's listed areas, so judgment on the pleadings was reversed. The complete report adds the clause's wider language, the distinction from Bryans, and the important separation between the controlling construction analysis, Cohen's personal observations, and Roberts's concurrence. The case is described at its 1966 stage; the surrounding textbook discussion of later statutes is editorial material, not a ruling in Galligan.
