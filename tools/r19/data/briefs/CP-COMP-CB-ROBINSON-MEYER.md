# Robinson v. Meyer

**135 Conn. 691, 68 A.2d 142 (1949) — August 2, 1949 — Civil Procedure**

Justice Jennings wrote for the Connecticut Supreme Court of Errors. Chief Justice Maltbie and Justices Brown, Ells, and Dickenson concurred. The five-judge court found no error in the judgment rejecting the plaintiffs’ quiet-title claim. Its pleading ruling concerned facts supporting statutory ouster, not simply completion of a fifteen-year adverse-possession period.

## Facts and procedural history

The parties disputed a triangular tract adjoining Bolton Road in Vernon, Connecticut. It lay between their properties, with its northern point at the road. The plaintiffs relied on deeds describing their lot’s western boundary as perpendicular to Bolton Road. The original deed carved the lot from a larger property in 1932, and subsequent deeds repeated that description. Under the plaintiffs’ claimed line, half of the defendants’ house, together with their garage and driveway, occupied land claimed by the plaintiffs. Both houses had been built in 1932; a predecessor of the defendants built the garage and driveway on the disputed area in 1944. (692–693.)

Defendants relied on estoppel, occupation, and monuments. Their occupation and that of their predecessors was open, visible, and notorious and would establish adverse possession if continued uninterruptedly for fifteen years. The court did not simply announce that the entire required period had already elapsed. A different statutory consequence was decisive: in the plaintiffs’ chain of title, Howard Hastings conveyed the disputed land to Ruth Evelyn Robinson on February 10, 1934, after a predecessor of the defendants had ousted him from its possession. Under the governing statute, that ouster made Hastings’s deed void as to the tract. (693.)

The plaintiffs filed their quiet-title action in February 1947. They argued on appeal that they first learned of the statutory theory upon receiving the trial court’s memorandum of decision. If notified earlier, they said, they would have presented evidence that Hastings had not actually been ousted when he conveyed. The trial court also found that the owners of both properties had accepted the defendants’ asserted boundary since 1933. The appellate court found the result plainly just, but still examined whether the plaintiffs’ procedural objection required a new trial. (692–693.)

## Issue and rule

Could the trial court apply the deed-invalidating statute when the defendants had pleaded the relevant possession facts but had not expressly pleaded that legal consequence or called the statute to the court’s attention at trial?

The statute was General Statutes, Revision of 1930, § 5020, later numbered § 7105 in the 1949 revision. The quality of ouster making a grantor’s deed void was the same quality required for adverse possession. When all necessary facts were pleaded and contested, expressly stating the resulting legal conclusion was proper and desirable but not mandatory. (693–694.)

## Reasoning

The defendants’ amended answer alleged facts adequate to establish adverse possession if those conditions existed for the requisite time. Although the plaintiffs filed no formal reply, they denied the allegation in open court. Considerable evidence on use and occupation was introduced, and the Supreme Court’s examination of the record confirmed that the facts underlying the defense were actually litigated. (693–694.)

Those pleaded facts also established the ouster that invalidated Hastings’s 1934 deed. The trial court therefore was not deciding on an unpleaded factual basis. It supplied the legal effect of facts already put in issue. Naming the statute might have clarified the defense, but omission did not prevent the court from applying it. The plaintiffs had joined issue on the underlying question, so applying the statute caused no procedural injustice warranting a new trial. The opinion cited other Connecticut cases in which courts applied statutory consequences under comparable circumstances. (694.)

This reasoning preserves two distinctions. First, the occupation’s potential to ripen into adverse-possession title over fifteen years explained the character of possession, while statutory ouster invalidated an intervening deed without requiring a finding here that the entire fifteen-year period had run. Second, the court’s view that the outcome was equitable did not replace its analysis of pleading and actual litigation; the plaintiffs’ technical claim was considered and rejected on that basis. (693–694.)

## Judgment and assigned-source comparison

The exact reported disposition was no error; all other participating judges concurred. No new trial or remand was ordered. (694.)
