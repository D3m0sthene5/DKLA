# Greene v. Lindsey

**456 U.S. 444 (1982) · Supreme Court of the United States · May 17, 1982 · Civil Procedure**

Justice Brennan wrote for six Justices, joined by White, Marshall, Blackmun, Powell, and Stevens. Justice O’Connor dissented with Chief Justice Burger and Justice Rehnquist. The Court affirmed the Sixth Circuit’s reversal of summary judgment for the officials. The assigned account appears in *CivPro.pdf*, physical pages 148–149, printed page 247. The decision concerns the constitutional adequacy of the particular notice procedure and circumstances presented.

## Facts and procedural history

Linnie Lindsey, Barbara Hodgens, and Pamela Ray lived in a Louisville public-housing project. In 1975, the Housing Authority brought detainer actions seeking possession of their apartments. Kentucky allowed an officer to leave and explain the notice to a family member over 16 if the tenant could not be found, and to post it conspicuously on the premises if no such person was there. In each tenant’s case, the officer posted the notice on the apartment door. The statute and usual practice did not require a second visit before resorting to posting. (446 and n.1, 454.)

The tenants said they never saw the notices. They learned of the eviction proceedings only when writs of possession were served, after default judgments and expiration of the appeal period. They then brought a federal class action under 42 U.S.C. §1983 against the Housing Authority, housing officials, the Jefferson County sheriff, and deputies, seeking declaratory and injunctive relief. This was a constitutional challenge to the notice practice, rather than a Supreme Court adjudication of the underlying entitlement to rent or possession. (447.)

On cross-motions, the federal district court granted summary judgment for the officials. It relied on the Sixth Circuit’s 1909 *Weber* decision approving a predecessor posting procedure, despite acknowledging undisputed testimony that others often removed posted notices. It considered the initial unsuccessful attempt to locate the tenant sufficient. The Sixth Circuit reversed and remanded, overruled *Weber*, and explained that later due-process decisions demanded a practical assessment of whether notice would reach the tenant. It also considered mailed notice an inexpensive addition that would not seriously obstruct the summary eviction process. The Supreme Court affirmed that appellate judgment. (447–449 and n.2, 456.)

## Issue and holding

Was posting alone, after one unsuccessful effort at personal or family service, reasonably calculated to notify these tenants of eviction proceedings when process servers knew posted notices were frequently removed?

The Court held that it was not. The state had deprived the tenants of property without constitutionally adequate notice. Its holding concerned the established posting practice and conditions in the record; it did not announce that all posting in all eviction proceedings was invalid. Mailing was a feasible way to improve reliability, but the Court expressly declined to prescribe the exact procedure Kentucky must adopt. (453–456 and n.9.)

## Majority’s reasoning

An opportunity to defend has little value if a person does not know that proceedings are pending. *Mullane* therefore required notice reasonably calculated, in the circumstances, to apprise interested parties and permit objections. Personal service was ideal, but due process did not universally insist upon it. The issue was whether the available substitute met the constitutional minimum. (449–450.)

The officials argued that eviction was an in-rem proceeding and therefore could be commenced by notice posted on the property itself. The tenants responded that modern detainer proceedings could extend beyond possession; the officials said these particular actions sought possession alone and conceded that a rent claim would require personal service under Kentucky law. Brennan refused to decide adequacy by assigning an in-rem or in-personam label. The nature of a proceeding could illuminate the consequences at stake, but notice ultimately had to inform people whose interests were threatened. Losing continued residence in one’s home was a significant deprivation even when possession was the only remedy requested. (450–451 and nn.3–5.)

The majority did not deny posting’s ordinary utility. A person interested in continuing to occupy a home could usually be expected to visit it, and a visible notice might often be especially effective. But the record showed that notices at these housing projects were not infrequently removed by children or other tenants. The process-server testimony recounted both reported problems and observed removals. That known unreliability defeated the premise that posting alone would adequately communicate the pending cases. (451–454 and nn.6–7.)

Nor was posting a true last resort simply because it followed an initial unsuccessful visit. A tenant’s absence at one time did not show abandonment of the apartment. Another attempt at a different time was not required by the practice, and inexpensive mail service was available at the very address implicated in the suit. The state could not continue exclusively using a materially unreliable method when feasible measures could enhance it. (454–456 and nn.8–10.)

Footnote 9 is an important limit. The Court said that its task was to identify the existing procedure as inadequate, not prescribe Kentucky’s new form of service. It nevertheless considered posting **plus** mailed notice constitutionally preferable to posting alone, even conceding that mail was imperfect. Its discussion did not demand the theoretically most effective possible substitute or proof of actual receipt in every case. (455–456 and nn.9–10.)

## O’Connor’s dissent

O’Connor emphasized the summary proceeding’s purpose: promptly deciding whether a landlord could recover possession while limiting undeserved loss to landlords and unjustified harassment of tenants. The statutory sequence first sought the tenant, then an appropriate family member, and only afterward permitted conspicuous posting. She regarded that arrangement as supported by both longstanding practice and similar laws in numerous states. (456–458 and dissent n.1.)

She disputed the strength of the removal evidence. Some servers described only occasional observations or suspicions; others said they had not seen removal, received no complaints, or avoided problems by placing notices high on the door. The dissent considered those differences too weak a basis for displacing legislative judgment. It also objected that the record did not compare posting against mail’s risks of loss, delay, misdelivery, or theft. A posted notice at least reached the tenant’s door, whereas unattended mailboxes could also be plundered. (458–460.)

Finally, she thought the majority’s professed refusal to prescribe a replacement was difficult to reconcile with its recommendation of mail. In her view, the Court had turned an unsupported preference about service methods into a constitutional requirement. The majority responded that the lower courts’ findings established posting’s shortcomings and that adding mail enhanced notice rather than guaranteed perfection. Their disagreement therefore involved the evidence, the practical comparison, and the deference owed to the legislature—not whether actual notice would be desirable. (455 n.9, 459 n.2–460.)

## Judgment and assigned-source distinction

The Court **affirmed** the Sixth Circuit, leaving its reversal and remand in place. It did not itself decide all issues in the federal class action or determine the merits of each eviction. (448–449, 456.)

The textbook says that due process required service by mail in these circumstances. The complete opinion’s narrower statement is that posting under this procedure was inadequate; footnote 9 explicitly leaves Kentucky to select its replacement while identifying supplementary mail as constitutionally preferable. The brief preserves that reservation. The textbook’s subsequent question about the dignity of keeping household financial trouble private is an editorial inquiry; Brennan’s identified deprivation was the property interest and continued residence, not a separately adjudicated dignity claim. (451, 455 n.9; assigned physical 149.)
