# Bowles v. Russell

**551 U.S. 205 (2007) — Supreme Court of the United States, June 14, 2007. Civil Procedure.** Justice Thomas wrote for Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Scalia, Kennedy, and Alito. Justice Souter dissented with Justices Stevens, Ginsburg, and Breyer. By 5–4, the Court held that filing outside the statutory reopening period deprived the court of appeals of jurisdiction, despite compliance with the district judge’s erroneously later deadline.

## Facts and procedural history

An Ohio jury convicted Keith Bowles of murder in 1999 for involvement in Ollie Gipson’s beating death and sentenced him to fifteen years to life. After an unsuccessful direct challenge, he sought federal habeas relief. The federal district court denied relief, and Bowles did not file a notice of appeal within the ordinary thirty-day period. He moved to reopen the appeal period on December 12, 2003, under Appellate Rule 4(a)(6). The case concerns this civil habeas appeal’s timing, rather than the substantive validity of the criminal conviction. 551 U.S. at 207.

On February 10, 2004, the district court granted reopening but specified February 27 as the filing deadline. That gave Bowles seventeen days, although Rule 4(a)(6) and 28 U.S.C. § 2107(c) allowed fourteen. He filed on February 26: before the deadline stated in the court’s order, but after the statutory period. Warden Russell objected that the notice was untimely. The Sixth Circuit concluded that the period was mandatory and jurisdictional and that it lacked jurisdiction. The Supreme Court granted review. Id. at 207–208.

## Issue and holding

Can reliance on a district court order excuse an appeal filed outside the reopening period Congress authorized? The majority answered no. The statutory limit on reopening the civil notice-of-appeal period was jurisdictional. A judge’s mistaken order could not supply authority Congress withheld, and the Court could not create an equitable exception to that limit. Id. at 209–215.

The holding does not mean that every time limit appearing in a procedural rule is jurisdictional. The statutory source of this particular limit was central to the majority’s reasoning. The opinion expressly distinguished court-promulgated timing rules from congressional restrictions on adjudicatory authority. Id. at 210–213.

## Statutory framework and majority’s reasoning

Section 2107(a) established the ordinary thirty-day civil appeal period. Section 2107(c) authorized limited reopening for fourteen additional days when specified conditions were satisfied. Rule 4 implemented that statute. The opinion reproduced the historical reopening conditions, including a motion deadline tied to entry or receipt of notice, failure to receive the required notice within twenty-one days, and absence of prejudice. The decisive defect was not an adverse finding on one of those conditions: the reopening order itself purported to allow more than fourteen days. Id. at 208–209 & n. 1.

The majority relied on longstanding treatment of statutory civil appellate deadlines as mandatory and jurisdictional. It distinguished recent decisions warning against imprecise use of “jurisdictional.” In Kontrick, for example, the deadline was established by a bankruptcy rule rather than a statute; only Congress could define lower federal courts’ jurisdiction. Other recent decisions involved limitations different from a statutory time to appeal. Thomas understood those cases as consistent with, rather than repudiating, jurisdictional treatment of § 2107. Id. at 209–211 & nn. 2–3.

The opinion also compared the Court’s own certiorari deadlines. A statute-based deadline for civil petitions had received jurisdictional treatment, while a rule-based criminal deadline could be relaxed. This comparison supported the distinction between legislative and judicial sources of a time restriction; it did not turn Bowles’s habeas proceeding into a direct criminal appeal. Id. at 211–212 & n. 4.

Within constitutional bounds, Congress determines whether federal courts can hear a class of cases and under what temporal conditions they can do so. The majority therefore regarded the statutory reopening limit as more than a claim-processing rule. Failure to comply deprived the Sixth Circuit of authority, and forfeiture or waiver could not cure it. The district judge’s additional three days were ineffective to enlarge that authority. Id. at 212–213.

## Reliance and the rejected equitable exception

Bowles invoked the unique-circumstances doctrine, rooted in Harris Truck Lines and applied in Thompson, which had protected litigants relying on a district court’s mistaken procedural assurance. The majority described those decisions but rejected the doctrine for jurisdictional requirements. Because the Court lacked power to create equitable exceptions to such requirements, it overruled those cases **to the extent** they purported to authorize an exception to a jurisdictional rule. The qualified extent of overruling matters; the opinion did not declare every proposition in either case invalid. Id. at 213–214.

The majority recognized that Congress could authorize rules excusing statutory compliance, but considered that authority absent here. It preferred legislatively authorized relief, with defined scope, to judge-made exceptions that would also create litigation over their boundaries. That was a statement about possible future congressional action, not an extension actually granted to Bowles. Id. at 214–215.

## Souter’s dissent

Souter disputed both the jurisdictional classification and the refusal to protect reasonable reliance. He viewed the majority’s use of older terminology as inconsistent with recent unanimous efforts to distinguish mandatory claim-processing rules from genuine limits on adjudicatory authority. In his analysis, a statutory deadline did not become jurisdictional simply because it appeared in a statute; Congress had not given this deadline the necessary jurisdictional designation. Treating it as jurisdictional eliminated waiver and excuse and required appellate courts to raise defects themselves. Id. at 215–219 & nn. 1–4.

He also argued that Harris Truck Lines and Thompson supported equitable relief. Thompson had relied on a judge’s mistaken assurance even after initially filing his own motions late. Bowles’s reopening error, by contrast, arose from the district court’s order rather than a comparable initiating mistake by Bowles. Souter found it especially unreasonable to withdraw protection in that situation. Id. at 219–221 & nn. 5–8.

The dissent explained why counsel could not necessarily detect the error merely by counting fourteen days from the order’s signature date: the governing period ran from entry, which might occur later. Counsel received the order by mail, and according to counsel, electronic docket access was unavailable. Souter did not insist that counsel had actually reasoned through delayed entry; he acknowledged that counsel probably simply trusted the stated date, which he considered reasonable on these facts. Opposing counsel had not flagged the discrepancy. Footnote 9 also explained why counsel might rationally use available time, since filing the notice triggered later record and briefing schedules. Id. at 221–223 & n. 9.

Souter would have vacated and remanded for consideration of the merits. His position was not the Court’s holding. Id. at 223.

## Judgment and assigned scope

The Supreme Court **affirmed the Sixth Circuit’s judgment that it lacked jurisdiction**. It did not review the habeas merits or validate the conviction independently. Id. at 215.
