# Blonder-Tongue Laboratories, Inc. v. University of Illinois Foundation

**402 U.S. 313 (1971) — Supreme Court of the United States, May 3, 1971.** Justice White wrote for a unanimous Court. Civil Procedure: defensive nonmutual issue preclusion; patent-invalidity judgments; a full and fair opportunity to litigate. Assigned source: *CivPro.pdf*, physical pages 670–672, the developed captioned account beginning at printed 1330 and its five editorial notes ending at printed 1334.

## Facts and the two tracks of litigation

The University of Illinois Foundation owned, by assignment, Dwight Isbell’s patent for frequency-independent unidirectional antennas. Issued in October 1965, the patent covered antennas used to transmit and receive radio-frequency signals, including television broadcasts. Its commercially important application was high-quality color-television reception. The Foundation brought several infringement suits. The controversy here arose because two federal courts reached opposite conclusions about the same patent’s validity. (314–317, nn.1–4.)

In an Iowa action against antenna manufacturer Winegard, the district court held the Isbell patent invalid for obviousness. Applying the framework of *Graham v. John Deere*, it concluded that a person ordinarily skilled in antenna design would have combined three features already suggested by the prior art. The Eighth Circuit affirmed, and the Supreme Court denied certiorari. The full opinion reproduces the technical basis for that determination in note 3; it does not treat invalidity as an unexplained procedural default. (314–315, n.3.)

Meanwhile, in March 1966, before the Iowa court had ruled, the Foundation sued a Chicago customer of Blonder-Tongue Laboratories in federal court in Illinois. This action alleged infringement of both the Isbell patent and a separate Mayes antenna patent. Blonder-Tongue voluntarily submitted to the court’s jurisdiction to defend its customer. It challenged validity and infringement, asserted unfair-competition and antitrust counterclaims against the Foundation and its licensee JFD Electronics, and alleged infringement of Blonder-Tongue’s own antenna patent. (315–316.)

The Illinois district court found the Foundation’s two patents valid and infringed, dismissed the unfair-competition and antitrust counterclaims, and held claim 5 of Blonder-Tongue’s patent obvious and invalid. It knew about the adverse Iowa judgment but relied on *Triplett v. Lowell*: a patent holder could litigate validity against a different defendant despite an earlier invalidity judgment. The Seventh Circuit affirmed the Isbell rulings, dismissal of the counterclaims, and invalidation of Blonder-Tongue’s claim 5. It **reversed** the Mayes-patent validity ruling and the accompanying infringement injunction and damages. Thus, the Supreme Court did not receive an appellate judgment affirming every aspect of the district court’s decision. (316–317.)

## Question presented and the parties’ positions

Blonder-Tongue sought Supreme Court review principally because the Seventh and Eighth Circuits disagreed about the Isbell patent. After granting review, the Court asked whether *Triplett* should remain controlling and, if not, whether the Winegard invalidity judgment bound the Foundation in this litigation. The added questions directed attention to preclusion rather than a third fresh determination of patent validity. (317.)

The Foundation defended *Triplett*. Blonder-Tongue’s brief initially did too, describing the opportunity for patentees to litigate against another defendant as a long-established safeguard. The United States, appearing as amicus, urged that mutuality should be reconsidered and that patent estoppel be assessed individually, with safeguards against unfair or anomalous results. At argument, Blonder-Tongue accepted the possibility of a limited modification where the earlier trial genuinely supplied an adequate opportunity to litigate. The Court considered reconsideration properly before it. Note 6 explains both the Court’s authority to consider important questions outside the original petition and the significance of the parties’ having briefed and argued this added question. (319–321, n.6.)

The issue was whether an infringement defendant who was not a party to the first action could invoke the patentee’s earlier loss on validity. That is **defensive nonmutual issue preclusion**: a new defendant uses an earlier judgment to prevent a plaintiff from retrying an issue it already lost. It is not claim preclusion dismissing every dispute between these parties, and it is not offensive use by a new plaintiff. (328–330.)

## Holding and the reason mutuality was relaxed

The Court overruled *Triplett* **to the extent that it foreclosed an estoppel defense by someone charged with infringement of a patent previously declared invalid**. It did not pronounce every earlier invalidity judgment automatically binding. The patent holder must be allowed to show that it lacked a full and fair opportunity to litigate the relevant issue in the first action. (332–334, 349–350.)

Mutuality required that both parties to the second suit be bound by the earlier judgment before either could use it. The Court traced the erosion of that requirement, including *Bernhard*, earlier exceptions, and commentary. But its account of this development was not wholesale approval of every suggested expansion of preclusion. The central concern was whether someone who had already received a fair opportunity to litigate should obtain repeated chances merely because more unrelated defendants were available. Requiring each defendant to reproduce a full merits defense consumed resources and let the previously unsuccessful plaintiff keep seeking a different result. Efficiency mattered, but fairness and the allocation of litigation burdens mattered too. (320–330.)

There was an important asymmetry. A person who never had an opportunity to present evidence and arguments could not be bound simply because someone else lost the same issue. The Court expressly distinguished that due-process problem and offensive nonmutual use, neither of which this case presented. Its ruling concerned a patent holder that had itself litigated invalidity and then sued another alleged infringer. (329–330.)

## The full-and-fair-opportunity safeguard

Identifying the same issue in a final adverse judgment is not sufficient by itself. The patent holder may demonstrate that its earlier opportunity was deficient procedurally, substantively, or evidentially. Relevant circumstances include its choice of forum and incentive to litigate. For an obviousness determination, the later court may examine whether the first court purported to apply the appropriate *Graham* standards, whether the prior judicial opinions reveal a rare wholesale failure to understand the technical subject matter, and whether the patent holder lost crucial evidence or witnesses through no fault of its own. There is no mechanical checklist that resolves every application; the trial court must exercise judgment informed by fairness and equity. (332–334.)

Patent cases can be technically difficult, but the Court rejected complexity as a reason to preserve an unrestricted entitlement to relitigate. An erroneous result was possible in any system, and careful examination of the first proceeding offered protection without always repeating the merits trial. Note 26 also considers the possibility of jury compromises while observing that most patent cases were tried to judges. The safeguard is a meaningful examination of the earlier opportunity, not a presumption that every first loss was unreliable. (330–334, n.26.)

## Patent policy and the economic argument

The Court’s extensive patent-policy discussion explains why docket reduction alone did not drive the ruling. Patent litigation was expensive and often unusually lengthy; notes 28–33 include testimony, litigation statistics, and a comparative trial-duration table. A defendant facing the statutory presumption of validity might prefer a royalty agreement to the cost of challenging a patent already held invalid. Repeated suits could therefore allow an invalid patent to generate payments and discourage entry even when a well-supported invalidity judgment already existed. (334–342.)

The public interest in limiting the patent monopoly to a genuinely patentable invention reinforced the change. Decisions restricting extensions of patent monopolies and facilitating validity challenges, including *Lear*, supplied the relevant background. Eliminating licensee estoppel under *Lear* did not solve everything: firms unable to finance litigation might continue paying, consumers could bear passed-on royalties, and royalty-free competitors could retain pricing advantages without producing fully competitive prices. Statutory fee and cost remedies worked only after litigation, leaving the initial expense barrier intact. A properly available estoppel defense could instead be pleaded and addressed before a complete trial. (343–348, nn.38–44.)

The Court also distinguished proposed legislation that would have made invalidity broadly binding without its full-and-fair-opportunity qualification. The lengthy proposed statutory provisions in notes 34 and 35 are legislative proposals, not enacted rules applied in this case; note 37 expressly identifies that difference. Even if only a small fraction of cases involved repeated litigation, the economic consequences of adhering to *Triplett* remained significant. Judicial time saved was an additional benefit rather than the decisive measure of the doctrine’s legitimacy. (339–342, nn.34–37; 348–349.)

## Exact disposition and what remained undecided

The Court **vacated the Seventh Circuit’s judgment and remanded the cause to the district court**. Because *Triplett* had prevented Blonder-Tongue from pleading estoppel, the Foundation had not yet had an opportunity to contest the fairness of imposing it. Blonder-Tongue could amend its pleadings; the respondents could amend theirs and supplement the evidence to show why estoppel should not apply; Blonder-Tongue could also supplement the record if necessary. The Court expressed no view on the other issues. It did not itself apply estoppel conclusively or independently hold the Isbell patent invalid in this litigation. (350.)

## Assigned account and limits

The assigned account emphasizes fairness, the trial court’s contextual assessment, and possible litigation savings. Its first quotation is labeled 328–333, although that quoted passage occupies 328–330 in the actual report; the separate fairness passage is at 332–334 and the later efficiency passage at 348–349. All five attached editorial notes have been read. They ask whether the change merely relocates litigation costs to a fairness inquiry, whether expansion beyond patents saves resources, how joinder affects decision quality, how *Bernhard* differs, and whether initiating the first litigation matters. The final note quotes an academic objection based on unequal exposure to litigation risk. These questions and criticisms are editorial discussion, not additional holdings of the Court.
