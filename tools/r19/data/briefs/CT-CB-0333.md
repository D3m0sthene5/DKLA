# Dixon v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A.
798 F. Supp. 2d 336 (D. Mass. 2011) | Contracts

## The promise and the alleged change in position
Frank and Deana Dixon owned and lived in a mortgaged home in Scituate, Massachusetts. According to their complaint, on or about June 8, 2009, they and the bank orally agreed to take the steps necessary for a mortgage modification. The bank told them to stop making payments and submit financial information; they promptly supplied the information and stopped paying. They understood that the missed installments would be added to the modified loan. They alleged that the bank then failed or effectively refused to follow through and instead proceeded toward foreclosure. On December 8, 2010, they received notice of a Land Court foreclosure proceeding with a January 10, 2011 return date. They alleged on information and belief that their home's value exceeded the balance and arrears. These are allegations the court assumed true for the dismissal motion, rather than findings that the bank made or broke the promise. 798 F. Supp. 2d at 339.

The wording of the commitment matters. Although the complaint initially described an agreement to enter into a modification, the Dixons clarified in opposing dismissal that the enforceable promise was to consider them for a modification and engage in negotiations after they took the requested steps. They did not ask the court to invent and impose all the terms of a completed new loan. The alleged harm arose because complying with the bank's instruction put them in default and exposed their home to foreclosure before the promised consideration occurred. The bank argued that an indefinite agreement to agree creates no legal obligation, and that federal banking law preempted the state claim even if it were otherwise plausible.

## Procedural posture and the question actually decided
The Dixons sued in Massachusetts Superior Court on January 6, 2011, seeking an injunction against foreclosure, specific performance, and damages. The state court issued a preliminary injunction on February 14. The bank removed the case to federal court on March 4 and moved to dismiss on April 11. At a May 9 hearing, Judge William G. Young denied the Dixons' remand motion and dismissed their ordinary contract claim as insufficiently pleaded. He reserved the sufficiency of their reliance theory and the bank's Home Owners' Loan Act (HOLA) preemption defense for supplemental briefing. The July 22 opinion decides those remaining issues; it does not reinstate the previously dismissed bargain-based contract claim. Id. at 338–339.

At the pleading stage, the court applied the requirement of a plausible claim under the federal dismissal standard. Whether the alleged promise, reasonable reliance, and resulting injury could actually be proved remained for trial. The central state-law issue was whether reliance on a promise to consider or negotiate a modification could be actionable even though the parties had not specified a new loan's essential terms. The separate federal question was whether enforcing that voluntarily made promise imposed a state lending regulation prohibited by HOLA.

## Massachusetts reliance law and the agreement-to-agree objection
Massachusetts treated a promise enforceable because of reliance as contractual in character. In Loranger Construction and Rhode Island Hospital Trust v. Varadian, its Supreme Judicial Court required the necessary elements of a contract other than consideration. The reliance-based plaintiff therefore needed a genuine commitment, understood objectively as an intention to act or refrain from acting in a specified way, rather than a prediction or invitation to discuss. The ordinary requirement of definiteness also presented a serious obstacle: courts generally refuse to enforce an agreement whose essential terms remain reserved for future agreement. Without a fallback standard, they cannot know what deal would have emerged, and parties must retain the ability to decline a proposed transaction. Id. at 340–344.

Young did not dismiss this objection as irrelevant. He acknowledged that reading the complaint as a promise to supply a completed loan modification would ordinarily end the inquiry. But the clarified reliance claim presented a narrower wrong. The bank allegedly induced the Dixons to stop paying and then took advantage of the resulting default without honestly considering the promised modification. Enforcing a duty to deal honestly after inducing that detrimental change would not require compelling either party to accept unspecified loan terms.

The opinion drew support from Massachusetts cases involving assurances during negotiations or assurances that induced a party to surrender an existing protection. In Greenstein, a landlord submitted a lease to a prospective tenant, then strung him along for more than four months before repudiating it; the misleading conduct made the tenant reasonably believe that only a bureaucratic formality remained. In McLearn, defendants allegedly induced abandonment of a timely action and then invoked the limitations period; the law could protect the reliance without requiring a separately bargained, explicit promise not to plead the statute. Cohoon involved assurances in the setting of a mortgage and a threatened foreclosure. Young used these cases to identify a recurring concern: a party should not induce a predictable loss of position and then exploit that loss by disavowing its undertaking. Id. at 344–347.

The court did not recognize an unrestricted duty to negotiate in good faith whenever negotiations begin. Nor did it require proof that the bank had a fraudulent intention at the moment it spoke. The pleaded promise, the requested steps, and the alleged detrimental reliance made this case different from mere unsuccessful discussions. The opinion also acknowledged uncertainty in Massachusetts precedent. Much of the supporting authority came from intermediate or trial courts, and Young would entertain a party's request for certification to the Supreme Judicial Court, although he did not initiate certification himself. The result is a federal district court's application and prediction of state law, not a new holding issued by Massachusetts's highest court. Id. at 347–348, 352–353 & n.5.

## Why the remedy is tied to reliance
The court treated remedy as part of the answer to indefiniteness. Requiring performance of a hypothetical modified loan could force a bargain the parties never made. Protecting the position the borrowers lost because they followed the bank's instructions could instead address the injury without assigning the benefits of an unformed agreement. The Dixons would need to prove their claim by a preponderance of the evidence. A failure to reach agreement after genuine consideration would not itself establish that a specified new mortgage must be imposed.

Footnote 2 illustrates a possible remedial approach, rather than ordering one: the bank might restore the loan to a nondefault status by amortizing the missed payments while requiring the original monthly payments to resume. If the borrowers could not make those payments, foreclosure could then proceed. The illustration underscores the narrower aim of undoing the detrimental reliance. It is not a judicially ordered modification, a damages calculation, or a promise that the Dixons could keep the house regardless of their ability to pay. Id. at 348–349 & n.2.

## HOLA preemption
The bank also invoked the federal regulatory regime for savings associations. Although the defendant was now Wells Fargo, the alleged conduct involved its predecessor Wachovia Mortgage, a federal savings bank; the court explained the relevance of that institutional history. Under the then-applicable Office of Thrift Supervision regulation, 12 C.F.R. § 560.2, specified categories of state lending requirements, including credit terms and servicing-related regulation, fell within the federal field. Contract and tort laws could survive when they only incidentally affected lending. The analysis therefore examined the claim as applied, rather than assuming that every suit bearing a common-law label escaped preemption. Id. at 353–359 & n.7.

Young concluded that the reliance claim sought to hold the bank to its own voluntary undertaking, not to prescribe new loan terms or require lenders generally to provide modifications. The duty to honor a promise after inducing harmful reliance was a basic rule of commercial conduct that affected lending incidentally. Allowing HOLA to insulate the bank from that obligation would transform a federal regulatory framework into protection against ordinary wrongful conduct. The absence of a private action under HOLA did not itself eliminate an independently available state-law claim. The opinion discussed divided authority and distinguished cases involving other kinds of modification promises or regulatory requirements. It did not hold that all mortgage-related contract or tort claims survive preemption. Id. at 353–360.

## Holding, judgment, and practical significance
Young held that the complaint plausibly stated a promissory-estoppel claim and that this particular claim was not preempted by HOLA. He denied the bank's remaining dismissal request and placed the case on the September trial list, requiring readiness on September 6, 2011. The existing preliminary protection and the need for prompt resolution supplied the practical setting, but this opinion awarded no damages and made no final finding that the bank was liable. Id. at 360–362.

The opinion's concluding discussion of distressed borrowers, banking markets, and the need for prompt trials explains the judge's concern with consequences and delay; it is not an additional element of promissory estoppel. For Contracts, the case demonstrates how identifying the promise precisely and limiting relief to the induced loss can answer an indefiniteness objection. It also shows the limits of that approach: allegations must become proof, the asserted commitment must exceed ordinary negotiation, and the court must avoid converting reliance protection into enforcement of a final agreement never reached. This brief describes the 2011 ruling and its regulatory analysis on their historical terms; it does not certify the present state of banking preemption law.
