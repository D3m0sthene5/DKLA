# Raila v. United States — district dismissal

District of Connecticut, February 26, 2003 | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Identity and source limitation

This brief concerns Judge Covello's district-court dismissal, separately from the Second Circuit's January 14, 2004 decision reported at 355 F.3d 118. The complete original district order and its exact docket citation have not been recovered. The available account is Katzmann's assigned discussion at printed 59–60, PDF 10–11 of the 50–70 selection, checked against the later appellate opinion's description at 119–120, paragraphs 5 and 8–10. Neither account is the district order itself.

## Parties, alleged facts, and posture

Lenore and Whitton Raila sued the United States under the Federal Tort Claims Act on September 11, 2002. They alleged that on November 26, 2001, a Postal Service employee left a package immediately below the front doorstep of their Norwalk, Connecticut home. Lenore allegedly slipped on it while leaving the house and was injured; Whitton asserted loss of consortium. Negligent placement and failure to warn supplied the alleged tort. These are complaint allegations, not findings reached after trial. (355 F.3d at 119, paragraphs 5, 8.)

The Government moved to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction under Rule 12(b)(1), contending that the FTCA's postal matter exception preserved sovereign immunity for the claim. Judge Covello granted the motion on February 26, 2003. The district result was jurisdictional dismissal, not a finding after trial that the postal worker acted carefully or that Lenore suffered no injury. (119–120, paragraphs 5, 7–10; assigned 59–60.)

## Statute and issue

The FTCA permits specified tort claims against the Government, subject to enumerated exceptions. At the time addressed, 28 U.S.C. § 2680(b) preserved immunity for claims arising from loss, miscarriage, or negligent transmission of letters or postal matter. The district question was whether transmitting a package included its negligent placement during delivery, when the resulting injury was to a person rather than the mail. (Assigned 59; appellate 119–120, paragraphs 9–10.)

## District interpretation and result

As the assigned account and the appellate opinion describe it, the district judge used the ordinary dictionary meaning of transmitting something to another person or place. He also relied on other district decisions that treated transmission as extending from a patron's deposit of mail through delivery. The appellate opinion identifies Robinson v. United States, 849 F. Supp. 799, 802 (S.D. Ga. 1994), for that temporal scope, and Bono v. United States, 145 F. Supp. 2d 441, 446 (D.N.J. 2001), for a slip caused by negligent package placement. The district court concluded that the exception covered this type of delivery negligence and that the Government had not waived immunity. It dismissed the complaint. (Assigned 59–60; appellate 120, paragraph 10.)

This states the district rationale recoverable from those accounts. The original's additional analysis, judicial notes, exact ordering language, and any treatment of alternative arguments remain unverified. No separate opinion or vote count is inferred from the single-judge posture.

## Subsequent reversal and textbook purpose

The Second Circuit unanimously reversed the dismissal and remanded. It considered the statutory phrase in context, including its associated words, and held that the exception did not bar the Railas' claims. The assigned passage begins that contrary analysis on printed 60. Those appellate conclusions must not be attributed to Judge Covello. Likewise, reversal did not establish negligence, causation, a particular damages award, or ultimate liability. The appellate judgment is separately briefed. (Assigned 60; appellate 120–123, paragraphs 13–27.)

Katzmann's account contrasts a broad dictionary-based understanding of transmission with the appellate panel's contextual interpretation. Its narrative and printed reference numbers are textbook material, rather than the district court's recovered footnotes. The supplied chapter does not include the corresponding book endnote bodies.
