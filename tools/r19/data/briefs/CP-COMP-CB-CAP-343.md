# Amgen Inc. v. Connecticut Retirement Plans and Trust Funds

**568 U.S. 455 (2013) · Supreme Court of the United States · February 27, 2013 · No. 11–1085**

**Civil Procedure — securities class actions, predominance, and the permissible scope of merits review at certification.** Assigned: CivPro.pdf, physical page 426, printed pages 823–824, the Amgen account in Note 3 before Note 4.

## Facts and litigation path

Connecticut Retirement Plans and Trust Funds purchased Amgen stock and sought to represent other purchasers in a private securities-fraud action under § 10(b) of the Securities Exchange Act and SEC Rule 10b–5. Its complaint alleged that Amgen and its executives misrepresented or omitted information about the safety, efficacy, and marketing of two leading drugs. According to the complaint, these public statements inflated Amgen’s stock price, and disclosures of the truth later caused losses. Those were allegations supporting the litigation, not factual findings that the Supreme Court adjudicated. Amgen stock traded on NASDAQ; Amgen expressly conceded market efficiency in its answer and did not contest that the challenged statements were public. Those concessions matter because the case did not require the Court to decide how a plaintiff must prove an efficient market. (463–465; 470–471 n.6.)

Ordinarily, reliance connects the defendant’s statement to the plaintiff’s purchase. Proving that every investor individually read and acted on a particular statement could make a damages class impracticable. Under Basic Inc. v. Levinson, however, investors may invoke a rebuttable fraud-on-the-market presumption: an efficient market incorporates public, material information into its price, so an investor relying on the integrity of that price may be presumed to rely on such information without personally encountering the statement. The presumption is a method of proving reliance in individual as well as class litigation. It does not eliminate the other elements of a securities-fraud claim. (460–463.)

The district court certified a Rule 23(b)(3) class of purchasers between the first alleged misrepresentation and the last alleged corrective disclosure. Amgen argued that Connecticut Retirement first had to prove materiality and that Amgen should be allowed to rebut materiality with public information already available to investors. The district court rejected those certification arguments. The Ninth Circuit allowed an interlocutory appeal under Rule 23(f) and affirmed, 660 F.3d 1170. The Supreme Court reviewed the timing of materiality proof and the related rebuttal question. Compliance with Rule 23(a) was not disputed. (459, 464–465; 491 n.7 (Thomas, J., dissenting).)

## Questions and holdings

Must securities plaintiffs prove that an alleged public misrepresentation is material before obtaining Rule 23(b)(3) certification through the fraud-on-the-market presumption? Must the certification court consider Amgen’s evidence offered to establish that the challenged statements were immaterial?

**No to both questions.** Materiality remains an essential element of the eventual securities-fraud judgment and an essential predicate of the presumption. But proving it before certification is unnecessary to determine whether common questions predominate. Materiality is an objective question common to the class; failure to prove it ends every class member’s fraud claim rather than leaving individually viable claims that require separate reliance inquiries. Amgen’s particular rebuttal evidence addressed that same materiality question and therefore belonged at summary judgment or trial. The Court affirmed the Ninth Circuit’s judgment. (467–470, 480–482.)

## Why the majority distinguished certification from success on the merits

Justice Ginsburg began with the function of Rule 23(b)(3): deciding whether common questions predominate so that the controversy can fairly and efficiently proceed through a class action. Certification does not require a plaintiff to establish that the common questions will ultimately be answered in the class’s favor. A court must rigorously test the Rule’s requirements and may investigate merits issues that bear on those requirements. It may not use certification as an unrestricted preliminary trial of the underlying claim. The relevant distinction is therefore not simply whether materiality is a merits issue. It is whether proving materiality is necessary to decide the certification issue. (465–467.)

The majority supplied two connected reasons why it is not. First, materiality asks what a reasonable investor would consider important in the total mix of available information. Because that standard is objective, an alleged statement’s materiality can be resolved for the class as a whole. The answer does not turn on each investor’s subjective reaction. Second, if the statement is immaterial, all of the investors lose on an indispensable element of their Rule 10b–5 claims. Individual reliance cannot rescue a claim founded on an immaterial statement. Thus failure of materiality does not transform a coherent class proceeding into many surviving individual proceedings. It supplies a common merits defeat. (467–470, including nn.4–5.)

This differed from other prerequisites of fraud on the market. If a market is inefficient or a statement is not public, the price-based presumption may fail while some investors retain claims based on direct reliance. Individual questions could then become important. Similarly, the timing of trades and disclosures helps establish the proper class and representative; the majority discussed typicality and adequacy concerns rather than treating timing as irrelevant. Amgen’s conceded efficiency and undisputed publicity made its proposed economic criticisms of Basic a poor vehicle for reconsidering that decision. The Court did not overrule Basic. (470–474 nn.6–8.)

Amgen argued that certification puts substantial settlement pressure on defendants, so courts should screen materiality before exposing a company to that pressure. The majority acknowledged the concern but found no basis in Rule 23 for a special advance merits requirement. Other essential elements, such as falsity and loss causation, likewise can be decisive without invariably requiring adjudication at certification. Congress had addressed securities-litigation abuses through the Private Securities Litigation Reform Act’s pleading requirements, discovery stay, and other protections, and through the Securities Litigation Uniform Standards Act. It had not imposed this additional certification hurdle. The Court also considered meritorious private enforcement an important supplement to public enforcement. (474–478.)

Amgen’s judicial-economy argument was also unpersuasive. A materiality minitrial could duplicate later merits proceedings. Moreover, absent investors would not be bound by the denial of certification and could pursue another attempt. Rule 23’s instruction to decide certification at an early practicable time did not demand that additional preliminary trial. (477–478.)

## Basic and the specific rebuttal evidence

The majority rejected Justice Scalia’s contention that Basic itself established a special certification rule requiring proof of all the presumption’s predicates. In Basic, the Court had left the certification order intact while remanding the materiality issue for further merits proceedings. Its recognition that certification could be adjusted later reflected the ordinary ability to alter a certification order as circumstances develop, rather than a special requirement that materiality first be established. The subsequent elimination of conditional certification did not eliminate the continuing authority to alter or amend certification before final judgment. (478–480 nn.9–10; 482 n.11.)

Amgen’s rebuttal evidence illustrated why the same analysis controlled both questions. An executive allegedly downplayed an FDA advisory committee meeting by saying it would not focus on an Amgen drug. Amgen produced a publicly available committee agenda showing that the drug’s safety would be discussed. The asserted defense was that the market already possessed the truth, so the statement did not significantly alter the total mix of information. The Ninth Circuit characterized this as a truth-on-the-market materiality defense, and Amgen did not dispute that characterization. The Supreme Court therefore addressed this materiality rebuttal; it did not decide that every possible rebuttal of fraud on the market must await trial. Even a conclusive showing of immateriality would defeat all the fraud claims together, without undermining common-question predominance. (480–482.)

## Separate writings and votes

**Justice Ginsburg’s opinion commanded six votes:** Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Breyer, Alito, Sotomayor, and Kagan joined her. Justice Alito also wrote separately. He joined on the understanding that the parties had not asked the Court to reconsider Basic and suggested that newer evidence questioning its economic premise might warrant reconsideration in a suitable case. His concurrence did not withdraw his vote from the Court’s holding. (482–483.)

Justice Scalia dissented separately and joined Justice Thomas’s dissent except Part I–B. Scalia accepted that the majority’s Rule 23 analysis could follow logically if Basic governed only substantive liability, but read Basic as independently governing certification too. He relied on Basic’s stated certification question, its materiality language, and the certification order’s susceptibility to later adjustment. He also distinguished the pleading-based approach used in Basic’s district court from later rigorous certification doctrine and stressed settlement pressure. (483–486; sole starred footnote at 485.)

Justice Thomas dissented, joined by Justice Kennedy throughout and Justice Scalia except Part I–B. He viewed proof of every fraud-on-the-market predicate as a condition precedent to making reliance a common question. Without materiality proof at certification, he argued, reliance remained individual; a later merits defeat could not retrospectively excuse an improperly certified class. His Part I–B developed the distinction between proving materiality as a common merits element and establishing the presumption that makes reliance common. His extensive historical discussion traced the presumption through Blackie, an influential law-review account, the Sixth Circuit’s Basic decision, and the parties’ Basic briefs. He would have reversed. Thomas criticized Basic’s foundation but, like Alito, acknowledged that reconsidering Basic itself was not presented. (486–502, all eleven dissenting footnotes, particularly 489 n.4 and 493–496 nn.8–9.)

## Assignment and source scope

The casebook correctly presents a six-to-three decision limiting merits inquiries to questions relevant to Rule 23 and quotes the distinction between selecting an adjudication method and deciding the controversy. The complete opinion supplies the essential explanation: immateriality causes a common merits loss, not surviving individual reliance claims. This brief describes the historical 2013 decision; subsequent securities-class-action developments require their own sources.
