# Greene v. McElroy
360 U.S. 474 (1959) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Decision and precise ground

On June 29, 1959, the Supreme Court reversed the D.C. Circuit and remanded to the District Court. Chief Justice Warren’s opinion held that, without explicit authorization from Congress or the President, Defense Department officials could not deprive William Lewis Greene of his private employment through an industrial-clearance proceeding denying confrontation and cross-examination. The Court did not decide whether that procedure would be constitutional if expressly authorized, whether the President possessed inherent power to establish it, whether legislation was indispensable, or the ultimate limits on either branch’s authority. (474–475, 493, 506–508.)

Eight Justices agreed with reversal; five joined the Court’s opinion. Frankfurter, Harlan, and Whittaker concurred in the judgment only because the challenged procedures had not been shown to have presidential or congressional authorization, expressly intimating no view on validity. Harlan wrote separately, criticizing constitutional discussion beyond that narrow basis. Clark dissented. The result consequently should not be presented as eight votes for the majority’s discussion of confrontation or an unconditional right to receive classified information. (508–524.)

## Career, clearances, and the first administrative process

Greene joined Engineering and Research Corporation in 1937 after aeronautical training and rose from junior engineer and draftsman to vice president and general manager. His work included a flight simulator and rocket launcher used by the Navy. ERCO developed and manufactured products for the armed services; classified projects required cleared personnel. Greene received Confidential and Top Secret clearances in 1949 and 1950. A security agreement required ERCO to exclude designated persons from areas containing military contract work and prevent unauthorized access to classified matter. The agreement’s own statement that exclusion did not necessarily imply dismissal did not preserve this executive’s job when access was essential to his duties. (475–477 & nn.1–2.)

In November 1951, the Army-Navy-Air Force Personnel Security Board warned ERCO that its facility clearance was in jeopardy because of a tentative adverse decision concerning Greene. The company supported him as loyal and discreet, but he took a furlough. In December the Board revoked his clearance, identifying associations with alleged Communists, embassy contacts, and attendance at a purported Communist-front dinner during 1943–1947. Greene sought review before the Industrial Employment Review Board. (477–478 & nn.3–5.)

At the January 23, 1952 hearing, he appeared with counsel and explained that many questioned persons were associates of his former wife, whose views he rejected. He attributed some publications and activities to that marriage, denied being Communist, and explained embassy visits as efforts to sell ERCO products. Company executives and military officers corroborated him. Government produced no witnesses, though questions revealed reliance on confidential investigative reports he could not see or test. On January 29 the review board nevertheless reversed the adverse decision and authorized him for Secret work. This favorable determination preceded, rather than followed, the later discharge under review. (478–480.)

## Reorganization, renewed revocation, and loss of profession

The Defense Secretary abolished both boards on March 27, 1953, directing the military departments to create regional boards with uniform standards and procedures. During the transition, prior criteria remained applicable. On April 17 the Navy Secretary independently informed ERCO that Greene’s continuing access was inconsistent with national security and requested his exclusion from classified areas and information. No hearing preceded that action. ERCO’s executive stated that, because there was no work Greene could perform in his position without access, the company had to discharge him and had no other reason to do so. The Navy refused further discussion as useless. (480–483 & nn.6–13.)

After reconsideration requests, his case went to the Eastern Industrial Personnel Security Board. A letter dated April 9, 1954, supplied thirteen general specifications nineteen days before a hearing beginning April 28 and continuing for two further days. The specifications covered his first marriage, publications, bookshop membership, investment in a radio station, embassy business contacts, and acquaintances. The Court’s long fourteenth note reproduces those allegations alongside contextual explanations; they are not Supreme Court findings that Greene committed espionage or held the alleged views. (483–486 & n.14.)

The Board told him the transcript would omit confidential investigations, informants’ identities, and source-revealing material. Greene underwent extensive questioning and offered corroborating witnesses, but again could not confront adverse declarants or investigators. He testified that his former salary was $18,000 and his current architectural-drafting salary $4,700; a separate affidavit described an initial $4,400 salary. His efforts to return to aeronautical engineering had failed because he lacked clearance. The opinion treats the resulting impairment of his chosen profession as substantial, not merely loss of access to one location. (486–489 & nn.15–16; 491 n.21.)

The regional board affirmed the adverse action and declined detailed findings for security reasons. Greene requested further review in September 1955. In March 1956 the review office supplied a general account of associations and political sympathies, questioned his credibility and trustworthiness, and affirmed. Note 19 observes that untrustworthiness had not previously been charged or found. These were administrative determinations, not the Court’s own factual findings of disloyalty. (489–490 & nn.17–19.)

## Judicial proceedings and parties’ positions

After the 1954 regional decision, Greene sued in the District of Columbia for a declaration that revocation was unlawful, an order preventing officials from acting on it, and notification to ERCO that it was void. Following final administrative review, both sides sought dispositive relief. The District Court granted the government summary judgment; the D.C. Circuit affirmed. Although the appellate court recognized severe employment and reputational harm, it considered the dispute nonjusticiable because clearance judgments belonged to the Executive and rejected demands for confrontation and disclosure. (490–492 & nn.20–21.)

Greene challenged both authorization and constitutionality, invoking his private employment and freedom to pursue his profession. Government accepted the serious consequences but characterized them as incidental results of necessary protection of military secrets. Alternatively, it argued that the safeguards provided were sufficient in a program requiring confidentiality to preserve information flows. The Supreme Court accepted standing to challenge substantially injurious unauthorized official action and chose the narrower authorization ground. (492–493 & n.22.)

## Traditional safeguards and the delegation question

The Court did not ask simply whether a clearance program could exist or whether secrets could be protected. It asked whether Congress or the President had authorized officials to impose substantial employment restrictions through factual determinations that withheld the traditional safeguards of confrontation and cross-examination. The programs and contested procedural rules had been created through departmental directives, not specific legislation or a presidential order establishing those procedures. (493–496 & nn.23–24.)

Warren emphasized that serious action resting on factual accusations normally demands disclosure sufficient to let the person demonstrate falsity. An investigator’s summary could reflect faulty memory, bias, mistaken inference, or malice. Here even the boards generally did not see the informants and often did not question the investigators. The twenty-sixth note illustrates how subjective impressions about Greene’s marriage and political views entered the inquiry; the twenty-seventh reproduces testimony acknowledging potentially multiple layers of hearsay. Those concerns explain why the Court demanded explicit authorization for a departure. They did not become a final constitutional adjudication of every possible clearance process. (496–500 & nn.25–27.)

## Executive orders, statutes, and appropriations

Executive Orders 10290 and 10501 regulated classification, transmission, and dissemination of sensitive information, including dissemination outside the Executive. They did not expressly authorize a program that could deprive civilians of their existing work and profession without an effective chance to challenge adverse evidence. The Court likewise found no sufficiently specific procedural authority in wartime Executive Order 8972. (500–502 & n.28.)

The National Security Act’s departmental structure and general administration powers, and the Armed Services Procurement Act’s contracting provisions, did not authorize the contested nonconfrontation procedure. Criminal statutes protecting classified and cryptographic information demonstrated the importance of secrecy but did not supply the missing authorization. Nor did funding establish ratification. The particular appropriation discussed concerned reimbursement of workers whose clearances were ultimately granted, and the hearings did not describe the disputed procedure sufficiently to show deliberate approval of it. General appropriations to bodies with many functions were also insufficient. (502–506 & nn.29–30.)

Delegation can often be inferred where government’s power to act is clear and officials implement general standards with political-branch acquiescence. The Court expressly had no difficulty recognizing authority for a clearance program affording confrontation and cross-examination. Its insistence on explicit action addressed a serious departure from traditional fairness in an area of questionable constitutionality. Political responsibility required the President or Congress deliberately to confront the need for that departure; silence could not relegate a consequential choice to administrators by default. (506–508 & n.31.)

## Concurrences and Clark’s dissent

Frankfurter, Harlan, and Whittaker joined the judgment on the limited failure to demonstrate authorization. Harlan’s additional opinion viewed the constitutional question as difficult and premature. He thought the majority unnecessarily discussed the issue it disclaimed deciding and relied instead on the rule of avoiding unnecessary constitutional decisions. He also answered Clark’s description of the result: nothing required that Greene be given classified material despite concerns about reliability. The unresolved issue was the permissible procedure, not a constitutional entitlement to military secrets. (508–510.)

Clark would sustain the officials’ action. In his view nobody had a constitutional right to classified information, and government’s choice to disclose it for procurement did not create a constitutional right merely because private employment depended on access. He also considered the provided administrative safeguards adequate, citing cases allowing confidentiality and disputing the majority’s characterization of its precedents. (510–516 & dissent nn.1–5.)

On authority, he read the wartime directives, departmental practices, National Security Act, reports to Presidents, 1953 reorganization, loyalty-program rules, congressional printing of regulations, and continued appropriations as ample approval. His detailed historical account differed from the majority’s requirement of deliberate authorization for the particular procedure. He warned that the decision threatened litigation and loss-of-wage claims and that its constitutional language cast a cloud beyond the stated narrow holding. Those were dissenting predictions, not a damages award or Supreme Court holding ordering disclosure of secrets. (516–524 & dissent nn.6–12.)

## Disposition and assigned qualification

The Court reversed and remanded to the District Court for proceedings consistent with its opinion. It did not finally decide constitutional validity of an expressly authorized nonconfrontation procedure or direct an unconditional clearance. The assigned October-b PDF 43–44 / printed 472–473 introduces Greene after the Cafeteria Workers account and reproduces the majority’s employment-impact passage. Its reference to clearance termination “without a hearing” is too broad: Greene had hearings, but not the opportunity to confront and cross-examine the adverse sources. That procedural deficiency, without explicit authorization for it, is the precise holding. (478–480, 485–490, 508–510.)
