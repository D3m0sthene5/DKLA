# Tagger v. Strauss Group Ltd.

**951 F.3d 124 (2d Cir. 2020) · Second Circuit · February 27, 2020 · No. 18–3189**

**Civil Procedure — alienage jurisdiction, lawful permanent residents, and treaty access to courts.** Assigned: CivPro.pdf, physical page 181, printed page 315, the Tagger parenthetical following the permanent-resident amendments discussion. Pinpoints below use the numbered pages of the preserved eight-page court-issued opinion, not an inferred reporter-page conversion.

## Facts and proceedings

Benjamin Tagger, an Israeli citizen, lived in Brooklyn as a lawful permanent resident of the United States. Acting without counsel, he sued Strauss Group Ltd., an Israeli corporation headquartered in Israel, for common-law contract and tort claims. He alleged that Strauss had falsely initiated legal proceedings against him in Israel, causing him to be prohibited from leaving that country. The Second Circuit did not decide whether those allegations were true or whether Strauss was liable. The question was whether an American federal court could hear this dispute. (2–4.)

Tagger invoked diversity jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1332(a). Strauss sought dismissal on several grounds, including lack of subject-matter jurisdiction and forum non conveniens. The Eastern District of New York, Judge Cogan, dismissed: Tagger’s lawful permanent residence did not make him a New York citizen for diversity purposes in a suit against another alien, and Israeli courts were the more appropriate forum. Tagger appealed. The Second Circuit reviewed jurisdictional factual findings for clear error and the legal conclusions de novo. (3.)

On appeal, Tagger did not challenge the district court’s statutory interpretation. Instead, he relied on the United States–Israel Treaty of Friendship, Commerce and Navigation, contending that its access-to-courts provisions supplied jurisdiction. The panel nevertheless explained both the statutory rule and why the treaty did not overcome it. (4–7.)

## Issues, holdings, and disposition

Does § 1332(a)(2) permit a suit by an Israeli citizen who is a New York-domiciled lawful permanent resident against an Israeli corporation? Does the 1951 treaty independently confer federal jurisdiction over his claims?

**No.** Permanent-resident status did not transform Tagger into a United States citizen for alienage jurisdiction. Both relevant parties were aliens, so § 1332(a)(2) did not supply diversity jurisdiction. The treaty guaranteed equal access and treatment; it did not remove the jurisdictional requirements governing access to federal court. The panel **affirmed the district court’s judgment**. Its stated grounds concerned subject-matter jurisdiction, not adjudication of the underlying contract and tort claims. (6–8.)

## Why permanent residence did not establish diversity

The opinion first distinguished residence from the citizenship required by the diversity statute. It acknowledged the ordinary rule that an individual’s diversity citizenship follows domicile, but Tagger remained an Israeli citizen. Strauss’s foreign status was undisputed: it was incorporated and headquartered in Israel. The issue was whether the statutory treatment of permanent residents supplied the missing United States citizenship on Tagger’s side. (3–4.)

The statutory history answered that question. In 1988 Congress had added a provision deeming an alien admitted for permanent residence a citizen of the state where the alien was domiciled. Courts disagreed about whether that language permitted a permanent resident to sue another alien through diversity jurisdiction. In 2011 the Federal Courts Jurisdiction and Venue Clarification Act removed that deeming provision. Congress instead specified an exception denying § 1332(a)(2) jurisdiction in actions between a citizen of a state and a foreign citizen lawfully admitted for permanent residence and domiciled in the same state. The change restricted jurisdiction in that same-state situation without declaring a permanent resident to be a United States citizen. (4–5.)

The legislative history explained that eliminating deemed citizenship avoided anomalous results and addressed possible Article III problems. The Constitution extends the judicial power to specified categories involving citizens of different states and citizens and foreign parties; the former wording risked treating litigation between foreign parties as though one had become a United States citizen merely by permanent residence. The panel used that history to reinforce the statutory reading. It did not purport to resolve every hypothetical constitutional question about suits involving foreign parties. (5–6.)

The resulting rule was straightforward for these parties: Tagger and Strauss were both aliens. Section 1332(a)(2) therefore did not authorize their dispute. The opinion’s two-foreign-party formulation concerns this posture; it should not be expanded into a prohibition on every action with aliens appearing on both sides. The assignment separately identifies § 1332(a)(3), involving completely diverse United States citizens on opposing sides with foreign parties as additional parties. That different configuration was not Tagger’s case. (6; assigned 314–315.)

## Why the treaty did not create a federal forum

Article V(1) of the 1951 United States–Israel treaty promises national and most-favored-nation treatment regarding access to courts and administrative tribunals, in pursuit or defense of rights. Tagger read that promise as conferring federal jurisdiction. The panel rejected the inference from equal access to an expanded federal adjudicatory power. (6–7.)

Relying on its earlier treaty interpretation in Blanco and the Supreme Court’s explanation in Sumitomo Shoji, the panel distinguished procedural equality from substantive entitlement. Access provisions protect matters such as filing fees, representation by lawyers, legal aid, and security for costs. National treatment means treatment equal to domestic nationals; most-favored-nation treatment means no less favorable treatment than nationals or companies of another country. Neither supplies an exemption from a court’s otherwise applicable jurisdictional rules. The court concluded that Tagger still had to satisfy the diversity requirement and could not use the treaty to establish federal jurisdiction where the statute did not provide it. (7–8.)

That reasoning does not announce that a treaty can never create an enforceable right or ground of federal jurisdiction. It interprets the particular access and treatment provisions invoked here. Nor does the affirmance decide Tagger’s common-law claims on their merits. The absence of jurisdiction was enough to sustain dismissal. (6–8.)
