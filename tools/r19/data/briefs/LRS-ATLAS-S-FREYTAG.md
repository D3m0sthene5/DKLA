# Freytag v. Commissioner of Internal Revenue
501 U.S. 868 (June 27, 1991), No. 90–762 | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Facts and statutory setting

The taxpayers sought Tax Court review of federal income-tax deficiencies after deducting approximately $1.5 billion in losses allegedly generated by a tax shelter. Their counsel described the litigation as potentially affecting 3,000 taxpayers and involving about fourteen weeks of evidence, 9,000 transcript pages, and over 3,000 exhibits. Its scale supported their argument that Congress could not have intended a special trial judge to determine it. The Court's factual account identifies the $1.5 billion as deducted losses; the accompanying footnote records counsel's separate description of alleged deficiencies. 501 U.S. at 871 and n.1.

The Tax Court, established as an Article I court in 1969, consisted of nineteen judges appointed by the President with Senate consent for fifteen-year terms. Congress authorized its Chief Judge to appoint special trial judges. Section 7443A(b) permitted assignments of declaratory-judgment proceedings, specified small-tax proceedings, cases within a $10,000 limit, and any other proceeding designated by the Chief Judge. Under subsection (c), special trial judges could enter decisions in the first three categories, but in the fourth could only hear the case and prepare proposed findings and an opinion; a regular Tax Court judge had to render the decision. 870–873.

The taxpayers' cases were assigned to Judge Wilbur in 1982, and trial began in 1984. When he became ill in November 1985, Special Trial Judge Powell became evidentiary referee, with proceedings videotaped. After Wilbur retired and assumed senior status in April 1986, the taxpayers expressly consented to reassignment to Powell for written findings and an opinion. Powell found sham transactions and additional taxes due. The Chief Judge adopted his opinion as the Tax Court's decision. 871–872.

## Procedure and arguments

The taxpayers challenged both statutory authority to assign a large, complex case and compliance with the Appointments Clause. The Fifth Circuit affirmed, sustaining the assignment and treating consent as waiver of the constitutional objection. The Supreme Court granted review of the statutory and structural constitutional questions. Neither here nor below did the taxpayers claim that the Tax Court itself was an illegitimate tribunal. 872–873.

The taxpayers argued that the catchall assignment provision should be confined to relatively minor matters comparable to the preceding categories. They also argued that special trial judges were inferior federal officers whose appointments had to come from a constitutionally eligible source, and that the Chief Judge was neither a department head nor a court of law. The Commissioner defended the statutory assignment, asserted waiver, characterized a special trial judge preparing recommendations as an employee, and alternatively treated the Tax Court as an executive department. An amicus advanced the court-of-law ground ultimately accepted by the majority. 873–878, 880–885.

## Holdings, votes, and judgment

The Court affirmed the Fifth Circuit. All nine Justices joined Blackmun's Parts I–III, holding that section 7443A(b)(4) authorized assignment of any Tax Court proceeding, regardless of amount or complexity, for hearing and proposed findings while reserving the final decision to a regular judge. Blackmun's constitutional analysis in Part IV had five votes: Rehnquist, White, Marshall, Stevens, and Blackmun. It reached the objection despite consent, held special trial judges to be inferior officers, and upheld appointment because the Tax Court was a court of law within Article II. 873–892.

Scalia, joined by O'Connor, Kennedy, and Souter, concurred in part and in the judgment. Those four agreed with the statutory holding, the officer classification, and affirmance, but would have enforced forfeiture of the constitutional objection. On the merits, they would uphold appointment because the Tax Court was an executive department headed by the Chief Judge. Thus the result was unanimous, while the court-of-law ground and decision to entertain the constitutional objection belonged to the five-Justice majority. 869–870, 892–894, 901–902, 922.

## Why the statute permitted this assignment

The phrase any other proceeding contained no amount, complexity, or subject limitation. Legislative history confirmed that Congress intended assignments regardless of amount, provided a regular judge entered the decision. The change was described as technical because special trial judges and predecessor commissioners already assisted with complex cases; that description did not narrow the enacted text. The taxpayers' theory confused authority to hear and recommend with authority to decide. 873–875.

Nor did the catchall render the earlier categories redundant. Those categories carried greater decisional authority; the fourth covered a broader range of cases but carried less. Restricting it to precisely the same cases would make the catchall itself superfluous. Gomez's construction of a different general assignment provision in the Federal Magistrates Act therefore did not control. 876–877.

The Court did not assume that simultaneous filing and adoption of Powell's lengthy report proved rubber-stamping. The record did not establish how long the Chief Judge had studied it, counsel acknowledged an earlier report, and the taxpayers bore the burden of any negative inference. A further argument about deferential review under Tax Court Rule 183 was outside the question granted; the Court reiterated the statutory reservation of final decision rather than resolving that new challenge. 872 n.2; 874–875 n.3.

## Why the majority reached the constitutional question

The majority treated the appointment objection as nonjurisdictional but structural. It acknowledged the ordinary requirement to raise objections at trial and the taxpayers' express consent. Nevertheless, drawing on Glidden and the judiciary's responsibility to maintain the constitutional separation of powers, it exercised discretion in what it described as a rare case: a serious challenge to the validity of the very adjudicatory proceeding under review. It did not announce that every structural objection could be raised at any time. 878–879.

The Commissioner's assurance that the Executive did not view its prerogatives as threatened was insufficient. The Clause protects the constitutional distribution of appointment authority for the Republic, rather than merely a branch's institutional advantage. Congress and the Executive could not agree away those structural limits. This explains review despite the government's consent to the statutory arrangement, while the majority's treatment of the litigants' own consent remained an exercise of appellate discretion. 879–880.

## Officer status and the permissible appointing authority

The special trial judge's office was established by statute, with legally specified duties, salary, and appointment method. Its holder conducted trials, took testimony, decided evidentiary issues, enforced discovery orders, and exercised substantial discretion. These features distinguished the position from temporary special masters. Lack of final decisional authority in this particular large case did not negate that significant authority. Independently, special trial judges could enter final decisions in the smaller statutory categories. A single office did not alternate between officer and employee status depending on the day's assignment. 880–882.

The constitutional question therefore became whether the Tax Court's Chief Judge qualified through one of the Clause's authorized repositories. The majority rejected the premise that every executive component was a department. It construed department heads to mean leaders of executive divisions like Cabinet-level departments, emphasizing limited diffusion of appointment authority and political accountability. It also found treating the Tax Court as an executive department inconsistent with Congress's explicit 1969 purpose to transform it into an Article I court. Importantly, footnote 4 reserved questions about principal independent agencies and did not simply equate constitutional departments with the closed statutory Cabinet list. 882–888 and 887 n.4.

The majority held that courts of law need not be Article III courts. Legislative tribunals could exercise judicial power, and their history supplied support for appointment of inferior officers. The Tax Court interpreted and applied tax law in government-taxpayer disputes, performed exclusively judicial functions, possessed familiar judicial powers, and issued decisions reviewed by courts of appeals in the same manner as district-court civil judgments. It was neither an advocate nor a political rulemaker. Those functions and its independence supported classification as a court of law eligible to appoint its own assistant judges. The opinion did not concern an incongruous appointment of personnel in another executive department. 883, 888–892.

## Scalia's separate account

Scalia distinguished invalidating an unconstitutional arrangement prospectively from setting aside a judgment after an unpreserved objection. Consent could not make unlawful conduct lawful, but procedural forfeiture could still foreclose a particular remedy. Ordinary forfeiture rules protected orderly trials, informed appellate review, and avoidance of strategic sandbagging. Subject-matter jurisdiction presented a distinct problem; the Tax Court's jurisdiction was not challenged here. He accepted discretion to hear truly exceptional forfeited claims but found no exceptional circumstance beyond their structural character and lack of frivolity, neither sufficient in his view. 892–901 and notes 1–3.

On substance, Scalia read the courts of law in Article II as Article III tribunals, whose life tenure and salary protections fortified them against legislative influence. Adjudication alone did not establish exercise of constitutional judicial power: executive officers also determined facts and applied legal rules. The Tax Court's time-limited judges, reducible salaries, and presidential removal made it an executive body on his account. Territorial courts and the precedents invoked by the majority did not establish otherwise. 901–914 and notes 4–5.

Scalia nevertheless regarded the Tax Court as a freestanding executive department. Department meant a separately organized executive establishment, not necessarily a Cabinet member's division. Historical appointment by the Postmaster General, modern independent-agency practice, and the Constitution's distinction between principal and subordinate officers supported appointment by the ultimate subpresidential supervisor. He criticized the majority's Cabinet-like formulation and reservation of independent-agency questions as uncertain. While recognizing that removal restrictions could weaken executive accountability, he did not find those concerns a basis for the majority's particular distinctions. 914–922.
