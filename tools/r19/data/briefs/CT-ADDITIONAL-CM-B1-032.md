# Ventura v. Titan Sports, Inc.
65 F.3d 725 (8th Cir. 1995) | Contracts

## Court, disposition, and governing law

Judge Magill, joined by Senior Judge John R. Gibson, affirmed the district court’s judgment in all respects on September 11, 1995. Judge Morris Sheppard Arnold dissented only from recovery for the period preceding negotiations by Ventura’s agent Barry Bloom. Rehearing and rehearing en banc were denied October 26. Minnesota law governed this diversity action. The affirmance preserved quantum-meruit recovery for exploitation of Jesse “The Body” Ventura’s commentary and the denial of interest for the period before filing suit. It was a decision following trial, not a ruling that allegations merely sufficed to proceed. (65 F.3d at 725, 727–729, 735–738; preserved full judicial reproduction, lines 49–62, 72–78, 150–178.)

## Agreements and exploitation

Titan operated the World Wrestling Federation. Its 1984 licensing arrangements led to toys and other merchandise, while video agreements produced approximately ninety videotapes involving Ventura. He began wrestling under an oral agreement in spring 1984. After medical problems, he returned as a commentator under a separate oral arrangement paying $1,000 weekly, without discussion of videotape licenses or royalties. A written Wrestling Booking Agreement effective January 1, 1985 governed his resumed wrestling. He left in March 1986 for acting, then returned later that year as a commentator under another oral agreement silent about videotape royalties. (Lines 63–67.)

In 1987, Bloom unsuccessfully negotiated with Titan’s production partner before Titan’s business-affairs vice president, Dick Glover, represented that royalties went only to performers featured in their own videotapes. Wanting the job, Ventura accepted commentary agreements waiving royalties. Bloom asked again in succeeding annual negotiations, and Glover repeated the policy. Contrary to those representations, Titan paid royalties to numerous wrestlers on tapes without a single featured performer. The district court credited Bloom and Ventura, found the representations false, and found that Ventura would not have accepted the waiver had he known the truth. (Lines 68, 115–120 & n.10.)

Ventura sued in December 1991 for fraud, appropriation of publicity rights, and quantum meruit. Only quantum meruit went to the jury, with a special verdict addressing misrepresentation. It found fraud and assessed $801,333.06 for videotape exploitation of commentary plus $8,625.60 for other merchandise. The district court subsequently concluded that the equitable claim carried no jury-trial entitlement, vacated that verdict, and entered its own consistent findings and conclusions. The operative judgment therefore rested on the court’s decision. It awarded interest from filing but denied prefiling interest. (Lines 70–75.)

## Before the royalty waiver: a benefit outside the contracts

Under Minnesota law, receiving a benefit alone did not establish unjust enrichment. The majority identified a benefit beyond the immediate labor for which Titan had paid: intellectual-property interests generated by Ventura’s performance. It predicted that Minnesota’s supreme court would recognize a right of publicity, distinguishing the pecuniary interest in controlling commercial exploitation from privacy protections against unwanted exposure. Titan’s unauthorized use of that interest could make retention unjust. This was a federal prediction of state law, not a holding previously announced by Minnesota’s highest court. Footnote 6 expressly recognized a troubling copyright-preemption issue but held it waived because Titan had not raised it timely on appeal; its supplemental-brief motion was denied. Preemption was not rejected on its merits. (Lines 78–90 & nn.5–6.)

An express contract barred quasi-contractual recovery on the same subject matter, but did not necessarily bar recovery for a distinct benefit it left unaddressed. The wrestling agreement foreclosed royalties for wrestling performances. The separate commentary agreements, however, were found not to cover videotape licenses. That finding concerned contractual intent and survived clear-error review: the parties had not discussed the rights, and initially Ventura lacked awareness of the impending commercialization. The opinion’s note acknowledged his testimony that he knew tapes were distributed in 1985; it did not assume continuing ignorance throughout the entire period. The majority sustained recovery for commentary, not an additional award for wrestling already governed by the written agreement. (Lines 91–98 & nn.7–8.)

## After the waiver: fraudulent inducement removed the contractual bar

The 1987–1990 agreements expressly covered royalties, so mere contractual silence could no longer support recovery. Fraudulent inducement instead permitted avoidance of the waiver and restitution of the reasonable royalty value. Although Ventura abandoned his separate fraud counts before trial and had not pleaded this particular policy misrepresentation sufficiently under Rule 9(b), Titan’s answer raised the express-contract defense. Because no responsive pleading was allowed, the then-applicable Rule 8(d) treated that defense as denied or avoided. Ventura could introduce inducement evidence to defeat it without recovering on an independent fraud cause of action. (Lines 100–111 & nn.3, 9.)

The testimony, repeated policy representations, inconsistent royalty payments, and credited evidence of reliance supported the district court’s rescission of the agreements. The majority observed that Minnesota restitution authorities did not require every element of a standalone fraud action as a universal prerequisite, but used those elements as a guide and rejected Titan’s specific attacks on materiality, inducement, reliance, causation, and injury. It deferred to the trial court’s credibility findings rather than treating the documentary waiver as conclusive despite the fraud. (Lines 112–120.)

## Valuation and interest

The court upheld admission of expert Weston Anson’s reasonable-royalty evidence under the governing evidence rules and reliability principles. Market licensing rates bore on both the reasonable benefit to Titan and the market value of Ventura’s rights. Titan’s negotiated-fee and internal-policy evidence could compete with that evidence; it did not make a broader market survey irrelevant. Anson’s survey of thousands of licenses supplied a sufficiently reliable method despite the absence of a perfectly identical transaction. The court rejected qualification and sufficiency objections. (Lines 121–141.)

Prefiling interest required a liquidated or readily ascertainable claim. Although valuation ultimately used market evidence, the royalty rate ranged from 3.5 to 7.5 percent and important payment details remained unsettled: the applicable sales base, classification of tapes involving multiple roles, and accrual and payment periods. Those contingencies required factfinding. The appellate court found no clear error in denying prefiling interest, while leaving intact interest awarded from filing. The expert’s highest estimate was not the judgment amount. (Lines 142–152 & nn.11–13.)

## Partial dissent and assigned-account correction

Arnold opposed recovery only before Bloom’s negotiations. He considered the prediction of Minnesota publicity rights unsupported, particularly given the state’s rejection of privacy torts. He also regarded Titan as the entrepreneur entitled to profits after paying agreed compensation and bearing production risks; Ventura had performed no additional services. Even assuming a publicity right, Arnold would construe authority to broadcast wrestling as including videotape dissemination, drawing an analogy to licenses permitting uses within an agreed medium. He would reverse that portion of the judgment rather than disturb the fraudulent-inducement recovery. (Lines 153–178.)
