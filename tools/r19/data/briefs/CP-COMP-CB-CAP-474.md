# Lydig Construction, Inc. v. Martinez Steel Corporation

**234 Cal. App. 4th 937, 184 Cal. Rptr. 3d 329 (2015)** — California Court of Appeal, Fourth District, Division One, February 26, 2015; D066854. Acting Presiding Justice Benke wrote; Justices Haller and McDonald concurred. Assigned: CivPro physical PDF page 602, printed 1188–1189, attachment note.

## Posture and result

The Court of Appeal affirmed the orders authorizing prejudgment attachment for **$203,315 against Martinez only**, and awarded Lydig its appellate costs. A cross-complaint alleging a larger offset did not automatically defeat attachment: the defendant had to establish the probable validity of that claim. Its conclusory declaration, contradicted by the applicant's business records, failed that standard. This was review of provisional security, not a final breach-of-contract damages award. (940, 943–946.)

## Facts and litigation history

Lydig won the 2010 general contract to expand San Bernardino County's Adelanto Detention Center. Its bid relied partly on Martinez's rebar bid. Their subcontract became effective January 14, 2011. Martinez could not provide the required payment and performance bonds; Lydig accepted its owner's personal guaranty instead. By February 2012 Martinez's raw-steel supplier required cash, so Lydig purchased steel and shipped it to Martinez for fabrication and installation. (940–941.)

In June 2012 Lydig discovered that Martinez had diverted 100,000 pounds of that steel to other projects. Lydig began obtaining rebar elsewhere, using Martinez employees for ten days to install the substitute supply. After a formal substitution process and an August county hearing, the county authorized termination of Martinez's subcontract. The hearing officer found failures to procure the steel and required bonds, and credited evidence of inadequate capacity and untrustworthiness. Lydig engaged another supplier. These facts supported the provisional application; the appellate decision did not turn that proceeding into the ultimate contract trial. (941.)

In March 2013 Lydig sued Martinez and its owner, alleging breach and damages exceeding $200,000. It applied for attachment in April, submitting employee declarations, business records, and a verified copy of the county hearing officer's findings. On May 14 Martinez and its owner answered and opposed the application. Their employee Harry Williams asserted retained payments, work billed after termination, disputed change orders, and more than 200,000 pounds of uncredited steel deliveries. (941–942.)

Williams did not rebut the application's specific factual account. He said Martinez had never missed a concrete pour and disputed the change orders without a supporting factual explanation. Lydig's reply supplied further testimony about delayed pours, accounting records defeating the retention claim, and a contemporaneous steel-delivery log contradicting the claimed additional deliveries. (942.)

By stipulation Martinez filed its cross-complaint on May 20, alleging amounts exceeding Lydig's claims. The court heard the attachment application that day, specifically heard argument about the offsets, and took the matter under submission. It later granted attachment against Martinez only, for $203,315. Lydig obtained writs in Riverside, Los Angeles, and San Bernardino counties. Martinez appealed from the right-to-attach order. The owner's presence in the complaint does not establish that the attachment order ran against the owner. (942–943.)

## Governing law and review

An appellant must demonstrate reversible error on an adequate record; silence supports presumptions favoring the order. When attachment depends on resolving factual conflicts in affidavits, the trial court's factual determination is not disturbed on appeal. The court applies that rule and the record-supporting presumption, rather than deciding that an appellate court must accept every pleaded offset as true. (943.)

Under the California provisions discussed in this 2015 decision, attachment generally required a money claim founded on express or implied contract, for a fixed or readily ascertainable sum of at least $500. Section 484.090 required an eligible claim, probable validity, a proper recovery purpose, and an amount to be secured **greater than zero**. Under section 481.190 probable validity means the claimant is more likely than not to obtain judgment. (943–944.)

The application needed an affidavit or declaration showing entitlement on particularized facts. Except where information and belief was expressly permitted, the declarant needed competent testimony based on actual personal knowledge. Documents needed proper identification, authentication, and an admissible basis, including a suitable business-records exception when applicable. Ultimate pleading allegations did not replace evidence. (944.)

The secured amount could include the claimed debt, estimated costs, and allowable attorney's fees. Section 483.015(b)(2) required reduction for an indebtedness asserted in a filed cross-complaint if the defendant's claim was itself one upon which attachment could issue. That qualifying language supplied the central statutory issue. The opinion's single note identifies subsequent statutory references as references to the Code of Civil Procedure. (943 n. 1, 944–945.)

## Why the alleged offset failed

Martinez argued that filing a cross-complaint larger than the attachment claim deprived the trial court of power to grant attachment. The court rejected that categorical proposition. A claim upon which attachment could issue must satisfy the same probable-validity requirement. Otherwise any defendant could defeat a well-supported attachment by asserting a speculative counterclaim, frustrating the Legislature's provision of prejudgment security. (945.)

The trial judge implicitly found Martinez's claims insufficient. The record showed that the judge noticed the offsets, heard argument, and said the parties' documentation would be considered. The appellate court therefore presumed consideration of the pertinent evidence where the record was otherwise silent. It did not treat the absence of an express finding as proof that the offsets were ignored. (945.)

The evidentiary problem was concrete: Williams's conclusory assertions concerning retained funds and 200,000 pounds of steel were contradicted by accounting records and contemporaneous delivery logs. The court agreed that those documents undermined the probable validity of the claims. Separately, it rejected Martinez's challenge to Lydig's own showing because declarations and documentation supported the validity and amount of that claim. The decision does not forbid offsets generally; it requires evidence meeting the statutory standard. (945–946.)

## Assignment and source qualifications

The textbook correctly presents an unsuccessful attempt to defeat attachment through an offset exceeding the applicant's claim. Its reference to the same standard of “probable cause” should be read as **probable validity**, the term defined and applied by the opinion. Its introductory statement that the secured amount is “not less than zero” also needs precision: the opinion requires an amount **greater than zero** before attachment issues. Neither difference warrants changing the actual statutory account. (Assigned 602; actual 944–945.)

**Affirmed; Lydig recovers appellate costs.** The report records a later denial of review on June 10, 2015, S225598; that is subsequent procedural history, not the decision date or a separate merits ruling here. No separate opinion or appendix appears. (946.)
