# C. Itoh & Co. (America) Inc. v. Jordan International Co.

552 F.2d 1228 (7th Cir. 1977) | Contracts

United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit, argued February 17 and decided April 4, 1977, No. 76-1707. Judge Sprecher wrote the opinion. The complete judicial source, including all ten footnotes, supplements the assigned C&M Textbook 1–350 excerpt at PDF 326–328, printed 281–283.

## Parties

C. Itoh & Co. (America) Inc. was the buyer, plaintiff, and appellee. The Jordan International Company was the steel seller, defendant, and appellant. Itoh sought judicial relief for allegedly defective steel and late delivery. Jordan contended that the dispute had to be arbitrated under a provision in its acknowledgment and sought to stay the litigation pending arbitration.

## Material Facts

Itoh sent Jordan a purchase order dated August 15, 1974, for steel coils. Jordan responded with its acknowledgment dated August 19, 1974. Its language expressly conditioned the seller's acceptance on the buyer's assent to the additional or different terms appearing in the form and on its reverse side. It also directed the buyer to notify the seller immediately if the terms were unacceptable. One reverse-side provision required arbitration. Itoh's purchase order contained no corresponding arbitration clause. The writings therefore did not establish agreement on arbitration. Jordan nevertheless delivered the steel, and Itoh paid for it. Itoh later complained that the steel was defective and had arrived late. The parties' performance showed that they were carrying out a sales transaction even though the seller had never obtained the assent required by its expressly conditional acknowledgment. The question became which terms governed the contract demonstrated by that performance. (1230.)

Before litigation, Itoh resold the steel to Riverview Steel Corporation. That separate contract contained an arbitration clause excluding quality disputes. After delivery and payment to Jordan, Riverview refused to pay Itoh, claiming defective steel and improper late tender. Itoh sued both Riverview for nonpayment and Jordan for defects and delay. These allegations were not final findings of breach. (1230 & n. 1.)

## Procedural History

Jordan sought a stay under FAA § 3. The district court apparently assumed an arbitration agreement between Jordan and Itoh but denied a stay because the quality exclusion in the Itoh–Riverview contract left some issues in court. It reasoned that sound judicial administration called for resolving the entire litigation in one forum. The Seventh Circuit rejected that rationale, then affirmed on a different ground: no contractual arbitration provision existed between Jordan and Itoh. (1230–1232; 1238.)

The historical interlocutory-appeal doctrine permitted review of the denial under 28 U.S.C. § 1292(a)(1), because the underlying lawsuit was an action at law. Itoh’s objection that the trial court had not resolved whether a written arbitration agreement existed went to the merits of the stay, not appealability. (1230–1231 n. 2.) The appeal did not finally decide the quality of the steel, timeliness of delivery, or damages.

## Issue

Could judicial economy justify refusing a stay if FAA § 3’s prerequisites were satisfied? Could UCC § 2-201 establish an added arbitration term?

When a seller expressly conditions acceptance on the buyer's assent to additional terms, the buyer does not assent, and both parties nevertheless perform, does § 2-207(3) create a contract? If so, can the seller's arbitration clause become one of that contract's terms through the buyer's receipt and payment or through the provision for supplementary Code terms?

## Rule and Holding

The seller's acknowledgment satisfied the expressly-conditional proviso in § 2-207(1). The exchange of writings therefore did not itself create a contract; the acknowledgment operated as a counteroffer. The parties' subsequent performance nevertheless established a contract under § 2-207(3). That contract included the terms on which the writings agreed and applicable supplementary Code terms.

The parties' writings did not agree to arbitration, and Article 2 did not supply arbitration as a gap-filling term. The unaccepted clause was therefore excluded. Delivery and payment established the sales contract without establishing assent to that additional clause. (1235–1237.)

## Judgment

The Seventh Circuit affirmed the denial of Jordan's motion to stay the proceedings pending arbitration. Itoh could continue litigating its claims in court. The decision resolved the asserted obligation to arbitrate; it did not establish that Itoh had proved breach or its claimed damages. (1238.)

## Reasoning

FAA § 3 used mandatory language. If the dispute was referable to arbitration under a written agreement and the applicant was not in default, the district court had to grant the stay. Judicial economy and the presence of another defendant whose disputes remained in court could not override that statutory command. The court’s affirmance therefore does not endorse the district court’s single-forum rationale. (1231–1232 & n. 3.)

Jordan also invoked New York decisions applying UCC § 2-201. The court distinguished that provision’s removal of a Statute of Frauds defense from proof that the parties made a contract or agreed to a particular term. A merchant’s failure to object to a confirmation can eliminate the writing defense, but § 2-207 governs whether additional terms enter an established agreement. Jordan argued that the exchange of forms itself created the contract, not that its form confirmed a prior oral agreement; § 2-201 did not establish the arbitration clause. The court also predicted that New York’s highest court would reject an interpretation effectively reading § 2-207 out of the Code. (1232–1234 & n. 4.)

Jordan's form used the condition that Dorton's forms lacked: it expressly tied acceptance to the buyer's assent to additional or different terms. That language prevented contract formation through the writings unless the condition was satisfied. The seller could not rely on § 2-207(1)'s ordinary rule treating a nonmatching acknowledgment as an acceptance while simultaneously insisting that its acceptance was withheld pending further assent. (1235–1236.)

The failure of the writings to form a contract did not end the analysis. Section 2-207(3) expressly recognizes contracts demonstrated by both parties' conduct. Jordan delivered and Itoh paid. Those reciprocal acts supplied objective evidence of a sales relationship. The Code thus avoided treating a completed commercial exchange as legally nonexistent merely because the parties' forms had failed to match or form a contract on their own. (1236.)

The same subsection determined the terms. Its agreed-terms rule excluded Jordan's additional arbitration provision because the buyer's purchase order did not agree to it. Requiring an express contradiction in the buyer's form would defeat that rule: a term present only in the seller's form still was not a term on which both writings agreed. Neither the seller's last word in the exchange nor the buyer's ordinary performance automatically made that provision binding. (1236–1237.)

Jordan also could not reintroduce the arbitration clause as a supplementary term. The court interpreted that language as referring to the standardized gap-filling provisions of Article 2. Such provisions can complete a contract by supplying applicable default obligations. They do not serve as a route for importing disputed boilerplate that the agreed-terms rule has just excluded. Arbitration was not a necessary missing term supplied by the Code. The sales contract could operate with disputes resolved through the ordinary judicial process. (1237.)

The court also addressed fairness to a seller choosing an expressly conditional form. Before the buyer assents or the parties proceed with conduct creating a contract, the seller benefits from not being committed through the writings alone. If the additional terms truly are essential, it can withhold delivery until it obtains the required assent. If it elects to deliver without that assent, it accepts the risk that the conduct-based contract will omit its unagreed terms. Giving the seller both freedom from a contract when it wishes to withdraw and automatic inclusion of its boilerplate when it wishes to perform would distort the statutory allocation. (1237–1238.)

The court distinguished Construction Aggregates, where the buyer objected to selected added terms and the seller agreed to the changes, showing negotiated assent beyond ordinary receipt and payment. No comparable additional conduct was suggested here. Its rejection of Roto-Lith’s common-law approach did not make receipt and payment incapable of operating within a fuller factual agreement; those acts alone did not adopt Jordan’s disputed terms in this case. (1236 n. 8; 1237 n. 9.)

Finally, even assuming an unagreed term could enter a subsection (3) contract through custom and usage in a simple diversity case, the FAA independently required a written agreement to arbitrate. A signature was not essential, but an agreement in writing was. The contract formed by performance contained no written arbitration provision, so a stay still could not issue. This was an alternative federal-ground analysis, not a finding that trade usage actually supplied arbitration. (1238.)

## Notes

C. Itoh illustrates the cost of making acceptance expressly conditional. That drafting can prevent formation through the forms, but it does not guarantee that the seller's preferred terms will govern if the parties proceed anyway. If agreement to arbitration is essential, the seller must obtain the requisite assent before relying on the conditional language and performing. The comparison with Dorton turns on the formation route. Dorton required further examination under subsections (1) and (2), including material alteration, because the acknowledgments did not clearly condition acceptance on assent. C. Itoh proceeded under subsection (3), where the additional arbitration term was excluded because it was not agreed upon and was not supplied by the Code. Its exclusion did not depend on deciding the material-alteration question that remained open in Dorton.
