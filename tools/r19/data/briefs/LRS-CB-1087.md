## Parties

Southern Utah Wilderness Alliance and other environmental organizations, collectively SUWA in the opinion, challenged the Bureau of Land Management's response to off-road-vehicle use on federal land in Utah. They sought court orders requiring additional land-management, monitoring, and environmental-review actions. The Secretary of the Interior and other federal officials defended the agency's conduct and sought Supreme Court review after the Tenth Circuit revived claims that the District Court had rejected. 542 U.S. at 58-62.

BLM's land-management responsibility did not make every requested protective measure an enforceable duty. The controversy concerned whether the plaintiffs could identify omitted acts of the kind a court may compel under APA § 706(1). The underlying environmental allegations and the existence of general statutory responsibilities supplied the setting for that inquiry, rather than independently answering it. Id. at 61-67.

## Material facts and statutory framework

The opinion described BLM as managing nearly 23 million acres of federal land in Utah. The Federal Land Policy and Management Act of 1976, or FLPMA, directed management under multiple-use and sustained-yield principles and required land-use planning. BLM also identified wilderness study areas, which remained under administrative management while Congress considered whether to designate them as wilderness. These study areas were not automatically subject to every rule applicable to congressionally designated wilderness. Id. at 58-60.

FLPMA § 1782(c) directed BLM to manage wilderness study areas so as not to impair their suitability for preservation as wilderness, subject to the statutory qualifications described in the opinion. This nonimpairment obligation applied pending Congress's decision, including to study areas the Secretary had not recommended for wilderness designation. Off-road-vehicle use was allowed in parts of the affected land, and SUWA alleged that increasing use threatened wilderness characteristics. The Supreme Court did not determine that every alleged environmental injury had occurred or that particular management practices complied with FLPMA. Id. at 59-61, 65-67.

SUWA identified three groups of asserted failures. First, it alleged failure to protect wilderness study areas against impairment from vehicle use. Second, it alleged failure to implement commitments in land-use plans, including preparation of a route-designation plan for the San Rafael area and use supervision and monitoring described in the Henry Mountains plan. Third, it alleged failure to undertake further environmental analysis under NEPA in light of increased vehicle use. The claims invoked different possible sources of duty and required separate analysis. Id. at 60-62, 67-73.

The land-use plans were broad management frameworks. They addressed numerous resource uses and scheduled further, more specific plans and implementation decisions. The San Rafael plan, for example, covered approximately 1.5 million acres and twenty resource-management categories. Its projected future work illustrated how a plan could guide agency priorities without making every announced future step an immediately enforceable command. Id. at 69-71.

## Procedural history

SUWA filed suit in federal district court in 1999. It sought to use APA § 706(1), which authorizes a reviewing court to compel agency action unlawfully withheld or unreasonably delayed, to obtain the protective and planning measures it alleged BLM had omitted. The District Court dismissed the three claims, and SUWA appealed. 542 U.S. at 60-62.

A divided Tenth Circuit reversed in relevant part. It treated BLM's alleged failure to prevent impairment as reviewable inaction, regarded implementation duties under the land-use plans as judicially enforceable, and concluded that the agency had to take a hard look at new information relevant to NEPA supplementation. The Supreme Court granted review to determine the reach of the asserted action-forcing obligations. Id. at 61-62.

Developments during the litigation narrowed the plan claims. BLM completed the San Rafael route-designation plan and created use-supervision files for designated areas in the Henry Mountains region. The Court treated the claims seeking those completed steps as moot to that extent. A separate demand for continuing monitoring at Factory Butte remained live even though a monitoring program had recently begun, because beginning the program did not answer the demand for its continuing implementation. The Court therefore examined the surviving demand and explained how the same reasoning would apply to any claimed continuing maintenance obligation. Id. at 67-68 & n.3.

The Supreme Court reversed the Tenth Circuit and remanded. It rejected the broad § 706(1) theory, held the relevant plan statements nonbinding as affirmative commands, and found no continuing major federal action requiring supplementation of the completed plan-approval analysis. Id. at 64-73.

## Questions presented

What limits apply when a plaintiff asks a court to remedy agency inaction by compelling action under APA § 706(1)? Did FLPMA's general nonimpairment obligation require the particular discrete acts SUWA wanted? Did the land-use plans create a legally binding affirmative monitoring duty, and was it necessary to decide whether the requested monitoring was discrete? Finally, did increased vehicle use require further NEPA analysis after the agency had completed approval of the pertinent land-use plan? Id. at 62-73.

## Rule and holding

A § 706(1) claim may proceed only when the plaintiff identifies a discrete agency action that the agency is legally required to take. The discreteness and legal-duty requirements perform separate work. A court may compel a required decision or act while leaving its lawful substance to agency discretion; it may not use § 706(1) to supervise general compliance with a statutory program. Id. at 62-65. FLPMA's nonimpairment mandate imposed a management objective, but it left BLM discretion over how to achieve that objective. SUWA's demand to compel compliance with that broad mandate did not identify a discrete required action of the kind § 706(1) permits a court to order. The existence of a mandatory objective therefore did not by itself establish the requested action-forcing remedy. Id. at 65-67.

The Henry Mountains plan's statements about future use supervision and monitoring did not create a legally binding commitment enforceable under § 706(1). Because the binding-duty requirement failed, the Court expressly found it unnecessary to decide whether the monitoring envisioned was sufficiently discrete. It did not hold that all monitoring is inherently nondiscrete. Id. at 69-72 & n.5.

NEPA supplementation depended on major federal action remaining to occur. Approval of the land-use plan was the relevant major federal action, and that approval was complete. The continuing existence of the plan did not create an ongoing approval action requiring supplementation on the theory advanced. Later plan amendment or revision could require additional NEPA analysis. Id. at 72-73.

## Actual judgment

The Supreme Court reversed the judgment of the Tenth Circuit and remanded for further proceedings consistent with its opinion. It did not order BLM to close particular routes, impose a judicially designed monitoring program, or prepare the requested supplemental environmental analysis. Nor did it decide that vehicle use caused no environmental harm or that BLM had no continuing responsibilities under federal land-management law. 542 U.S. at 73.

## Reasoning of the Court

### 1. The APA defines the kind of agency action a court can compel

APA § 551(13) defines agency action through categories such as rules, orders, licenses, sanctions, and relief, together with their equivalents, denials, and failures to act. The listed categories concern identifiable administrative acts. The Court read the residual language in the context of those categories: an equivalent or failure to act must remain connected to a sufficiently circumscribed agency action. The definition does not encompass every alleged deficiency in an agency's administration of a program. Id. at 62-63.

The Court also distinguished a denial from a failure to act. A denial reflects a decision rejecting requested agency relief; a failure may involve omission of a required response. Although both can fall within the APA's definition, a plaintiff invoking inaction must still identify the relevant action that was omitted. Labeling a broad criticism of agency management a "failure to act" cannot supply the missing specificity. Id. at 62-64.

### 2. The action must be legally required

Section 706(1)'s reference to action unlawfully withheld carries forward limits associated with mandamus: the duty to act must be sufficiently specific and legally obligatory. The provision authorizing relief for unreasonable delay does not eliminate that requirement. As the Court explained in footnote 1, an agency cannot unreasonably delay an act it has no legal obligation to perform. Id. at 63-64 & n.1.

A required act may nevertheless leave discretion about substance. For example, when an agency must issue regulations by a specified deadline, a court may compel agency action without dictating the regulations' content when the statute leaves that content open. The relevant distinction is between discretion over what a required decision will say and freedom not to undertake the asserted act at all. Id. at 64-65.

### 3. General program supervision exceeds this remedy

The Court connected its reading to the prohibition on broad programmatic attacks described in Lujan v. National Wildlife Federation. Courts review particular agency action within the statutory framework; they do not take charge of administering whole programs. An order directing an agency to comply with every aspect of a broad statutory mission would require continuing judicial judgments about priorities, resources, and implementation. The Court understood Congress to have left those management responsibilities with the agency, subject to review through the legally available forms of action. Id. at 64-65.

That reasoning does not establish that all agency inaction is immune from review. It identifies the conditions for a particular action-forcing claim. A specific legally required act remains susceptible to compulsion; an allegation that overall management falls short must be connected to an appropriate reviewable action or another available legal route. Id. at 64-67.

### 4. The nonimpairment requirement does not prescribe SUWA's requested acts

Section 1782(c) required protection of wilderness suitability, but it did not specify the particular management methods SUWA wanted the Court to mandate. BLM could exercise judgment among means of preventing impairment. Treating the general objective as a judicial command to undertake whichever measures the plaintiffs requested would transfer that selection of means to the court. Id. at 65-67.

A broad compliance decree would also make enforcement of the decree depend on continued judicial evaluation of everyday land-management decisions. The Court rejected that form of supervision under § 706(1). The holding concerns the absence of a discrete required act in this claim, rather than the absence of any substantive limit on BLM's management. Id. at 66-67.

### 5. Plan conformity and mandatory implementation are different obligations

SUWA relied on FLPMA § 1732(a), which directs management in accordance with land-use plans. The Court accepted that a plan can constrain later agency decisions. A specific action inconsistent with a plan may present a challenge under § 706(2). It rejected the further inference that the conformity requirement converts every statement of planned future activity into an affirmative duty enforceable under § 706(1). Id. at 67-69.

The planning regulations reinforced this distinction. Plans guide and control future management and the development of more detailed plans; they ordinarily do not themselves constitute final implementation decisions requiring additional process. Agency review also treated plan approval differently from implementation: plans could be protested to the BLM director, while specific implementation decisions were subject to a different administrative appeal route. Those features supported the Court's understanding of plans as frameworks rather than comprehensive command lists. Id. at 69-70.

### 6. Context matters when reading a plan's future-tense commitments

The San Rafael example showed the scope of activity a comprehensive plan might project: additional work on vehicle routes, grazing, habitat, fire, recreation, cultural resources, watersheds, and other uses over several years. Treating each projected step as a mandatory judicially enforceable obligation could commit resources not yet appropriated and displace more pressing work. The Court read budget and priority qualifications into the Henry Mountains statements even though that plan did not explicitly state every such qualification. Id. at 70-71.

The Court also identified exceptions to a blanket rule against compulsion. A plan might reiterate a duty independently imposed by law, or its language might create a binding commitment. The particular Henry Mountains supervision-and-monitoring statements did neither. The Court consequently rejected compulsion for want of a legally binding duty and stopped before deciding discreteness. Id. at 71-72.

Footnote 5 preserved another distinction: a regulation specifically addressing monitoring might present a different claim. That regulatory basis was not the claim before the Court. The decision therefore does not resolve every possible monitoring duty under FLPMA or its regulations. Id. at 72 n.5.

### 7. NEPA supplementation requires a remaining major federal action

NEPA required an environmental impact statement for qualifying major federal action, and the applicable regulation required supplementation for significant new circumstances or information bearing on the action or its effects. Under Marsh v. Oregon Natural Resources Council, a hard look at new information could be necessary when major federal action remained underway. In Marsh, the dam construction project had not been completed. Id. at 72-73.

Here, the relevant action was approval of a land-use plan. Approval had occurred, so continued management under the plan did not make the approval itself an ongoing major action. The Court accordingly rejected the asserted supplementation duty based only on later information about vehicle use. It expressly recognized the need for additional NEPA analysis if the plan were amended or revised. The case's actual reasoning therefore depends on identifying the federal action and its stage of completion. Id. at 73.

## Separate opinions

There were no concurrences or dissents. Justice Scalia's opinion and the judgment were unanimous. The disagreement described in the procedural history belonged to the Tenth Circuit, not to a separate Supreme Court opinion. 542 U.S. at 57, 61-62.

## Study notes and limits

The two § 706(1) requirements should be applied separately. The nonimpairment claim sought enforcement of a broad objective without a discrete specified act. The surviving plan-monitoring claim failed because the plan did not impose a binding affirmative duty; the Court reserved whether that monitoring was discrete. The NEPA claim required an antecedent inquiry into whether a supplementation duty existed at all. Combining those analyses into one assertion that all three claims were merely nondiscrete would lose the grounds the Court actually used. Id. at 65-73.

The full opinion's mootness discussion prevents another overstatement. Some plan-preparation claims had become moot through agency action, while a continuing-monitoring demand remained for decision. The Court's treatment of the remaining claim should not be presented as a merits disposition of every originally requested measure. Id. at 67-68 & n.3.

The assigned excerpt emphasizes the two-part APA requirement and land-use planning. The full opinion adds the NEPA section and express reservations about particular regulatory duties. The casebook's later examples of agency inaction are editorial material, not additional holdings of Norton. Casebook pp. 1087-1091; 542 U.S. at 72 n.5, 72-73.

### Assigned editorial aftermath

The unnumbered discussion on casebook p. 1091 describes subsequent lower-court dismissals of challenges that did not identify a discrete agency action the defendant was required to perform. It cites Perch-Ahern’s 2005 article for that account. The editors then connect Norton with Ohio Forestry Association v. Sierra Club and describe the Forest Service’s 2005 planning rule and its 2006 categorical-exclusion changes. In their account, the Service characterized plans as strategic guidance for future project decisions and excluded plans, amendments, and revisions under its 2005 rule from NEPA requirements. The book cites the rule at 70 Fed. Reg. 1023, 1025 and the exclusion at 71 Fed. Reg. 75,481, 75,483, along with Blumm and Bosse’s 2007 critical discussion. These are the editors’ historical descriptions of later litigation and agency actions. They are not additional Norton holdings, and they do not establish the current validity or scope of those later rules.
