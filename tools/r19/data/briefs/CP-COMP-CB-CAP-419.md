# City of Monterey v. Del Monte Dunes at Monterey, Ltd.

**526 U.S. 687 (1999) — Supreme Court of the United States, May 24, 1999.** Civil Procedure. Assigned: CivPro PDF page 550, printed pages 1082–1083, Note 2. The Court **5–4 affirmed the Ninth Circuit**, sustaining submission of the particular §1983 takings claim to the jury. Justice Kennedy wrote the judgment and most controlling analysis, joined by Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justices Stevens, Scalia, and Thomas. Parts I and II were unanimous. Part IV-A-2 had only four votes because Scalia declined to join it and wrote separately. Justice Souter, joined by Justices O’Connor, Ginsburg, and Breyer, concurred in part and dissented in part. Report pages, rather than PDF page numbers, supply the pinpoints below.

## Facts: repeated efforts to satisfy changing conditions

Del Monte Dunes and its predecessor sought to develop a 37.6-acre oceanfront parcel in Monterey. It was zoned for multifamily housing and surrounded largely by industrial, commercial, residential, and transportation uses. A former oil facility had left debris and contaminated sand. Introduced ice plant threatened remaining vegetation, including buckwheat habitat for the endangered Smith’s Blue Butterfly. Surveys had found only one larva on the property, but habitat protection remained one of the city’s concerns. 526 U.S. at 694–695.

In 1981 the owners proposed 344 units, far below the zoning’s potential density. The planning commission denied that proposal and suggested 264 units; it then denied the revised proposal and suggested 224. After another rejection, the city council directed consideration of 190 units. The owners prepared four plans at that size. The commission rejected them, but the council approved one conditionally and issued an eighteen-month use permit. The owners revised the plan to provide substantial public open space, beach access, landscape areas, view corridors, and habitat restoration; only 5.1 acres were allocated to buildings and patios. The architectural review committee approved it, and the planning staff recommended approval as substantially satisfying the conditions. Id. at 695–697.

In January 1986 the commission nevertheless rejected the plan. The council later rejected it too, refusing both to specify further curative measures and to extend the permit. It cited access, environmental layout, and butterfly habitat concerns that the owners argued had been addressed through earlier city-directed changes. A sewer moratorium would impede starting again with a new proposal. After five years, five formal decisions, and nineteen site plans, the owners concluded that the city would not permit development under any circumstances. Id. at 697–698.

## Litigation and the questions presented

The owners brought a federal §1983 action alleging an uncompensated regulatory taking, equal-protection violations, and substantive-due-process violations. The district court initially dismissed for lack of ripeness. The Ninth Circuit reversed, finding the development denial sufficiently final and concluding that California had not provided a compensatory remedy for temporary regulatory takings at the relevant time. The Supreme Court later treated this as the case’s established procedural posture, rather than independently redesigning the ripeness inquiry. Id. at 698–699.

On remand, the district court sent the takings and equal-protection issues to a jury and reserved substantive due process to itself. The jury was instructed to find a taking if the owners were deprived of all economically viable use or if rejection of the final 190-unit plan did not substantially advance a legitimate public purpose. The instruction treated the city’s environmental, open-space, safety, and community-quality purposes as legitimate; the disputed question was the relationship between the final decision and those purposes. The city had proposed the instructions’ essential substance. Id. at 699–701.

The jury returned verdicts for the owners on takings and equal protection and awarded $1.45 million. The judge ruled for the city on substantive due process and denied posttrial motions. The Ninth Circuit affirmed on the takings ground without reaching equal protection. The city sought review of whether takings liability could be submitted to a jury, whether the analysis allowed improper reconsideration of land-use policy, and whether the appellate court had wrongly invoked Dolan’s rough-proportionality standard. Id. at 701–702.

## What the Court held—and what it did not reconsider

The unanimous Part II rejected extending Dolan’s rough-proportionality rule beyond the special context of development exactions to this challenge to denial of development. But the jury instructions did not use that rule, and the Ninth Circuit’s discussion of it was unnecessary to sustaining the verdict. Its presence in the appellate opinion therefore did not require reversal. Id. at 702–703.

The Court also rejected the characterization that the jury had been authorized to rewrite general zoning policy. The city had accepted the underlying instruction, and the Court declined amici’s suggestions to revisit the substantive takings precedents on which it was modeled. The owners conceded the legitimacy of the city’s regulatory purposes and challenged the final decision in the specific history of this application. They did not ask the jury to invalidate zoning generally, to disapprove every development condition, or to substitute its own preferred environmental policy. Id. at 703–707.

The jury right rested on the **Seventh Amendment**, not a statutory jury guarantee in §1983. The phrase “action at law” in that statute was insufficient to imply a statutory entitlement. The constitutional inquiry asked first whether the action was legal or analogous to an action at law in 1791, then which particular decisions historically or functionally belonged to a jury. Id. at 707–709.

Five Justices agreed that a §1983 action seeking legal relief sounds in tort and falls within the Amendment. The owners sought compensatory damages for denial of just compensation, a legal remedy. Their claim involved the then-established absence of an adequate state compensation remedy, rather than an ordinary government-initiated proceeding conceding liability and determining compensation. The majority’s analysis adopted Scalia’s account of §1983’s character as a tort-like remedial action. Id. at 709–711.

## The plurality’s additional analogy and Scalia’s concurrence

In Part IV-A-2, four Justices separately rejected the city’s proposed exception for takings claims. They distinguished ordinary condemnation, where the government recognizes the obligation to compensate, from this disputed claim that the government denied both compensation and an adequate forum. Historical tort and quasi-contract actions responding to uncompensated interference with property supplied an analogy to suits at law. The plurality answered objections that the government’s ability to take for public use made the interference categorically lawful: the constitutional duty to compensate was an essential condition. This extended historical comparison is **plurality reasoning**, not a five-Justice holding. Id. at 711–718.

Scalia preferred to focus on the §1983 cause of action itself rather than searching for a separate analogue for each underlying constitutional right. Drawing on the Court’s treatment of §1983 in other contexts, he viewed it as a distinctive tort-like vehicle for enforcing federal rights. A damages action through that vehicle implicates the Seventh Amendment even if an alternative inverse-condemnation remedy also exists. His first footnote preserves the importance of the remedy: seeking only equitable relief does not acquire a jury entitlement merely because the underlying action sounds in tort. Id. at 723–731 & 726–727 n.1.

Scalia also agreed that the jury need not decide everything. Whether the asserted government purposes were legitimate was a legal question, properly removed from the jury by the instruction. Whether this particular refusal furthered those purposes was a sufficiently factual issue in the unusually particularized circumstances. His second footnote expressly withheld an opinion on the substantive liability standard because the city had forfeited its objection. Id. at 731–732 & n.2.

## Allocation of issues and the holding’s limits

The majority found no exact historical counterpart to the liability questions, so it considered precedent and process. Earlier takings cases usually arose in settings where the Seventh Amendment did not apply, or had merely assumed rather than decided a jury’s role. Whether the property had lost all economically viable use was predominantly factual. The question linking this final permit denial to the stated purposes was mixed law and fact but, as framed here, essentially bound to the concrete record and appropriately submitted to the jury. Id. at 718–721.

The Court expressly did not decide the jury’s role in an ordinary inverse-condemnation suit or precisely allocate every issue in challenges to zoning ordinances, general policies, or regulation as applied. It did not definitively reformulate temporary-regulatory-taking elements. Its holding concerned this §1983 damages action, these accepted instructions, and the owners’ contention that the city had departed from its own policies and repeatedly changing requirements. Id. at 721–723. The judgment was **affirmed**, with no new trial or remand ordered by the majority. Id. at 723.

## Souter’s dissent and the assigned-source discrepancy

Souter agreed with Parts I and II and with rejecting a statutory jury right. But he regarded direct condemnation as the strongest historical analogue to inverse condemnation because both enforce the Fifth Amendment obligation to pay just compensation. Historically the former lacked a constitutional jury entitlement; changing which party initiated proceedings did not justify a different result. He disputed the plurality’s liability-versus-damages distinction, examined the inconsistent early property-remedy theories, and rejected a general §1983 analogy as weaker than the shared constitutional right and remedy. He also emphasized judges’ institutional competence in deferential review of government action. Id. at 733–755 & nn.1–13.

His last footnote proposed remand without necessarily retrying the facts: the district judge could treat the verdict as advisory and record independent findings consistent with it. That was the dissent’s proposed procedure, not the Court’s disposition. Id. at 755 n.14.

The assignment accurately describes three writings and the 5–4 result, but says that Scalia regarded §1983 itself as providing a jury. His original page 723 expressly denies a statutory jury guarantee and locates the right in the Seventh Amendment’s application to the §1983 action.
