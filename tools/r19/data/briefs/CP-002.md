# Tickle v. Barton
142 W. Va. 188, 95 S.E.2d 427 (1956) | Civil Procedure

## Facts and the contested service

Richard Tickle, an infant suing through a next friend, sought damages for injuries allegedly caused by a vehicle owned by Raymond Barton and driven by Barton's agent, Lawrence Coleman. The accident occurred on private property in McDowell County, West Virginia. Barton lived in Austinville, Virginia. The private-property location mattered because Tickle initially attempted service through the West Virginia Auditor under a statute directed to accidents involving nonresident motorists on public streets, roads, or highways. Barton challenged that service by a plea in abatement; the issue remained unresolved when the later service dispute arose. 95 S.E.2d at 428–429.

On December 5, 1955, one of Tickle's lawyers obtained alias process and delivered it to a deputy sheriff. According to Barton's amended plea, the lawyer then telephoned Barton at his Virginia home and purported to extend a special invitation on behalf of the sponsors of a banquet honoring a championship football team at War Junior High School. Barton's son had belonged to an earlier team at that school. The caller declined to identify himself despite Barton's request, had no connection with or authorization from the sponsors, and invited Barton solely to secure personal service in West Virginia. Barton alleged that he had neither known about nor planned to attend the banquet before this invitation, accepted it as genuine, and traveled to War on December 6. The deputy served him there that evening. These were pleaded allegations assumed true for the legal question, rather than factual findings against the lawyer. Id. at 429–430.

The plea also alleged a follow-up call to Barton's wife to confirm that he had departed, followed by the lawyer's denial that he had made either call or arranged the second. Those details supported the alleged plan, but the decisive causal claim was that the unauthorized invitation induced a trip Barton otherwise would not have made. His existing knowledge that Tickle had already sued him did not, under the majority's analysis, eliminate the alleged deception concerning this trip. Id. at 430, 433.

## Procedural posture and issue

Barton challenged the alias service through an amended plea in abatement. Tickle demurred, testing the plea's legal sufficiency. The Circuit Court overruled the demurrer and, on both sides' application, certified that ruling to the Supreme Court of Appeals. The appellate question was whether the well-pleaded material allegations, accepted as true on demurrer, were sufficient to invalidate service induced by the plaintiff's lawyer's trickery. Neither the negligence claim nor the truth of the lawyer's alleged conduct was being tried on this certified review. Id. at 429–431.

## Rule, reasoning, and holding

The court adopted the generally recognized rule that a party cannot secure personal service on an otherwise out-of-reach nonresident by fraud, trickery, artifice, or a wrongful device inducing the person to enter the court's territorial jurisdiction. The rule extends to conduct by the party's lawyer, agent, or another person acting on the party's behalf. On proof of such inducement, the service will be vacated and set aside. Id. at 431–433.

The majority's authorities supplied an important explanation for this result. Ordinary personal service might otherwise place the person within the court's power, but a court should refuse to exercise jurisdiction in aid of a party who procured that service unlawfully. Fraud in obtaining process compromises the integrity of the court's own proceedings. The opinion also describes the resulting service as invalid and insufficient to justify exercising jurisdiction. Those formulations should be read together: the case does not hold that physical presence can never support jurisdiction over a nonresident, but denies judicial assistance to the particular wrongful means used to create the opportunity for service. Id. at 431–432.

Applied to the accepted allegations, the lawyer's concealment was more than a failure to warn Barton that service was possible. He claimed sponsorship authority he lacked, hid his identity and purpose, and allegedly induced a new trip for the sole purpose of service. The plaintiff was legally responsible for his lawyer's conduct. The plea therefore alleged a legally sufficient basis for setting aside the alias service. Id. at 433.

The Supreme Court of Appeals affirmed the Circuit Court's ruling overruling the demurrer. It expressly preserved the plaintiff's opportunity to deny the plea through a proper replication and contest the allegations with competent evidence. The ruling did not conclusively establish fraud, finally quash service after a factual hearing, resolve the original Auditor service, dismiss the underlying injury claim, or decide liability. Id.

## Given's dissent, joined by Riley

Judge Given agreed with the legal rule but disagreed that the facts properly pleaded triggered it. He separated factual allegations from conclusions labeling the conduct enticing or deceitful. In his view, the lawyer had asked about and invited Barton to an actual social function, withheld his own name, and arranged service when Barton attended; the plea showed no false statement sufficient to constitute fraud. Barton already knew of the litigation and its connection to McDowell County. The later confirmation call could not have caused a decision already made. Id. at 433–434.

Given emphasized the requirement that fraud be specifically alleged and proved rather than presumed. Treatise qualifications distinguished fraudulent inducement into the jurisdiction from taking advantage of a voluntary entry, even where misinformation creates an opportunity for service after the decision to enter. He viewed the majority's precedents as supporting the general rule without supporting its application to these facts. Riley joined this dissent. The published judicial text identifies those two dissenters and Haymond as the majority author; it does not individually identify the other judges joining the majority. Id. at 434–435.

## Assigned scope and significance

The assigned casebook excerpt appears on PDF pages 46–48, printed pages 31–35, and includes both the majority and the Given/Riley dissent. The full majority and dissent, including their cited-authority discussions, were read before drafting; no judicial footnotes appear in this reproduction.

The central procedural lesson is to separate legal sufficiency from factual proof. Affirming that a plea alleges grounds for relief does not establish that its allegations happened. The jurisdiction lesson is equally precise: territorial presence obtained through a plaintiff's wrongful inducement does not entitle that plaintiff to the court's assistance.
