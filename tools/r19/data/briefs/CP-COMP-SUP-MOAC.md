# MOAC Mall Holdings LLC v. Transform Holdco LLC

**598 U.S. 288 (2023) • Supreme Court of the United States • April 19, 2023**

**Assigned source:** Civil Procedure Supplement, printed pp. 506–507 (PDF 342). Justice Jackson delivered the unanimous opinion; there were no separate opinions.

## Facts and procedural history

Sears filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in 2018 and administered its estate as debtor in possession. In 2019 it agreed to sell most assets to Transform Holdco. The approved sale included the right to designate assignees of certain Sears leases, rather than an immediate assignment of every lease. Transform later designated Sears's lease at the Minnesota Mall of America for assignment to a wholly owned subsidiary. MOAC, the landlord, objected that the proposed assignee lacked the adequate assurance of future performance required by § 365, including its special shopping-center protections. The Court treated Transform and the subsidiary collectively because the distinction was immaterial to this appeal. 292–293 & n.1.

The bankruptcy court rejected MOAC's objection and entered an Assignment Order. MOAC sought a stay because it feared that § 363(m) would obstruct appellate relief after assignment. That provision generally protects the validity of a sale or lease to a good-faith purchaser or lessee against the effects of an appellate reversal or modification of a covered authorization, unless the authorization and transaction were stayed pending appeal. The bankruptcy court denied a stay, reasoning that this Assignment Order did not fall within the provision and emphasizing Transform's representation that it would not invoke § 363(m). The lease was assigned. 291–294.

MOAC appealed to the district court, which initially accepted its adequate-assurance argument and vacated the relevant portion of the Assignment Order. Transform then sought rehearing, invoking § 363(m) despite its earlier representation. The district court considered the conduct an especially strong candidate for judicial estoppel, but circuit precedent required it to treat the provision as jurisdictional and therefore immune to that response. It dismissed the appeal, leaving the Assignment Order effective. The Second Circuit affirmed. The Supreme Court granted certiorari to resolve a circuit split. 294–295, 297–298.

## Issues and holdings

**Mootness:** Had the completed lease transfer made effectual appellate relief impossible? The Court rejected dismissal on that basis. MOAC sought ordinary appellate relief and retained a concrete interest; its position that legal relief remained available was not frivolous. Transform's contested arguments about the eventual availability of relief confused mootness with the merits. 295–297 & n.4.

**Statutory jurisdiction:** Is § 363(m) jurisdictional? **No.** It limits the effects of a successful appeal in prescribed circumstances, rather than clearly limiting adjudicatory power. 297–305.

The Court did not decide whether § 363(m) actually governed this Assignment Order, settle its other disputed questions of meaning and scope, or hold that Transform was estopped on remand. Those were distinct from the jurisdictional classification. 293 n.2, 305 n.10.

## Reasoning

A statutory direction may be mandatory and important without being jurisdictional. Jurisdictional rules concern judicial power and require dismissal even where waiver, forfeiture, or estoppel would otherwise be appropriate. Congress must clearly indicate that character. Conventional interpretation may reveal its intent without magic words, but a merely plausible or comparatively better jurisdictional reading does not satisfy the standard. Transform's belated invocation showed the practical stakes. 297–298.

Section 363(m)'s text assumes that courts can exercise judicial power over the relevant authorizations and expressly contemplates appellate reversal or modification. It then constrains the effect on certain completed transactions. Its protection has exceptions: a bad-faith purchaser, a qualifying stay, or action other than reversal or modification does not fit the same statutory constraint. These features support protection of acquired property interests within an otherwise valid exercise of jurisdiction. They do not clearly define the classes of disputes courts may hear. 299–300.

The provision is also separate from bankruptcy jurisdiction grants and lacks a clear tie to them. The Court contrasted § 305(c)'s express review language, while specifically declining to hold that § 305(c) itself was jurisdictional. Emphatic legislative directions did not cure the absence of a clear statement. 300–301 & nn.5–7.

Transform argued that traditional in rem principles supplied the missing jurisdictional character: once the property left the estate, the court lost control of the relevant res. The Court rejected that inference. It depended on contested background principles rather than the enacted text; at most it proposed a reason Congress might have chosen a jurisdictional limitation. Moreover, § 363(m) permits some effects on completed transactions involving bad faith or the statute's other exceptions. If that power derived from in rem jurisdiction, the jurisdictional grant was broader than Transform's supposed traditional limit; if it derived from another source, the theory was still more disconnected from this provision. Bankruptcy jurisdiction is not exclusively in rem. 301–303 & n.8.

Transform also invoked former Bankruptcy Rule 805 and older lower-court practice treating completed-sale appeals as unavailable. The Court found no adequate proof that the predecessor rule was technically jurisdictional. The cited decisions all preceded § 363(m)'s 1978 enactment and the Court's modern discipline in using jurisdictional terminology. Dismissal without reaching a sale challenge's merits did not itself establish the required jurisdictional character. 303–304 & n.9.

## Exact disposition

The Court **vacated the Second Circuit's judgment and remanded** for further proceedings consistent with its opinion. 304–305. It did not immediately restore MOAC's district-court victory, invalidate the lease assignment, or adjudicate adequate assurance itself. Its mootness ruling likewise did not guarantee that MOAC would ultimately obtain effective relief; it allowed the nonfrivolous dispute to proceed.

## Course significance and source checks

The assigned account uses MOAC to separate a constraint on appellate remedies from a constraint on appellate jurisdiction. A successful appeal can be within a court's power even where a statute limits its practical effect. Likewise, the nonjurisdictional classification permits consideration of appropriate party-conduct doctrines without itself deciding their application.
