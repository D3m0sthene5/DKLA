# NY Drilling, Inc. v. TJM, Inc.
573 F. Supp. 3d 854 (E.D.N.Y. 2021) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and the scope of the holding

Judge Eric Komitee decided this diversity action on November 30, 2021, granting TJM’s Rule 12(b)(6) motion against NY Drilling’s second amended complaint. The alleged equipment failures were assumed true for the motion, not established after a trial. Although the casebook extracts the implied-warranty disclaimer analysis, the complete fifteen-page order also rejects a damages count recast in opposition as an agency theory and an asserted third-party-beneficiary claim under the manufacturer’s warranty. The court dismissed the entire complaint and directed entry of judgment and closure of the case. Official opinion, slip pages 1–2, 12–15.

## The contracts and alleged failures

HD Engineering, based in Hong Kong, manufactured the rigs. TJM, a Pennsylvania distributor, allegedly had the exclusive right to distribute HDE rigs in the United States. NY Drilling, a Queens construction company, bought through TJM rather than directly from HDE. HDE and TJM had a one-year warranty agreement between manufacturer and distributor; it did not name NY Drilling. These relationships matter because the court distinguished TJM’s sale contracts with the purchaser from HDE’s separate warranty to its own buyer. Slip 2–3 & nn.1–2.

In July 2018 NY Drilling signed a document titled “Final Revised & Updated Quotation” for an HD110-4T rig at $482,137. Immediately below its representative Wayne Fried’s signature block, an all-capitals paragraph excluded express and implied warranties and specifically named merchantability and fitness for a particular purpose. The paragraph also made ordering assent to its terms, barred superseding terms, and required signed writing for modification. According to the complaint, the rig’s front collapsed shortly after delivery, TJM supplied parts and repaired it, and it failed again a few months later. TJM then replaced it with an HD200A under a separate November 28 contract containing the same disclaimer in the same location. Slip 3–4.

The opinion’s replacement sentence contains an inconsistent first-model designation. The initial contract and preceding narrative identify HD110-4T; that clerical variation does not establish that a third rig was involved. Footnote 3 further warns that the amount of additional money actually paid for the replacement was unclear: its contract listed $568,614 but described payment as Key Bank financing. The brief therefore does not aggregate both quoted prices as established cash expenditure. Slip 4 & n.3.

The HD200A allegedly malfunctioned repeatedly, and TJM made at least two repairs. In July 2019 NY Drilling reported a failing mast section that could cause structural failure. TJM collected the machine at the end of August and allegedly had not repaired or returned it by September 2021. NY Drilling sued in July 2020 and twice amended its complaint in response to identified deficiencies. The operative pleading alleged merchantability and fitness breaches; a related count requested $93,000 in repair and replacement costs. A fourth count claimed beneficiary rights under the HDE–TJM warranty and requested $369,155, including asserted out-of-pocket and financing payments. Those sums were demands, not amounts adjudicated or awarded. Slip 4–5.

## Whether the written disclaimers were conspicuous

Under New York UCC §2-316(2), excluding merchantability by writing requires mentioning merchantability and making the disclaimer conspicuous. An exclusion of fitness must be written and conspicuous. The applicable §1-201(b)(10) definition asks whether presentation is such that a reasonable person against whom the term operates ought to notice it; the court decides conspicuousness. Here the words named both warranties explicitly, leaving presentation as the disputed question. Slip 6–7; casebook 621–622.

The location counted against enforcement. A reader ordinarily should understand the agreement by the signature stage, yet these disclaimers followed the signature blocks. They were grouped with material apparently outside the contracts, including a Facebook invitation. No language above the signature directed the reader to the later paragraph. The court gave those disadvantages meaningful weight instead of announcing that capital letters alone always decide the issue. Slip 8–9; casebook 622.

Other features ultimately overcame the placement problem: the text was entirely capitalized, clear about the warranties excluded, separated by blank space, and presented as a discrete paragraph with font and type size differing from nearby text. Although several payment terms and headings were more prominent and the disclaimer resembled the size of much of the contract, the relevant overall presentation sufficiently drew attention. New York authority directly addressing below-signature placement was sparse. The court considered the Justice Court decision in Ireland and persuasive out-of-state decisions applying substantially equivalent definitions, including Vision Graphics and Fleming Farms. It concluded that the statutory standard did not impose an absolute rule requiring a disclaimer before the signature. Slip 7–11 & n.6.

The full notes add two qualifications absent from the abbreviated assigned passage. Footnote 4 observes that the New York legislature removed statutory examples of conspicuous formatting in 2014; the court inferred that one specific form of emphasis was not required. The note prints inconsistent section-number references, so this account uses the provision identified correctly in the main analysis rather than treating the inconsistent references as separate applicable statutes. Footnote 5 explains that the attached contracts initially looked faded, but the court ordered clearer versions and based its assessment on the clear documents produced. Thus the dismissal did not rest on treating a poor reproduction as proof of the original disclaimer’s readability. Slip 7–8 & nn.4–5.

Viewed as a whole, both sale contracts effectively excluded the implied warranties, requiring dismissal of Counts One and Two. Slip 11. The analysis resolved exclusion, rather than adjudicating whether the alleged mechanical defects proved a breach of an existing warranty. Nor did it substantively establish every prerequisite of a particular-purpose warranty, such as a purpose distinct from ordinary use; the introduction notes that the complaint did not explain the intended use of the rigs. Slip 1, 6–11.

## The additional claims omitted from the assignment

Count Three, as pleaded, sought additional damages resulting from the same implied-warranty breaches. In opposition, NY Drilling characterized it as a common-law contract claim based on TJM’s supposed agency for HDE and a duty to enforce HDE’s warranty. Those agency allegations did not appear in the operative complaint. The court dismissed the count as an alternative damages request dependent on the already-dismissed warranties; it did not decide after factfinding that every distributor relationship could never constitute agency. Slip 12.

Count Four asserted that NY Drilling could enforce the HDE–TJM warranty as a third-party beneficiary. The court supplied four reasons for dismissal. First, the pleading neither showed that only NY Drilling could recover for breach nor identified clear contractual intent permitting its enforcement. The warranty defined the buyer as the party contracting with HDE under the signed contract—TJM—and the complaint itself acknowledged that agreement’s parties. Second, even if NY Drilling were a beneficiary, the warranty promisor was HDE, which was not a party to this action. TJM’s receipt of the manufacturer’s guarantee did not make TJM the party furnishing that guarantee. Slip 12–14.

Third, the agreement specified Hong Kong law, and NY Drilling did not explain its alleged beneficiary status under that law or why another law should apply. Fourth, the agreement required Hong Kong arbitration. A nonsignatory claiming beneficiary rights under a contract is also bound by its arbitration clause. NY Drilling’s statement that arbitration abroad would be unreasonable supplied no legal answer to that clause. These were cumulative grounds for dismissing the asserted claim in this action, not an affirmative holding that the plaintiff possessed an enforceable warranty claim against HDE in another forum. Slip 14–15.

## Exact disposition and sources

The entire second amended complaint was dismissed, with directions to enter judgment and close the case. The order did not grant another opportunity to amend or award the requested damages. Slip 15.
