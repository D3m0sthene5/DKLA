# United Kingdom Ministry of Defence v. Trimble Navigation Limited
422 F.3d 165 (4th Cir. 2005) | Contracts

## Decision and its limited reach
On September 6, 2005, the Fourth Circuit unanimously reversed a dismissal for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. The Contract Disputes Act did not govern the United Kingdom Ministry of Defence's suit against a private supplier merely because the alleged beneficiary rights arose from federal procurement contracts. The Act's dispute procedures concern claims between the United States and its contractors, and neither side of this suit was asserting such a claim. The court expressly declined to decide whether the Ministry actually had enforceable third-party-beneficiary rights. Traxler wrote for Widener and Gregory; the disposition was reversal and remand for further proceedings, not a damages judgment or a merits victory on the breach claim. (165–167, 173 & n. 6.)

## Procurement arrangement and alleged defects
The Arms Export Control Act authorized the United States to procure military goods for cash sale to foreign governments. In that arrangement, the foreign government paid the United States in advance, the United States contracted with a supplier, and the supplier furnished goods to the foreign government. The United Kingdom and fourteen other NATO countries had agreed to a memorandum governing access to NAVSTAR GPS. That memorandum required acquisition of certain related equipment through the Foreign Military Sales Program and a Letter of Offer and Acceptance, whose general conditions were nonnegotiable. (167 & n. 2.)

In July 1998, the United States Department of Defense agreed to procure more than 2,000 auxiliary output chips made by approved supplier Trimble for the Ministry, on a nonprofit basis. DoD entered four procurement contracts with Trimble, and the parties agreed that those contracts were covered by the CDA. The Ministry nevertheless was not a contracting party to those supplier contracts. (167.)

Deliveries began in early 2000. According to the Ministry, about 95 percent of the initial chips failed military specifications and were returned for repair or replacement; it claimed that about 20 percent of the repaired or replaced chips remained defective. Continuing problems in 2000 and 2001 allegedly delayed a cruise-missile program and increased its cost. The Ministry requested United States assistance in recovering costs from Trimble. The Air Force investigated the claim and the proof necessary for possible causes of action. In January 2003, DoD released an Air Force legal opinion, concurred in by the Air Force's General Counsel, that did not recommend action against Trimble, while assuring continued monitoring of product quality. The appellate court was recounting the Ministry's allegations and the agency's response, rather than finding the defects or a recoverable damages amount after trial. (167–168.)

## Dismissal and the jurisdictional question
The Ministry sued Trimble for breach of the DoD–Trimble contracts, alleging that it was an intended beneficiary. It invoked diversity jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1332(a)(4), governing suits by foreign states against qualifying United States citizens when the amount exceeds $75,000. No party disputed the Ministry's qualifying governmental status or the amount threshold. (168 & n. 4.)

Trimble sought dismissal on three grounds: the CDA displaced district-court jurisdiction, the Ministry was not a third-party beneficiary, and the United States was an indispensable party that had not been joined. The district court reached only the first ground. It reasoned that rights derived from procurement contracts necessarily fell within the CDA scheme. The Ministry appealed, arguing that the scheme concerned contractor–United States disputes rather than a foreign beneficiary's claim against a private supplier. The Fourth Circuit reviewed that legal jurisdictional issue de novo. The United States, invited to participate as amicus with an opportunity for the parties to respond, supported Trimble's construction. (167 n. 1; 168 & nn. 3–4.)

## A covered contract does not make every related dispute a CDA dispute
The court separated the procurement contracts' coverage from the identities of claimants and defendants covered by the statutory procedures. Section 602 applied to the federal procurement contracts, but § 605 supplied procedures only for a contractor's claim against the government or the government's claim against a contractor. Section 601 defined a contractor as a party to a government contract other than the government. The Ministry, claiming as a beneficiary rather than a party, did not fit that definition. Its suit also sought relief from Trimble, not from the United States. (168–169 & n. 5.)

The remaining review provisions reinforced this reading. A contractor could appeal a contracting officer's decision to an agency board or bring an action in the Court of Federal Claims; the government had specified routes for appeals involving board decisions. Nothing established a procedure for this foreign beneficiary to proceed against the private supplier. Moreover, the Court of Federal Claims could enter the relevant monetary judgments against the United States, not against a private company. Trimble's reading would direct the Ministry to a forum incapable of granting its requested relief. Congress had not written language broad enough to extinguish otherwise available rights merely because they related to a procurement contract. Decisions concerning subcontractor suits against prime contractors supplied analogous support. (169–171.)

## Statutory purpose and derivative contract rights
Trimble and the United States argued that specialized procurement tribunals promoted consistent interpretation and possessed expertise absent in ordinary district courts. The court found the statutory language and structure unambiguous; legislative purpose could not enlarge their reach. It also examined the legislative history in the alternative and found a concern with inefficient, unfair procedures for claims by or against the United States, rather than a demonstrated effort to transfer every dispute between other parties. General references to district-court expertise did not create the missing statutory procedure. Ordinary contract principles were generally applicable, and the district court was competent to apply them. (171–172.)

A separate argument invoked the beneficiary principle that a third party cannot acquire greater rights than the contracting party through whom it claims. Because the United States was bound to CDA procedures, Trimble argued, the Ministry must be equally bound. The court answered that Congress determines federal subject-matter jurisdiction by statute. Rules limiting derivative substantive contract rights could matter on the merits but did not expand the CDA beyond its specified categories of parties. This distinguished contractual limitations on an alleged beneficiary's recovery from a statutory removal of the district court's authority to consider the claim. (172–173.)

## Matters reserved and exact disposition
Trimble alternatively asked the appellate court to affirm because the Ministry was not an intended beneficiary. Both parties agreed that intent to benefit the third party was necessary, but disputed the complete test. Intent required a fact-sensitive examination of the contract and its formation circumstances, best undertaken by the district court first. The court therefore declined to resolve beneficiary status. Note 6 particularly preserved the possible relevance of the United States' deliberate retention of control over sales of sensitive military articles. It expressed no opinion on the ultimate viability of the contract claims. The district court's unresolved indispensable-party ground likewise was not adjudicated by this jurisdictional disposition. (168 n. 3; 173 & n. 6.)

The judgment was reversed and remanded. The assigned problem at printed page 1174 asks whether the Ministry may act as a beneficiary when the Air Force will not sue. This cited 2005 decision establishes that the CDA does not itself close the district-court forum; it does not establish the Ministry's status, prove defective performance, or award recovery. Converting that procedural holding into a conclusion that every recipient of a federal procurement contract may enforce it would omit the issue the court expressly reserved.
