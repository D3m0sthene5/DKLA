# Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc. v. New York State Department of Environmental Conservation

**834 F.2d 60 (2d Cir. 1987). Decided December 1, 1987. No. 79, Docket 87-6124.** Judge Newman wrote for a panel also including Judges Van Graafeiland and Meskill. The American Petroleum Institute (API) was the appellant from denial of intervention. No separate opinion or judicial footnote appears. Assigned account: Civil Procedure casebook, physical PDF page 412, printed page 792, the developed NRDC account within Note 1 on adequate representation.

## Underlying suit and proposed intervention

NRDC and several lung-health organizations brought a Clean Air Act citizen suit against New York’s environmental agency and commissioner and the federal Environmental Protection Agency and administrator. Under § 304(a), they sought to compel New York to implement four ozone-control strategies in its State Implementation Plan (SIP), and to compel EPA to require revisions to New York’s ozone and carbon-monoxide strategies. One strategy, Stage II, required equipment capturing gasoline vapors released while filling automobile tanks at gasoline stations. **834 F.2d at 61.**

API’s members owned or supplied approximately 3,500 New York City gasoline stations. API sought intervention to contest Stage II’s efficacy and advance defenses the existing state and federal defendants were not asserting. For example, it wanted to argue that a SIP commitment was not an emission standard or limitation enforceable against the State through this citizen-suit provision. District Judge Morris Lasker denied intervention, and API appealed. The appeal did not decide whether Stage II equipment worked, whether New York had violated the Act, or whether the requested SIP revisions were ultimately required. **Id. at 61.**

## Issue and API’s argument

The question was whether API’s economic interests were inadequately represented by the governmental defendants for purposes of intervention as of right under Rule 24(a)(2). API relied on NYPIRG v. Regents, where the Second Circuit had allowed a pharmacists’ trade association to intervene in defense of a ban on prescription-price advertising. API argued that its economic interest likewise differed from New York’s governmental interest. **Id. at 61–62.**

## The actual issues in the suit mattered

The district judge correctly distinguished attacking Stage II’s efficacy from litigating its implementation. The decision to adopt that strategy already had been made through SIP approval. A challenge to whether Stage II was a permissible compliance strategy could have been raised through statutory review of EPA’s approval under § 307(b). This suit instead concerned whether New York had unlawfully delayed implementation and owed a nondiscretionary duty to act, together with EPA’s asserted nondiscretionary duty to require strategy revisions. **Id. at 61.**

That distinction limited the relevance of API’s proposed contribution. Intervention could not be justified merely by identifying an industry concern about a policy when the pending action concerned enforcement of a previously approved policy. The opinion did not resolve API’s proposed citizen-suit defense on its merits; it considered whether the asserted interest showed a representation deficit in the litigation actually before the court.

## Holding and adequacy reasoning

The court affirmed denial of intervention. A different motive for resisting the plaintiffs’ suit did not, by itself, show inadequate representation. If an existing party was sufficiently motivated to defend vigorously and present all colorable contentions, the district court could conclude that the proposed intervenor’s interests were adequately represented. **Id. at 61–62.**

NYPIRG illustrated what was missing here. The pharmacists there did not simply have an economic reason for supporting a law that the State supported for another reason. They possessed an interest tied to an additional substantive justification: protecting independent pharmacies from destructive advertising competition. That justification differed from the Regents’ principal consumer-health justification, and the Regents acknowledged potentially different interests. The association could present the economic justification more vigorously than the governmental defender. **Id. at 62.**

API did not make a comparable showing connecting its economic interest to colorable defenses that the state and federal defendants were less able to assert. Its desire to press contentions those defendants apparently considered unavailing did not establish the required connection between its interest and an inadequately presented defense. The court thus distinguished a genuinely different litigation contribution from disagreement about whether a particular argument was worthwhile. It did not hold that every argument omitted by existing counsel becomes colorable or that governmental representation is always sufficient. **Id. at 62.**

The combined legal resources of the United States and New York also weighed against finding an abuse of discretion. The opinion expressly recognized that this was not a governmental parens patriae suit, even while drawing guidance from Hooker Chemicals. That qualification matters: the result was not obtained simply by labeling the State a representative of every affected citizen. It rested on the particular defense, asserted interests, and failure to demonstrate a meaningful representational gap here. **Id. at 62.**

## Permissive intervention and disposition

The court separately concluded that the district judge had not exceeded his broad discretion in denying permissive intervention under Rule 24(b). It gave that question only a short discussion rather than a separate developed test. The **district court’s order denying intervention was affirmed**. API did not become a party through this appeal, and the underlying citizen-suit merits remained distinct from the intervention ruling. **Id. at 62.**

## Assigned significance

The assigned account quotes the distinction between an economic motive and an inadequately represented interest, then asks whether the result is consistent with Smuck. The full opinion shows why the court thought motive alone insufficient: API needed to connect its economic stake to a colorable defense the governmental parties would be less able to present. It also supplies the distinction between an efficacy challenge to an already approved strategy and the pending implementation suit. The neighboring Trbovich account presents a different statutory and representational setting; this opinion does not purport to overrule that Supreme Court decision or establish an absolute barrier to private intervention alongside government.
