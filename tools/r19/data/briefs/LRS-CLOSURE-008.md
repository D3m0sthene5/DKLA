# Hamdi v. Rumsfeld
542 U.S. 507 (June 28, 2004) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Detention, the contested facts, and the habeas litigation

Yaser Esam Hamdi was born in Louisiana and moved to Saudi Arabia as a child. In 2001 he was in Afghanistan, where Northern Alliance forces seized him and eventually transferred him to the United States military. The government initially held him in Afghanistan, then at Guantanamo Bay. On learning that he was an American citizen, authorities moved him in April 2002 to a naval brig in Norfolk, later transferring him to Charleston. The government classified him as an enemy combatant and claimed authority to hold him without criminal charges or ordinary judicial proceedings. His father disputed that account, asserting that Hamdi had gone to Afghanistan for relief work and became trapped there when the military campaign began. Those competing accounts had not been adjudicated as established facts. (Plurality 510–511.)

Hamdi’s father petitioned as next friend under 28 U.S.C. § 2241 in June 2002. The district court appointed counsel and ordered access, but the Fourth Circuit reversed that access order and instructed the court to begin with cautious, deferential procedures. The government then supplied Michael Mobbs’s declaration as its evidentiary support. Mobbs, a Defense Department policy adviser reporting from records and reports, asserted that Hamdi affiliated with a Taliban unit, received weapons training, remained with that unit after September 11, and surrendered a rifle when the unit surrendered to Northern Alliance forces. He described screening teams and an interview as supporting the enemy-combatant classification. Hamdi had not been able to speak through counsel to contest those assertions. (512–513, 526.)

The district court considered the declaration generic hearsay insufficient to support the detention and ordered extensive material for in camera review, including interview records, capture information, interrogator identities, and officials responsible for the classification. It certified whether the declaration alone permitted meaningful judicial review. The Fourth Circuit reversed and ordered dismissal, reasoning that capture in an active foreign combat zone was undisputed and that separation of powers barred further factual inquiry. It also treated the Authorization for Use of Military Force as authority for detention. The Supreme Court vacated that judgment and remanded. It neither found Hamdi an enemy combatant nor ordered his immediate release. (513–516, 539, 553–554.)

## The fractured vote and the narrow authorization question

Justice O’Connor announced the judgment and wrote a four-Justice plurality joined by Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justices Kennedy and Breyer. Justice Souter, joined by Justice Ginsburg, disagreed that the existing detention was statutorily justified but concurred in the judgment subject to express qualifications. Justice Scalia, joined by Justice Stevens, dissented because a different, stronger remedy was required. Justice Thomas dissented because he would affirm. Thus six Justices supported vacatur and remand, but there was no six-Justice adoption of the plurality’s entire detention or procedural rationale. (509, 539–554, 554–555, 599.)

The September 2001 AUMF authorized necessary and appropriate force against those responsible for or harboring those responsible for the attacks. The plurality confined its question to the government’s alleged category here: a person part of or supporting hostile forces in Afghanistan who engaged in armed conflict against the United States. It did not supply a comprehensive definition of enemy combatant; note 1 left category boundaries for later cases. Detaining such combatants to prevent return to battle was a fundamental incident of war included in Congress’s authorization, even without the word detention. Citizenship did not categorically exempt an otherwise qualifying combatant, as the plurality read Quirin. (516–519, 522–524 & n.1.)

The plurality did not reach an independent Article II detention power. Nor did it decide whether 18 U.S.C. § 4001(a), the Non-Detention Act, applied to military detention or whether explicit congressional authorization was constitutionally required. Assuming those predicates, it concluded that the AUMF supplied the requisite authorization for the narrow category. This must not become a holding that § 4001(a) was wholly inapplicable or that the President could detain citizens contrary to Congress. (516–517.)

Duration mattered. The plurality tied detention to relevant active hostilities, not a perpetual global war or interrogation alone. It recognized that an unconventional conflict might eventually differ so substantially from traditional law-of-war circumstances that the authorization analysis would unravel. As of June 2004, however, active Taliban combat in Afghanistan continued. The plurality distinguished Milligan’s civilian circumstances and read Quirin as the more applicable precedent for wartime enemy combatants, while disputing Scalia’s proposed distinction between conceded and proved combatant status. Justice Thomas also accepted congressional authority to detain, although he rejected the plurality’s limiting analysis in important respects. (519–524; Thomas 587–589.)

## The plurality’s process requirement

All parties agreed that habeas remained available to a person detained within the United States absent suspension, and that Congress had not suspended it here. The habeas statutes contemplated denial of the return’s factual assertions and presentation of evidence; they did not require one unvarying trial format. The government nevertheless asserted either near-total judicial deference or a some-evidence review based solely on its own account. The plurality rejected the claimed factual concession: residence in Afghanistan did not concede battlefield capture, hostile-force affiliation, or armed participation, particularly when Hamdi had not been permitted to speak. (525–528.)

Applying Mathews, the plurality weighed the core liberty interest in freedom from physical detention against preventing actual combatants from returning to battle and the military burdens of greater process. It evaluated liberty from the perspective of the erroneously detained person, rather than assuming the contested allegation true. Mistaken detention of aid workers, journalists, or others remained a serious possibility. Conversely, demanding a full criminal-trial model, intrusive operational discovery, or distant litigation by combat personnel could impose genuine burdens. Neither the government’s minimal model nor the district court’s apparent trial-like model struck the balance. (528–533.)

At the minimum, a citizen challenging this classification had to receive notice of its factual basis and a fair opportunity to rebut the government before a neutral decisionmaker. The plurality allowed that hearsay might be necessary as the most reliable available evidence and that credible government evidence could carry a rebuttable presumption shifting an evidentiary burden, provided a fair rebuttal opportunity remained. Those accommodations were possible means of satisfying the requirement, not a finding that this particular declaration already proved detention lawful. Initial battlefield captures did not have to receive this process; the requirement concerned continued detention. Treaty-based access to a status tribunal was reserved in note 2 because the constitutional ruling sufficed. (533–535 & n.2.)

Separation of powers did not require courts to leave the individual factual basis wholly unexamined. The plurality treated habeas review as a check on executive detention, while preserving substantial military discretion concerning conduct of war. A some-evidence standard ordinarily reviewed a record developed after adversarial process; it was not an adequate substitute for any opportunity to challenge the facts. Interrogation by a captor was not neutral adjudication. A properly authorized and constituted military tribunal might satisfy the articulated standards; absent adequate prior process, the habeas court had to ensure them itself. Hamdi unquestionably had a right to counsel for remand proceedings. Because he had received counsel access since certiorari, the plurality did not resolve all questions concerning immediate counsel access at the beginning of detention. (535–539.)

## Souter and Ginsburg: clear statutory authority and qualified concurrence

Souter read § 4001(a) robustly to require clear congressional authority for citizen detention. Its enactment replaced emergency-detention legislation against the background of Japanese-American internment, and its language was deliberately broadened beyond detention governed by Title 18. Endo’s clear-statement reasoning and the need for congressional participation in balancing security against liberty reinforced that interpretation. Statutory location in the criminal code did not confine the prohibition to ordinary prisons. (539–547 & nn.1–2.)

Neither the AUMF’s general force language nor authority to spend money caring for prisoners adequately justified the detention as presented. Note 3 distinguished authority to spend when prisoners existed from authority to imprison. Souter acknowledged a potentially qualifying argument that authorized warfare included customary combatant detention, but the government had not shown conformity with the laws of war: it invoked the Geneva Convention while denying prisoner-of-war treatment and had not used the status-determination procedures contemplated by military regulations. He did not finally determine every treaty violation. The later PATRIOT Act’s strict limits on detention of aliens further undermined an inference of unrestricted citizen detention. Any genuine emergency necessity would at least expire with the emergency and did not explain more than two years of confinement. (547–553 & nn.3–4.)

Souter would ordinarily vacate and remand for proceedings addressing that lack of authority, without deciding the plurality’s due-process questions. Because that disposition lacked a majority, he joined the plurality’s remand to secure an opportunity for Hamdi to contest combatant status. He agreed that notice, fair rebuttal before neutrality, and counsel were minimum protections, but expressly declined to imply agreement with an evidentiary presumption or with military adjudication obviating or curtailing judicial habeas inquiry. This qualified concurrence supplies two votes for the judgment without converting those disputed procedural details into six-vote propositions. (553–554.)

## Scalia and Stevens: prosecution or lawful suspension

Scalia began from the tradition that a citizen accused of waging war against the country was prosecuted for treason or another offense, with Congress able to authorize suspension when the Constitution’s conditions allowed. The executive could not avoid criminal safeguards merely by calling its restraint preventive rather than punitive. His extended treatment of the Petition of Right, the 1679 Habeas Corpus Act, founding-era materials, the War of 1812 cases, and Milligan supported a choice between ordinary criminal process and suspension, rather than an improvised partial substitute. (554–569 & n.1.)

He criticized Quirin’s treatment of Milligan but also distinguished its conceded enemy-force membership from Hamdi’s contested status. Notes 2–4 elaborated that distinction and separated military detention immunity cases from authority to hold a citizen without prosecution. The AUMF was not a suspension and, in his view, did not clearly authorize this citizen detention. Even an ordinary statute expressly permitting it could not replace the constitutional suspension mechanism. (569–575 & nn.2–5.)

Scalia rejected borrowing benefits-case balancing to invent a reduced process for this liberty deprivation and rejected using habeas remand to cure omitted executive process. He would require a habeas decree directing release unless criminal proceedings promptly began or Congress suspended the writ. His analysis concerned citizens held within the territorial jurisdiction of a federal court; he reserved possible differences for citizens captured and held abroad and accepted lawful criminal pretrial detention. Whether the attacks qualified as an invasion warranting suspension was for Congress, not this case’s judicial balancing. (573–579 & n.6.)

## Thomas: deference to wartime executive determinations

Thomas would affirm because the government possessed wartime detention authority and, in his view, courts lacked the competence to second-guess the enemy-combatant determination. His structural analysis emphasized executive unity, secrecy, speed, and political accountability, while recognizing Congress’s essential role and judicial competence to decide the legal existence of asserted authority. He distinguished that legal question from factual combatant status, which he considered committed to the political branches. He accepted AUMF authorization, reserving the need to decide inherent Article II power, and disputed the plurality’s limits concerning cessation of hostilities and treaty reliance. (579–589 & nn.1–2.)

He read Moyer, Luther, and related authorities as allowing precautionary detention through executive process, distinguished Milligan’s punishment and unfulfilled statutory procedures, and rejected Scalia’s criminal-process-or-suspension dichotomy. Suspension removed a remedy and did not itself make otherwise unconstitutional detention lawful; its prerequisites might also be absent in an emergency. Thomas believed the detention afforded the process due in these circumstances. (589–594 & nn.3–4.)

Alternatively, he considered the plurality’s Mathews balancing defective: it undervalued intelligence gathering, classified information, military litigation burdens, and the courts’ own lack of information. Analogies to targeting and other military operations tested the proposed line between military necessities and judicially required process, rather than deciding the constitutionality of those operations. Notes 5–7 addressed the parties’ failure to invoke Mathews, his view of war-law conformity, and the potential security cost of counsel access. He did not join the remand or its minimum procedural requirement. (594–599 & nn.5–7.)

## Exact disposition and the assigned account

The Court vacated the Fourth Circuit’s dismissal and remanded for further proceedings. Six Justices supported that judgment; eight rejected the government’s requested disposition through importantly different approaches, while Thomas alone would affirm. The plurality’s four-vote rationale authorized narrowly defined wartime detention subject to process; Souter and Ginsburg withheld agreement on authorization and on specific procedural accommodations. No finding that Hamdi actually fought with the Taliban, no blanket approval of indefinite interrogation detention, and no immediate release order followed from this judgment. (539, 553–554, 599.)
