# In re Apple iPhone Antitrust Litigation

**846 F.3d 313 (9th Cir. 2017) — January 12, 2017 — Civil Procedure**

Judge William A. Fletcher wrote for the panel of Judges A. Wallace Tashima, William A. Fletcher, and Robert W. Gettleman, a district judge sitting by designation. There is no separate opinion. ## Allegations and four complaints

The plaintiffs alleged that they bought iPhones and apps between 2007 and 2013 and that Apple monopolized, or attempted to monopolize, the iPhone app market. Apple introduced the iPhone in 2007 and the App Store in 2008. According to the complaint, Apple controlled which apps could run on its closed system, required developers to sell iPhone apps through the App Store, and threatened to cut off developers who used other channels. It also discouraged users from downloading unapproved apps by threatening their warranties. When a user bought a third-party app, Apple collected payment, retained thirty percent, and sent seventy percent to the developer. These were allegations accepted for purposes of a Rule 12(b)(6) decision, not findings that Apple had violated antitrust law. (Slip 3–4.)

The procedural sequence explains why Apple's eventual defense appeared in a fourth dismissal motion. Four plaintiffs filed the first complaint on December 29, 2011. Counts I and II concerned Apple's app monopoly; Count III alleged an Apple–AT&T Mobility conspiracy involving iPhone voice and data services. Plaintiffs alleged iPhone purchases but no app purchases or attempted purchases. Apple's first motion asserted failure to join AT&T under Rule 12(b)(7), but consolidation with another action mooted that motion. (5.)

Seven plaintiffs then filed a consolidated complaint on March 21, 2012, again without app-purchase allegations or AT&T as a defendant. Apple again sought dismissal for nonjoinder and alternatively challenged Count III under Rule 12(b)(6). The district court dismissed the whole complaint without prejudice, directed plaintiffs to add AT&T or abandon Count III, and declined to decide the conspiracy merits without AT&T. Plaintiffs' September 28 amended complaint instead marked Count III as preserved for appeal. Apple moved to strike that count and, for the first time, challenged Counts I and II under both Rule 12(b)(1), for lack of Article III standing, and Rule 12(b)(6), under Illinois Brick's antitrust direct-purchaser rule. Plaintiffs invoked Rule 12(g)(2) because Apple had omitted the latter defense from earlier motions. (5–6.)

The court struck Count III and dismissed the remaining counts with leave to amend because plaintiffs had not alleged buying or attempting to buy apps. Without that alleged Article III injury, it declined to decide the Illinois Brick issue as an advisory matter. The September 5, 2013 fourth complaint finally alleged app purchases and contained only the app-monopoly counts. Apple promptly renewed the Illinois Brick objection. This time the district court reached it and dismissed with prejudice, reasoning that plaintiffs were indirect purchasers. They appealed. (6–7.)

## Rule 12: preserving a defense is different from using another pre-answer motion

The panel reviewed Rule 12 interpretation and the dismissal de novo. Rule 12(g)(2) generally bars another Rule 12 motion raising an available defense omitted from an earlier motion. Its consequences depend on the defense. Under Rule 12(h)(1), omission can entirely waive personal jurisdiction, venue, process, or service defenses under Rules 12(b)(2)–(5). A failure-to-state-a-claim defense is preserved, but Rule 12(h)(2) channels it into a Rule 7 pleading, a post-answer Rule 12(c) motion, or trial. Preserving that defense therefore does not itself authorize an otherwise prohibited successive pre-answer Rule 12(b)(6) motion. The panel rejected the Seventh Circuit's contrary interpretation and distinguished a subject-matter jurisdiction objection, which could be raised at any time. (7–9, 12.)

The panel nevertheless agreed with the Third and Tenth Circuits' forgiving approach to appellate review of an improperly renewed merits motion. Rule 1's concern with just, speedy, inexpensive resolution mattered: forcing an answer, another substantially equivalent motion, and repeated briefing could impose delay without changing the question or standard. This reasoning did not erase Rule 12(g)(2)'s text or equate all omitted defenses. It explained why a reviewing court could find a particular procedural error harmless. (9–12.)

Here, the panel did not conclusively decide that Apple's fourth motion was untimely. Its earlier statutory-standing motion might instead have been premature, because the complaint then lacked sufficient Article III allegations and the district court properly refused an advisory ruling. Assuming the fourth motion was late, any error remained harmless. Apple had promptly challenged each complaint, without an apparent strategically abusive purpose. Its delayed Article III objection caused no identified harm, although an earlier objection might have prompted earlier amendment. Requiring one of Rule 12(h)(2)'s alternative procedural vehicles would have delayed resolution of the same Illinois Brick question for no apparent purpose. Plaintiffs therefore could not obtain reversal merely because the district court had considered the defense in that motion. (12–14.)

## The direct-purchaser question

The dismissal still failed on the merits of the defense. The panel explained the Hanover Shoe–Illinois Brick framework: a direct purchaser may recover the alleged overcharge without proving that it did not pass the cost onward, while an indirect purchaser generally cannot recover a passed-on overcharge from the upstream seller. Tracing the effects of successive price changes is difficult and risks duplicate liability or dilution of the direct purchaser's incentive to enforce antitrust law. UtiliCorp retained that structure even where regulated utilities might pass through all their costs. The question here was where the plaintiffs stood in the actual distribution chain relative to Apple, the defendant they sued. (14–18.)

Apple characterized itself as selling distribution services to developers, rather than selling apps to users, and compared its App Store to a shopping mall leasing space to stores. The panel rejected that characterization as a reason to deny these purchasers standing. Developers did not have their own stores in the alleged arrangement; Apple required sales through its own App Store. Whether developers might separately be direct purchasers of Apple's distribution services was expressly not decided and did not resolve users' status. (20–21.)

The Ninth Circuit's Delaware Valley decision supported a functional analysis. There, a buyer purchasing products from a distributor was direct as to that distributor and indirect as to the manufacturer, even though an agreement involving the buyer helped determine the wholesale price. Here Apple performed the distributor's role. The panel disagreed with the Eighth Circuit's Ticketmaster majority, which treated an antecedent transaction as making consumers indirect even though they bought through the defendant distributor. (18–20.)

Three transactional details did not alter the result. Collecting payment alone was not the basis for standing, because changing electronic payment arrangements should not determine who distributes the product. Calling Apple's compensation a commission rather than a markup was also immaterial. Finally, developers' role in setting the app price did not make users their direct purchasers; Delaware Valley had already shown that price-setting arrangements do not displace the distribution relationship. Apple was the distributor selling directly to these users, so they could sue Apple for the alleged monopoly. (21–22.)

## Judgment and assigned significance

The panel reversed the dismissal and remanded for further proceedings. It held that any error in considering the fourth Rule 12(b)(6) motion was harmless and that plaintiffs were direct purchasers with statutory standing under Illinois Brick. It did not determine whether Apple actually monopolized the market, award damages, or certify the proposed class. (23.)
