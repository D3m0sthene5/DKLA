# Smith v. Kansas City Title & Trust Company

**Citation and court:** 255 U.S. 180 (1921), Supreme Court of the United States, No. 199, decided February 28, 1921. Justice Day delivered the Court's opinion. Justice Holmes dissented, joined by Justice McReynolds; Justice Brandeis did not participate. The decision was 6–2 among participating Justices. Holmes and McReynolds expressed no judgment on the constitutional merits because they considered federal jurisdiction absent.

**Assigned reading:** CivPro, physical page 193, printed page 340, the complete Smith account in Note 5, plus the discussion of Smith within the Merrell Dow account on physical page 194, printed pages 341–342. Both original scans and the entire native context were read. The complete official report was read, including Day's opinion at 195–213 and Holmes's dissent at 213–215. The preceding reporter syllabus and counsel arguments are distinguished from judicial reasoning. There are no judicial footnotes or appendices; the note at 195 identifies counsel at the earlier hearing.

**Facts and relief sought.** A shareholder of the Missouri trust company sought to prevent its directors from using corporate funds to buy farm-loan bonds. The directors proposed to invest $10,000 in bonds of each of two kinds of institutions created under the Federal Farm Loan Act of 1916, as amended in 1918: Federal Land Banks and Joint Stock Land Banks. The shareholder maintained that Congress lacked constitutional authority to establish these institutions and exempt their securities from taxation. If that premise was right, their bonds would be unauthorized securities that the company could not lawfully purchase. The asserted corporate duty supplied the shareholder's claim; federal constitutional validity supplied the decisive proposed reason for enjoining the investment. 255 U.S. at 195–199.

The case did not concern an investment in ordinary Treasury debt. The challenged bonds were issued by the statutory land banks and backed by deposited farm mortgages and notes. Federal Land Banks made loans through cooperative associations of borrowers, while Joint Stock Land Banks had private capital. The bill described substantial federal stock ownership and bond purchases, but alleged that the Treasury had not generally used the banks as public-money depositaries. Three Federal Land Banks had, however, served as federal financial agents in making seed-grain loans to drought-stricken farmers in 1918. These allegations supported the dispute over whether the system was a federal fiscal instrument or essentially a private mortgage-lending enterprise. Id. at 196–198.

**Procedure and the parties' positions.** The Federal Land Bank of Wichita and First Joint Stock Land Bank of Chicago intervened as defendants. The trust company moved to dismiss in the nature of a general demurrer; the district court dismissed the bill, and the shareholder appealed directly to the Supreme Court. No party objected to original or appellate federal jurisdiction, but the Court examined it first. Diversity was lacking, so original jurisdiction required a case arising under the Constitution or federal laws under § 24 of the Judicial Code. Id. at 198–199.

On the merits, the shareholder attacked both incorporation and tax immunity. The principal objection was that private, long-term farm lending did not carry out the indispensable federal fiscal functions associated with the national banking system. Merely allowing Treasury to designate these corporations as depositaries or agents, the argument ran, could not constitutionally transform their private operations into federal instrumentalities. The defenders relied on Congress's power to create appropriate fiscal agencies, the banks' authorized public functions, and the power to protect the resulting securities against taxation that might defeat those functions. The Court addressed these arguments rather than treating the jurisdictional holding as an automatic victory for the shareholder. Id. at 208–213.

**Jurisdictional issue and holding.** Could a shareholder's state-law demand to restrain an unauthorized corporate investment arise under federal law when its asserted basis depended on the constitutionality of a federal statute? The Court held that it could. A substantial federal question appearing in the plaintiff's own statement of the claim could support jurisdiction even though federal law did not itself create the shareholder's corporate cause of action. The asserted federal claim had to rest on a reasonable foundation and be more than merely colorable. Id. at 199–202.

**Why federal jurisdiction existed.** The proposed investment was challenged solely because the federal legislation authorizing the bonds was allegedly unconstitutional. The directors relied on that legislation as valid authority; the shareholder said its invalidity made their investment unlawful. Thus the constitutional issue was directly presented and necessary to the requested relief. It was not a possible defense injected into an otherwise independent dispute. One construction of federal law would sustain the investment against the stated objection; the opposite construction would undermine it. Id. at 199–201.

Day invoked earlier descriptions of cases whose correct decision depended upon federal constitutional or statutory construction and the shareholder proceedings in Pollock and Brushaber. He concluded that the allegations concerning the shareholder's interest, threatened misuse of corporate funds, and constitutional objection supported district-court jurisdiction and authorized direct Supreme Court review. The Court's power to hear the controversy therefore did not turn on whether the constitutional challenge would ultimately succeed. Id. at 199–202.

**The statutory scheme.** Before deciding the merits, the Court described the Treasury-based Federal Farm Loan Bureau and supervisory Board, twelve districts, capitalization and organization, borrower associations, farm mortgages, bond collateral, and the different privately capitalized Joint Stock Land Banks. Banks could be designated government depositaries and financial agents and could buy United States bonds. The statute exempted specified institutions, mortgages, bonds, and associated income from taxation, while expressly preserving certain real-property and Joint Stock Land Bank share taxation. The Court was reviewing this particular statutory arrangement, not recognizing unlimited immunity for everything connected with a federally chartered institution. Id. at 202–209.

**Constitutional merits and reasoning.** The Court upheld Congress's creation of the institutions. McCulloch and Osborn established that Congress could charter banks as appropriate means of exercising its enumerated fiscal and other national powers, despite substantial private ownership and private banking activity. Congress's discretion to choose suitable instrumentalities was capable of responding to changing national needs. Designating the land banks as potential public depositaries and financial agents, requiring reasonable service in those capacities, and authorizing government-bond transactions brought them within that established authority. Id. at 208–211.

The limited actual use of these functions did not negate the power to authorize them. Nor would the Court inquire into an alleged legislative pretext once Congress acted within its constitutional authority. Restricted banking powers, the lack of some ordinary banking activities, and the additional purpose of cheaper farm lending did not make the institutions unconstitutional. Congress could give a valid fiscal instrument additional private-business powers without destroying its public character. Id. at 209–211.

Because the institutions were valid federal instrumentalities, Congress could exempt the securities from federal and state taxation. Taxing their bonds could obstruct the means of obtaining funds needed for their operations. The Court drew on the supremacy and tax-immunity reasoning of McCulloch and related precedents. Whether Congress had chosen wise agricultural or fiscal policy was outside the constitutional question the Court was deciding. Id. at 211–213.

**Holmes's dissent.** Holmes would have denied federal jurisdiction. In his account, Missouri law created the shareholder's right to prevent a corporate investment contrary to the directors' duties. Missouri could have authorized the investment outright, leaving no such claim, or could incorporate a federal constitutional standard into its own restrictions. Either way, federal law became relevant only because Missouri law made it so. Incorporating another body of law as a criterion did not make that body of law the source of the action. Id. at 213–214.

Holmes accepted, for argument, that federal law might create only part of a cause of action. But it had to contribute by its own force: the suit, rather than simply a question within it, had to arise under federal law. He relied on American Well Works and distinguished the majority's use of Brushaber. McReynolds joined this dissent, and both declined to reach the validity of the land-bank legislation. Their position therefore cannot be described as a dissent holding that Congress lacked power to create the banks. Id. at 214–215.

**Exact disposition and significance.** The Court **affirmed the decree dismissing the shareholder's bill**. Jurisdiction existed, but the constitutional objection failed; the proposed investment was not prohibited for the reason asserted. Id. at 213. The assignment presents the case as an important counterpoint to Holmes's creation test. Its later Merrell Dow discussion emphasizes the nature of the federal issue, distinguishing a challenge to an important federal statute from use of a federal safety standard within state tort law. That is later assigned context, not a test announced in Smith in 1921. The assignment's reference to § 1331 is its modern framing; the actual decision applied Judicial Code § 24. This brief verifies the historical decision and assigned accounts, without claiming a comprehensive current-law or later-history check.
