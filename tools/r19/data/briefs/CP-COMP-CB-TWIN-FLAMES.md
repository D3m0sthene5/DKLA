# Twin Flames Universe.com, Inc. v. Cole

**Civil Procedure · CP-COMP-CB-TWIN-FLAMES**  
**528 F. Supp. 3d 708 (E.D. Mich. 2021)** · March 23, 2021 · District Judge Gershwin A. Drain  
**No. 20-11660, ECF No. 18**  
**Assigned:** CivPro.pdf, physical PDF page 127, printed pages 203–204, Internet-jurisdiction account; and physical page 230, printed page 417, Note 8 on dismissal versus transfer.

## Concrete facts and chronology

Jeffrey and Shaleia Ayan founded Twin Flamesuniverse.com (TFU), a Michigan corporation principally operating in Sutton Bay. Their enterprises offered spiritual guidance, relationship instruction, online courses, coaching, and a religious organization. They described their programs as promoting self-love and helping participants connect with their “Twin Flame.” Their complaint described an Internet business with roughly 10,000 members and hundreds of hours of course materials. These descriptions explain the setting; the court did not adjudicate whether the programs' claims were true. Official court opinion, pp. 1–3.

The plaintiffs sued four nonresident critics for defamation, interference with contractual relations, and civil conspiracy. The critics were former participants or relatives of current participants and lived in Texas, Arizona, Florida, and Belgium. Only Arizona resident Louise Cole's jurisdictional motion was decided in this opinion. Cole was the mother of a current TFU member. In 2019 she posted comments on a public TFU-related subreddit, calling the organization a cult and a multilevel marketing enterprise and describing her concern that her daughter had become alienated from her family. Other challenged online statements accused the plaintiffs of manipulation, exploitation, and additional misconduct. Those assertions were the allegedly defamatory statements and plaintiffs' allegations, not judicial findings that such misconduct occurred. Id. at 1–5.

A Vancouver-based reporter interviewed Cole for a February 5, 2020 Vice Media article. Cole described her daughter's earlier work abroad, her mistaken belief that the daughter was taking Chinese-medicine classes, payments to the organization, and the later loss of family communication. Other interviewees described different experiences and concerns. The reporter characterized the Ayans as Michigan YouTubers, and unidentified parents said they contacted Michigan police. Those particular statements were not attributed to Cole. She reposted the article on social media. Id. at 5–8.

The plaintiffs then sent a March 2020 cease-and-desist letter demanding retractions and deletion of criticism. Among its threats, the letter expressly said defendants would have to attend Michigan proceedings and pay travel, hotel, food, court, and lawyer expenses despite living elsewhere. A March 11 follow-up article discussed that letter. Cole declined to retract and commented that members' favorable videos sounded scripted. Plaintiffs alleged that at least four people canceled TFU contracts after publication. Cole moved under Rule 12(b)(2); the judge heard argument on March 3, 2021. Id. at 2, 8–9.

## Parties' arguments and governing test

Cole argued that comments made from her Arizona home to a Canadian journalist and a worldwide online audience were not conduct directed at Michigan. Plaintiffs argued that the allegedly harmful events and effects occurred in their district, that Cole knew their Michigan connections, and that she deliberately aimed her statements at the forum. They suggested that the Vancouver publication was chosen to reach nearby American consumers, including southeastern Michigan, because plaintiffs had conducted Toronto workshops. If jurisdiction failed, they requested transfer to Arizona rather than dismissal. Id. at 5–6, 11, 21.

Plaintiffs bore the jurisdictional burden. On an affidavit-based motion, a prima facie showing sufficed, but they had to offer reasonably particular facts rather than simply rest on the complaint. The court construed the presented facts favorably to plaintiffs and did not credit Cole's contradictory factual account against them at this stage. It treated Michigan's long-arm reach as coextensive with constitutional due process and applied the circuit's specific-jurisdiction requirements: purposeful availment or consequences deliberately caused in the forum, a claim arising from forum activities, and a sufficiently substantial connection to make jurisdiction reasonable. Id. at 9–13.

The opinion's sole judicial footnote, on court page 13, explains that Michigan's general-jurisdiction provision for individuals was inapplicable. The disposition therefore concerns specific jurisdiction over Cole, not a finding about corporate home-state jurisdiction or every defendant in the case.

## Holding and inferential reasoning

**Knowledge of the plaintiffs' location was insufficient.** *Calder* permitted jurisdiction where the allegedly defamatory publication had a developed forum focus, including local sources and substantial circulation. *Walden* requires the defendant's own meaningful connection with the forum, rather than a connection supplied exclusively by the plaintiff's residence or resulting injury. The district judge also relied on the Sixth Circuit's recent *Blessing* decision concerning social-media criticism of Kentucky students. Calls to identify or shame people with forum connections had not, by themselves, shown communications directed specifically to that state's audience. Id. at 13–16.

**The Canadian interview supplied no Michigan targeting.** Plaintiffs offered no evidence about Vice Media's Michigan circulation or intended Michigan audience. Their affidavit did not fill that gap, and they did not explain why choosing a Vancouver reporter rather than a southeastern Michigan outlet demonstrated forum targeting. The article's description of the Ayans as Michigan residents was the reporter's statement; the reference to Michigan police was made by unnamed parents, whereas Cole identified herself. The judge could not combine other speakers' forum references and attribute them to Cole. Michigan was not the focal point of her interview. Id. at 16–17.

**The public posts concerned the business rather than a Michigan audience.** The subreddit was open to TFU followers and interested readers generally. Neither its comment board nor Cole's challenged comments identified Michigan, TFU's place of incorporation, or its headquarters. Referring to the Ayans by initials did not establish a geographic target. Plaintiffs did not allege Michigan social-media followers or evidence that Cole hoped to reach Michigan specifically, as opposed to readers anywhere. A complaint about an Internet business headquartered in the forum is not necessarily a communication aimed at the forum's public. Id. at 17–19.

**Local readers and local economic effects could not supply Cole's contacts.** Plaintiffs said Michigan members read the material and canceled contracts. But third-party readers' locations and choices could not establish that Cole created forum contacts. The record supplied no preexisting Michigan relationship, direct forum transaction, or communication intentionally sent to plaintiffs or their customers in Michigan. Cases involving such additional relationships were therefore distinguishable. General accessibility did not make Cole subject to suit wherever a reader or the subject of a post happened to live. Id. at 18–19.

**The knowledge dispute did not change the result.** Cole asserted that she learned plaintiffs lived in Michigan only from the first Vice article. The court expressly declined to resolve that conflicting assertion against plaintiffs on this motion. Even assuming the knowledge they alleged, they still needed something more connecting Cole's conduct with Michigan. Foreseeable reputational or economic injury at plaintiffs' chosen location was insufficient. Plaintiffs failed their prima facie showing of minimum contacts; the court did not reach an independent merits determination of defamation or conspiracy. Id. at 19–20.

## Transfer was separately denied

The judge recognized authority to transfer a case despite missing personal jurisdiction when the interests of justice support it, discussing §§ 1631 and 1406(a). Ordinarily transfer may protect a claimant from losing a merits determination solely because of a forum mistake. The court nevertheless found apparent bad faith here: the cease-and-desist letter explicitly threatened defendants with the expense of litigation far from their homes, supporting the inference that the improper forum was chosen to increase Cole's costs. The litigation was still in its infancy, plaintiffs identified no prejudice from dismissal, and they suggested no limitations bar to filing properly. Transfer to Arizona was therefore declined. Id. at 21–22.

A source detail should be preserved accurately: the request and legal discussion identify § 1406(a), while the application paragraph on court page 22 refers to § 1404(a). The opinion does not explain this change. Its operative conclusion is clear—no interest-of-justice transfer—and the brief does not silently convert that paragraph into a holding under a different statutory citation. The assigned transfer account quotes the general discretion and bad-faith exception; the complete opinion supplies the letter and absence of limitations prejudice that explain this case's actual result.

## Exact disposition and relationship to the assignment

The court **granted Cole's Rule 12(b)(2) motion** and declined transfer. This order decides the claims against the moving defendant, not the merits or all other defendants' jurisdiction. District Judge Drain was the sole judicial author; there are no separate opinions or appendices. The certificate of service following his signature is administrative material, not an additional holding. Id. at 22.
