# Alibri v. Detroit Wayne County Stadium Authority
470 Mich. 895, 683 N.W.2d 147 (2004) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and disposition
On July 20, 2004, Michigan’s Supreme Court reversed the Court of Appeals and reinstated the Wayne Circuit Court’s July 11, 2000 order granting partial summary disposition for Freda Alibri and dismissing the remaining claims. The court had previously granted leave to appeal and considered briefs and oral argument. Its short order relied on a somewhat different basis from the circuit court. No individually authored opinion, separate writing, voting division, or monetary award is specified in the complete reproduced order. (470 Mich. at 895.)

## Assigned factual setting
The assigned note supplies a fuller transactional narrative than the Supreme Court’s two-paragraph order. It describes a memorandum of understanding involving Detroit’s Downtown Development Authority, Wayne County, and the Detroit Lions and Tigers for a downtown stadium project. Negotiations with Alibri began in 1996 because her property was represented as necessary stadium parking. The authority threatened acquisition through eminent domain if necessary. The assignment states that she conveyed the property in January 1997 for $264,551.94 and that the stadium project and parking requirements were later downsized. Those date, price, and negotiation details come from the complete assigned account; the Supreme Court order does not separately recite them. They should not be presented as a damages award or as an adjudication of the legality of eminent-domain proceedings. (Assigned printed 1089–1090.)

## Issue and holding
Could the property transaction be rescinded on the basis of an inaccurate representation of the project’s need for Alibri’s property, although knowing falsity was not indicated? The court held that innocent misrepresentation justified rescission. It identified the representation that all west-side properties, including Alibri’s, were needed for project parking and the subsequent determination that this was incorrect. It expressly noted the absence of any indication that the defendant knew the representation was inaccurate. Lack of demonstrated knowledge therefore did not defeat this rescission ground. (470 Mich. at 895.)

## Reasoning and limits
The order cited United States Fidelity & Guaranty v. Black generally for innocent misrepresentation. It did not rest its decision on proof of deliberate deceit, and it did not say that every later change in a development plan automatically establishes an actionable false representation. The relevant facts were the particular representation of need and its established inaccuracy. The text does not independently set out a full elements test, detailed reliance findings, a risk-allocation clause, or a damages measure. Those omissions should remain omissions rather than be supplied from assumptions about general mistake doctrine. (Id.)

The case illustrates the assignment’s relationship between mistake and misrepresentation: one party’s mistaken factual assertion can induce the other’s transaction even without fraudulent knowledge. The remedy identified by this court was rescission on that ground, not an award of tort damages for intentional fraud. The exact operative action was reinstatement of the specified circuit-court order, including its dismissal of remaining claims. The Supreme Court did not itself describe the mechanics of reconveyance or repayment, so the assigned sale price should not be converted into a new repayment decree stated by this appellate order. (Id.; assigned printed 1089–1090.)
