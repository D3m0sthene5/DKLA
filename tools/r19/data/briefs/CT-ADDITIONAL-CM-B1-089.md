# Wood v. Lucy, Lady Duff-Gordon — Appellate Division
177 A.D. 624 (1st Dep't 1917) | Contracts

## Agreement and alleged breach
Otis Wood sued Lucy, Lady Duff-Gordon on a written fashion-marketing agreement reproduced in his amended complaint. The agreement recited her position as a creator of fashion in America, England, and France and the monetary value of endorsements carrying her name. It also described Wood's organization as suited to placing endorsements she approved. Wood received the exclusive right to place endorsements he considered advantageous and those approved by Duff-Gordon or her adviser, and the exclusive right to sell or license manufacture and sale of certain articles she created. (177 A.D. at 625.)

Wood would collect profits and revenues from endorsements, sales, and licenses. Duff-Gordon would receive half, and he would retain the remainder after paying the costs of obtaining the revenues. He had to account monthly. He also would obtain patents, copyrights, and trademarks he considered necessary and take protective proceedings he considered appropriate, with the latter expenses shared equally. These express undertakings were the provisions the court tested for enforceability, rather than an unstated fixed compensation payable regardless of success. (Id. at 625–626.)

The complaint alleged that Duff-Gordon placed endorsements herself for consideration without Wood's consent, defeating his exclusive right and depriving him of collection and his revenue share. That was an allegation supporting his breach claim, not a trial finding that she had wrongfully retained a particular proven amount. She argued that the writing lacked mutuality and consideration because Wood had not promised the essential revenue-producing activity. (Id. at 625–626.)

## The pleading decision under review
New York Special Term denied Duff-Gordon's motion for judgment on the pleadings, which consisted of Wood's amended complaint and her demurrer. The order was entered January6,1917. She appealed. On April20,1917, the Appellate Division, First Department, resolved whether the complaint stated a sufficient cause of action on the writing. Justice Davis wrote; Presiding Justice Clarke and Justices Laughlin, Dowling, and Shearn concurred. The complete report contains no separate writings or judicial footnotes. (Id. at 624–625,627.)

## Express downstream obligations did not require upstream activity
The court distinguished collecting and distributing revenue from undertaking to create transactions producing it. Wood's promise to collect and account would bind him if he placed an endorsement, made a sale, or granted a license. But the court found no provision obligating him to obtain endorsements, make sales, or grant licenses in the first place. Duff-Gordon could not compel those preliminary acts. His downstream promises consequently depended on an act he had not agreed to perform. (Id. at 626.)

The patent and protective-proceeding language did not solve the problem. Those promises concerned endorsements he was under no duty to place, and their performance was left to his judgment. The court concluded that all of his dependent obligations required revenue-generating acts the writing did not require him to undertake. In its reading, no provision could be enforced against him if he simply did nothing. It drew support from Commercial Wood & Cement's proposition that a plaintiff who had obligated itself to do nothing could never breach. The cited decision was an authority within Davis's reasoning, not another adjudicated dispute in this appeal. (Id. at 626.)

Thus the court regarded Duff-Gordon as granting the exclusive opportunity while Wood supplied no binding reciprocal undertaking to exploit it. It held the contract void for want of mutuality and consideration. It did not determine how much effort a binding reasonable-efforts promise would require; its premise was that the agreement created no such enforceable obligation. (Id. at 626–627.)

## Exact judgment and distinct assigned stage
The Appellate Division reversed the order denying judgment on the pleadings, with ten dollars costs and disbursements, and granted Duff-Gordon's motion with ten dollars costs. This was a ruling against the sufficiency of the pleaded contract claim, not a damages calculation after trial. (Id. at 627.)
