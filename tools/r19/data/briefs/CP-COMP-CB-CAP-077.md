# Jones v. Flowers

**547 U.S. 220 (2006) — Supreme Court of the United States, April 26, 2006.** Chief Justice Roberts wrote for Justices Stevens, Souter, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Justice Thomas dissented, joined by Justices Scalia and Kennedy. Justice Alito did not participate. The Court reversed and remanded by a 5–3 vote. Assigned account: CivPro.pdf, physical pages 149–150, printed pages 248–250, Note 7 and its questions.

## What happened and how the case reached the Court

Gary Jones bought a house at 717 North Bryan Street in Little Rock in 1967. After separating from his wife in 1993, he moved to an apartment elsewhere in the city; his wife continued living in the house. His mortgage company paid the property taxes until he paid off his mortgage in 1997. Taxes subsequently went unpaid, the property was certified delinquent, and Arkansas's Commissioner of State Lands began the tax-sale process. Jones had not kept his address current with the tax collector as state law required. 547 U.S. at 223–224, 231–232.

In April 2000, Commissioner Mark Wilcox sent a certified letter to the house advising Jones of the delinquency and an impending public sale if he did not redeem the property. Nobody was home to sign or subsequently retrieved the letter; it returned marked “unclaimed.” The scheduled sale was approximately two years away. In 2002, the State published notice in a newspaper. No public-auction bids were received, but Linda Flowers subsequently made a private purchase offer. A second certified letter to Jones at the house also returned unclaimed. Flowers bought the property for $21,042.15, although the parties stipulated that its fair market value was $80,000. The postsale redemption period expired. Jones learned what had happened when his daughter received an unlawful-detainer notice from Flowers. Id. at 223–224, 230.

Jones sued Flowers and the Commissioner, contending that the notice procedure deprived him of property without due process. On cross-motions for summary judgment, the trial court ruled for the defendants; the Arkansas Supreme Court affirmed. It treated mailing notice in compliance with the statutory procedure as adequate without requiring actual receipt. The Supreme Court granted review to decide the narrower question whether the State's knowledge that its certified-mail attempt had failed required a practicable follow-up before the sale. Id. at 224–225.

## Issue, governing rule, and holding

When a certified tax-sale notice returns unclaimed before the sale, must the State undertake additional reasonable notice efforts? The Court held that it must do so **if practicable**. Arkansas had reasonable additional options here and could not proceed simply by ignoring the returned letters. The holding does not require actual receipt, does not make certified mail inherently unconstitutional, and does not impose a limitless search for the owner. Id. at 225–226, 234–239.

The governing standard was Mullane's requirement of notice reasonably calculated, under all the circumstances, to inform interested parties and allow objections. A procedure can be reasonable initially yet require reconsideration when the sender learns, before the deprivation, that it did not work. The inquiry asks what a person actually desirous of informing the owner would reasonably do with the information then available. Id. at 226–230, 238.

## Why the majority required another reasonable step

**The returned letters changed the relevant circumstances.** Ordinary mail may initially satisfy due process even without proof that the recipient read it. But the Commissioner received affirmative information that nobody had claimed these letters and still had time to respond before taking the property. Learning this shortly after the first mailing, approximately two years before the sale, differed from learning only afterward that a generally adequate procedure had failed. The majority's storm-drain example illustrated the point: someone watching his mailed notices fall into a drain would not reasonably regard the mailing itself as the end of his obligation. Id. at 227–230.

**Jones's own shortcomings did not discharge the State's constitutional duty.** Arkansas argued that owners know taxes are due and that Jones violated his address-update obligation. The majority answered that an owner can know taxes are owed without knowing that the State is preparing a particular sale. His negligence did not permit a taking without constitutionally adequate notice. Nor could the State automatically treat occupants as agents responsible for retrieving a certified letter addressed to an absent owner. The majority specifically said there was no record evidence that attempted-delivery slips were actually left at the house; Thomas pointed instead to postal rules requiring such slips. Those are different propositions, and the latter does not establish the former as a record fact. Id. at 231–234; id. at 241 n.1 (Thomas, J., dissenting).

**Several modest alternatives addressed the particular failure.** An unclaimed letter could mean that Jones remained at the house but was absent when delivery was attempted, or that he had moved. Regular mail could remain at the house without a recipient's signature and might be forwarded or brought to the owner's attention. Posting a notice or addressing mail to “occupant” could alert those still living there, whose own occupancy might be affected by a sale. Jones's daughter eventually did tell him after receiving the detainer notice, illustrating why notice to occupants could matter. These alternatives were opportunities to make a reasonable attempt, not findings that any one would certainly reach him. Id. at 234–235.

**The duty had practical limits.** The Court rejected Jones's proposal that the State search the telephone directory and government records for his new address. “Unclaimed” did not establish that the listed address was incorrect, and an open-ended search imposed substantially greater burdens than the identified follow-up options. Conversely, the Commissioner offered no estimate of returned letters or factual support for the asserted burden of finding tens of thousands of properties. Arkansas already required personal service for certain homesteads after returned certified mail and transferred notice costs to the taxpayer or purchaser. The Court used those features to evaluate the burden rather than to announce a universal personal-service requirement. Id. at 236–237.

Newspaper publication did not cure the defect because more adequate warning was reasonably possible. The Court also rejected the contention that its ruling would discourage certified mail: that method gives valuable delivery documentation, but also supplies information that the State cannot disregard when the letter returns. Id. at 237.

## Thomas's dissent and the disagreement about timing

Thomas would have affirmed. Certified mail to the owner-supplied address, supplemented by publication, was reasonably calculated to inform Jones when sent. Existing doctrine did not guarantee actual notice or require heroic efforts. In his view, judging the initial procedure by later information replaced an ex ante inquiry with a hindsight-driven obligation that might have no stopping point. Ownership and the statutory address-update duty supported expecting Jones to protect his own interests. Id. at 239–244.

Thomas distinguished cases in which officials already knew, before mailing, that the addressee was jailed or incompetent. Here, “unclaimed” could reflect refusal or failure to retrieve mail rather than an obsolete address; it did not tell the Commissioner how to fix the problem. He regarded regular mail as less traceable, posting as burdensome and potentially ineffective, and occupant-addressed mail as easily discarded. His footnotes address postal-delivery requirements, publication as a supplementary method, the owner's protective responsibilities, alternative postal labels, the storm-drain analogy's timing, and the uncertain justification for rejecting broader searches while requiring these narrower measures. Id. at 241–249 & nn.1–6.

The majority answered that its inquiry was not hindsight after a completed taking. The State learned of the failure while reasonable additional action remained possible. It distinguished Dusenbery because the prison had signed for that notice, whereas these letters returned to the sender. Both opinions accepted that actual receipt was unnecessary; they disagreed over what the State must do with notice-failure information before the deprivation. Id. at 238, 243–244.

## Judgment, limits, and assigned context

The Court **reversed the Arkansas Supreme Court and remanded for proceedings consistent with its opinion**. It found this notice effort inadequate; it did not itself enter a decree awarding title to Jones or prescribe a replacement notice statute. States retained choices among reasonable procedures. Id. at 238–239.

The assigned account accurately emphasizes the available alternatives, rejection of an open-ended address search, and Thomas's timing objection. Its questions about the significance of a home, whether Jones lived there, and whether this decision developed or merely applied Mullane invite analysis rather than add holdings. The majority stressed the extraordinary power of taking and selling a house, but did not condition due process on the owner residing there. Id. at 239; assigned physical pages 149–150. This brief addresses the historical decision; it does not certify subsequent notice doctrine or current Arkansas statutes.
