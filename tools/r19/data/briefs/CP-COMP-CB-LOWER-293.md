# Shutts v. Phillips Petroleum Co. — Kansas remand

**240 Kan. 764, 732 P.2d 1286 (1987) — February 25, 1987 — Civil Procedure**

Chief Justice Schroeder delivered the Kansas Supreme Court’s opinion. Justice Allegrucci did not participate; no dissent, concurrence, or numerical tally appears. On remand from the United States Supreme Court, the court examined the laws of five states and sustained recovery of interest on suspended gas royalties at the rates in Phillips’s federal undertaking. It nevertheless changed the post-judgment interest rules and starting date. The judgment was affirmed as modified and remanded for calculation and entry of judgment. This is the 1987 Kansas decision, distinct from the 1985 Supreme Court judgment on jurisdiction and choice of law. (765, 800–802.)

## Why the controversy returned to Kansas

Phillips collected tentative gas-price increases while awaiting Federal Power Commission approval, but withheld the associated royalty increases unless owners provided indemnity and security against possible refunds. After approval, it paid suspended royalties without interest despite having used the money in its business. The opinion recounts payments of $3.7 million in 1976, $4.7 million in 1977, and $2.9 million in 1978. Owners who furnished the required indemnity and received immediate payments were not material to this dispute. (798–799.)

Earlier litigation, Shutts I in 1977, involved 6,400 owners and interest from a different suspension period. The present action, brought by Irl Shutts and Robert and Betty Anderson for approximately 28,100 owners, produced Shutts II in 1984. Kansas then accepted jurisdiction over nonresident plaintiffs after mailed notice, representation, and an opportunity to opt out. It awarded interest under equitable principles, using Phillips’s FPC undertaking: seven percent before October 10, 1974; nine percent through September 30, 1979; and thereafter the average prime rate compounded quarterly. Kansas also imposed its fifteen-percent post-judgment rate. (765–767.)

In 1985 the United States Supreme Court upheld jurisdiction but rejected the Kansas rationale for applying forum law to every claim. Adequate class procedure, the forum’s convenience, and failure to opt out did not independently justify selecting Kansas substantive law. Nor did commingling royalty funds create a specific Kansas res. Constitutionally permissible selection required significant contacts creating state interests so that the choice was neither arbitrary nor fundamentally unfair. The Supreme Court identified putative conflicts but left their detailed examination and actual choice of law to the Kansas courts. (767–768.)

The Seward County district court found no conflict on liability and the applicable pre-judgment rate and adopted the prior Kansas result. Phillips appealed, asserting failure to obey the mandate, genuine differences among state laws, and error in the fifteen-percent post-judgment award. Although gas came from eleven states, Phillips briefed conflicts only for Texas, Oklahoma, Louisiana, New Mexico, and Wyoming; the court treated arguments about other jurisdictions as abandoned. (768–769, 798.)

## The state-law inquiry: compensation for the use of money

The court did not simply repeat a preference for forum law. It examined statutes and decisions in each contested jurisdiction, often predicting the answer where no state court had decided this precise royalty question. Its common premise was that Phillips’s role as stakeholder and actual use of money supported compensation under equitable principles of unjust enrichment. The specificity of those predictions matters: the opinion is a Kansas court’s application of other states’ law, not five newly issued judgments from those states. (769–800.)

For Texas, the court relied on Stahl and related decisions recognizing an equitable exception to the common-law restriction on interest awarded under that name. It rejected Phillips’s purchaser-versus-producer distinction because Phillips undertook royalty disbursement and still used the retained funds. A “without interest” provision could not defeat the federal obligations permeating the transaction. Using rather than reselling gas likewise did not avoid the lease’s royalty obligation. (769–775.)

Phillips also argued that merely offering indemnity agreements stopped the accrual of interest. The court distinguished an unconditional tender or actual relinquishment of funds from these conditional offers. Owners had to promise repayment with interest and obtain an acceptable irrevocable bank letter of credit. Unexecuted offers left Phillips with reasonably free use of the money. Although earlier Texas decisions used six-percent statutory interest, they had not resolved the effect of Phillips’s undertaking and the higher federal rates. (775–778.)

Oklahoma authorities similarly allowed compensation for another’s use of money and treated ascertainability by calculation as significant even where liability was disputed. The court predicted that Oklahoma would reject Phillips’s purchaser and gas-user objections and would not treat receipt of principal as necessarily extinguishing this interest obligation. The federal undertaking could be viewed as creating an implied contractual obligation rather than leaving the six-percent default rate controlling. (778–784.)

The Louisiana analysis distinguished royalty overpayments, withholding pending federal approval, and money actually deposited in court through interpleader. Decisions about those settings did not establish that Phillips could retain the benefit of using suspended royalties without compensation. The court treated the corporate undertaking as a third-party-beneficiary stipulation under Louisiana law and found equitable support for the federal rates, rather than the default statutory rate. It acknowledged confusing authorities and characterized Boutte’s discussion of interest after federal approval as dictum. (784–793.)

New Mexico allowed interest for loss of the use and earning power of money. Its contract principles permitted interest when the sum was fixed or calculable and, in other situations, allowed discretionary interest when justice required. The purchaser argument again failed. Wyoming also recognized the economic value of money and required liquidated or mathematically ascertainable sums. Its ordinary notice requirement was impractical here: Phillips itself computed and withheld royalties using information unavailable to the average owner. Equity did not require owners to tell Phillips the amount it already calculated. (793–797.)

## Why the federal rates remained the measure

After that analysis, the court concluded all five jurisdictions would impose liability and imply an agreement to compensate owners under Phillips’s undertaking. Phillips could never retain the suspended principal outright: federal approval determined whether it went to purchasers as refunds or to royalty owners. Its indemnity demands used the same federal interest framework when owners received money early and a refund later became necessary. Those facts supported symmetrical compensation when Phillips retained the money pending approval. (798–800.)

The opinion also describes the “United States rule,” adopted in Kansas and four of the five contested states, for crediting partial payments first toward interest and then principal, absent a contrary agreement or statute. Wyoming lacked authority on that point. The court did not announce that every state’s law is identical in all interest disputes; it found no outcome-changing conflict on these obligations under these circumstances. (798–800.)

## The distinct post-judgment ruling

Phillips prevailed on an important part of its final objection. The original trial judgment was entered May 20, 1983, and the new district judgment after remand was entered April 30, 1986. A mere appellate adjustment can preserve interest from the original judgment, but a full reversal wipes that judgment out. The Kansas court construed the Supreme Court’s choice-of-law reversal as vacating the original award of interest and therefore required post-judgment interest to begin April 30, 1986. Interest under the federal undertaking continued to that new judgment date. (800–801.)

The court also rejected automatic application of Kansas’s fifteen-percent post-judgment rate to leases in the five contested states. It directed Texas’s lesser of the implied-contract federal rate or eighteen percent; Oklahoma fifteen percent; Louisiana seven percent; New Mexico fifteen percent; and Wyoming ten percent. Kansas leases and all other jurisdictions retained fifteen percent. These are the historical rates specified in the opinion, not verified present rates. (801–802.)

The exact mandate affirmed the judgment as modified and remanded for calculation of each plaintiff’s interest and entry of judgment.
