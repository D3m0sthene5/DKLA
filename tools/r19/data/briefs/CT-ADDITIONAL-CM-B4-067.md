# Lloyd v. Murphy
25 Cal. 2d 48, 153 P.2d 47 (1944) | Contracts

## Court and procedural result
The California Supreme Court decided Lloyd on October 31, 1944. Justice Traynor wrote for the court; Chief Justice Gibson and Justices Shenk, Curtis, Edmonds, Carter, and Schauer concurred. The court affirmed a judgment declaring the lease, as modified by the landlords' waiver, still effective and requiring the tenant to pay unpaid rent with interest, less sums received through reletting. Rehearing was denied November 28. There was no dissent. (48, 50–52, 58.)

## Lease, wartime restrictions, and repudiation
On August 4, 1941, the landlords leased Murphy premises at Almont Drive and Wilshire Boulevard in Beverly Hills for five years beginning September 15. The specified purpose was displaying and selling new automobiles, servicing and repairing them, and selling a major oil company's petroleum products, with occasional used-car sales allowed. Other uses required written consent, as did assignment or subletting. The property had gasoline pumps, suitability for a service station, and a one-story storeroom adaptable to other commercial uses on a major traffic artery. (50–51.)

Federal restrictions arrived in January 1942. A January 1 order stopped new-car sales; a January 8 modification allowed sales to persons engaged in military activities; and a January 20 priorities system permitted sales to persons with qualifying preference ratings. Thus the initial ban did not remain an absolute prohibition of every new-car sale. (51.)

On March 10, Murphy explained the restrictions to a landlord authorized to act for the others. The landlords orally waived the use and subletting restrictions and offered a rent reduction if Murphy could not operate profitably. Murphy nevertheless vacated on March 15, gave oral notice repudiating the lease, and followed it with written notice on March 24. The landlords confirmed their waiver in writing March 26. After attempting to persuade him to perform, they relet under the lease to mitigate damages. They sued May 11 for declaratory relief and unpaid rent. (51.)

At trial, the court found that the waiver allowed any legitimate use and subleasing to any responsible party. Murphy continued selling and servicing cars at two other locations, including one at which he sold new cars exclusively. He acknowledged that other new-car dealers remained in business, but said this particular location could not succeed. His estimate that new cars accounted for ninety percent of his gross business and gasoline ten percent was testimony, not a trial finding. The trial court ruled that wartime conditions had not discharged his obligations. Murphy appealed on frustration of purpose. (51–52.)

## Rule: destruction of expected value differs from inability to perform
The court recognized that frustration could apply to a lease in a proper case. It did not adopt the categorical view that a lease's conveyance of an estate places every risk on the tenant and precludes frustration. California decisions had already applied the doctrine to leases. (52–53.)

Frustration differs from impossibility, including impossibility's broader recognition of extreme impracticability. In frustration, performance remains possible, but an unforeseen event destroys the expected value of the promised counterperformance to the party seeking discharge. The inquiry allocates the risk by examining the parties' relationship, contractual terms, and formation circumstances. The promisor must establish that the event's risk was not reasonably foreseeable and that the value of counterperformance was totally or nearly totally destroyed. Foreseeable or controllable events, and counterperformance that retains value, ordinarily defeat this defense. Where the risk was foreseeable but the contract did not address it, the court inferred its assumption. (53–55.)

The demanding threshold protects reliance on commercial arrangements. A government regulation that makes a business less profitable, more difficult, or more expensive does not by that fact excuse a contractual obligation. The court declined a looser substantial-frustration standard that would encourage tenants to repudiate whenever emergency regulations reduced profitability. (54–55, 57.)

## Why Murphy failed on foreseeability and remaining value
First, the restrictions were not a contingency wholly outside the parties' contemplation. The lease preceded the United States' entry into war, but national-defense mobilization was already under way. Statutory authority to allocate materials and mobilize industry had existed for more than a year; automobile production was converting to military and lend-lease needs; and buyers were purchasing cars in anticipation of restrictions. An experienced automobile dealer could foresee war's effects on production and sales. The absence of a provision protecting Murphy against those effects supported assigning that risk to him. (55–56.)

Second, the lease retained substantial usefulness. The rules restricted new-car sales rather than rendering them uniformly illegal, and Murphy himself conducted such sales elsewhere in the county. Gasoline could still be sold. Reduced profitability at this location did not demonstrate destruction of the lease's value. (56.)

The landlords' waiver supplied an additional reason. The original use and subletting limitations were protections reserved for their benefit, rather than a covenant guaranteeing Murphy a profitable automobile business. The landlords relinquished them instead of enforcing them. Their action made other lawful commercial uses and responsible subtenancies available in premises particularly suited to a range of businesses. Prompt reletting confirmed their continuing value. The proposed rent reduction and cooperative conduct also mattered to the court's assessment. The opinion does not say that an unspecified reduction was actually agreed upon or use a numerical reduced rent in calculating the judgment. (56–57.)

The court distinguished premises usable only for an activity made wholly unlawful, such as a lease restricted to a saloon after prohibition. It also discussed a saloon-and-cigar-store lease whose ancillary lawful uses prevented discharge. Here both the surviving uses under the original lease and the expanded uses under the waiver defeated the claim of nearly complete loss. (57–58.)

## Judgment and significance of the assigned accounts
The judgment was affirmed. Murphy remained bound by the lease as modified by the waiver, with rent and interest reduced by receipts from reletting. The opinion states no exact monetary award or monthly contractual rent for this case; the $650 monthly figure in its discussion belongs to Grace v. Croninger, an earlier authority. (52, 58.)

The assigned account at printed pages 1134–1135 uses Lloyd to distinguish frustration from impossibility. The second account, at printed page 1159, highlights the landlords' concessions and suggests that their choices may affect whether performance retains value. That is supported by the actual reasoning, but the court also relied independently on foreseeability and surviving permitted automobile activity. It did not impose a general obligation to renegotiate whenever circumstances change, and its affirmation should not be reduced to a holding that a landlord's proposed rent concession alone governs the tenant's obligation.
