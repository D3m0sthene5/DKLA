# Bondi v. VanDerStok

145 S. Ct. 857 (2025); No. 23-852, decided March 26, 2025

## Court, opinions, and assigned scope
Justice Gorsuch wrote the Supreme Court's seven-Justice opinion, joined by Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Sotomayor, Kagan, Kavanaugh, Barrett, and Jackson. Sotomayor, Kavanaugh, and Jackson each wrote a concurrence. Thomas and Alito each dissented. The Fifth Circuit's judgment was reversed and the case remanded.

The assigned eight-page Class 3 reading is expressly limited to the weapon-parts-kit issue. It includes Jackson's concurrence and portions of Thomas's dissent, but omits the majority's separate frame-and-receiver analysis, the Sotomayor and Kavanaugh concurrences, and Alito's dissent. Those materials are supplied here from the full opinion. Pinpoints identify the author and that opinion's separately numbered slip-opinion pages; they are not PDF-viewer page numbers.

## Parties and material facts
Jennifer VanDerStok and other individual and business challengers contested a 2022 rule promulgated by the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives. The federal government defended the rule through the Attorney General and other officials. The rule applied existing Gun Control Act licensing, recordkeeping, serialization, and background-check requirements to certain weapon-parts kits and unfinished frames or receivers. It did not simply prohibit every kit or every homemade firearm. Gorsuch, slip op. at 1–6.

The Act defines a firearm to include, under 18 U.S.C. §921(a)(3)(A), a weapon that will, is designed to, or may readily be converted to expel a projectile by explosive action. It expressly includes starter guns. Subsection (B) separately includes the frame or receiver of such a weapon. Other subsections address silencers, mufflers, and destructive devices. The distinction between a weapon and a component independently classified as a firearm is central to the litigation. Id. at 2, 4–5.

ATF's rule addressed kits that are designed to or may readily be completed or assembled into operating firearms. It also addressed partially complete, disassembled, or nonfunctional frames and receivers that can be completed or converted to function as those components. The agency identified limits and excluded objects not yet sufficiently identifiable as unfinished weapon components. The challengers argued that these regulatory definitions exceeded Congress's language. Id. at 3–6.

The record included Polymer80 products that the majority regarded as close enough to their intended final form to satisfy the Act. Evidence about the small amount of work needed to complete them supported the ready-conversion analysis. The photographs illustrated the relationship between the products sold and the resulting weapons or components. The legal significance was their identity and degree of completion, not a judicial instruction on how to manufacture them. Id. at 8–13, 17–19.

## Procedural history
The challengers filed a pre-enforcement APA action and described their challenge as facial. They sought to have the rule set aside for exceeding statutory authority. The Northern District of Texas granted summary judgment and vacated the rule. The Fifth Circuit largely affirmed, concluding that weapon-parts kits categorically were not weapons under subsection (A), and that subsection (B) reached only completed frames or receivers. Id. at 5–7.

The Supreme Court granted certiorari on both questions. It accepted, for this case, the parties' framing that the challengers had to show the regulatory provisions inconsistent with the statute on their face. The majority treated the government's formulation as unchallenged in the briefing. The dissents disputed both the appropriateness of that framework and the inference that the challengers had accepted it. Id. at 7 & n.2; Thomas, slip op. at 6–9; Alito, slip op. at 1–5.

The Court reversed and remanded. It did not decide the permissible application of every part of the rule to every product, nor did it finally settle the general test for all pre-enforcement APA challenges. Gorsuch, slip op. at 7 n.2, 24.

## Issues
Can a sufficiently complete weapon-parts kit already count as a “weapon” that may readily be converted to fire under subsection (A), even though assembly remains? Can an unfinished but sufficiently developed component count as a “frame or receiver” under subsection (B), despite the absence there of subsection (A)'s express ready-conversion phrase?

A related procedural issue concerns what suffices to reject the challengers' facial claim: is identification of some lawful applications enough in the case as presented, or must the court examine whether the regulatory definition as a whole extends beyond statutory limits? The latter dispute is especially important to Alito's dissent and is largely absent from the assigned excerpt.

## Rule and holding
The challenged provisions are not facially inconsistent with the Gun Control Act. At least some weapon-parts kits satisfy subsection (A)'s requirements, and at least some unfinished frames or receivers fall within subsection (B). The Fifth Circuit's categorical exclusions therefore cannot stand. Gorsuch, slip op. at 7–24.

The holding is bounded by the question presented and the examples used to resolve it. It does not establish that every collection of parts is a weapon, that any object capable of eventual conversion is already a frame, or that the agency may regulate raw material simply because it could be used to make a firearm. Id. at 13, 20–24.

## Actual judgment and consequences for remand
The Supreme Court reversed the Fifth Circuit's judgment and remanded for further proceedings consistent with its opinion. The rule survived the facial statutory objections as litigated. The majority left open difficult questions about particular products and the lawful weight of certain classification factors. Id. at 23–24.

The majority also addressed the dissents' procedural objection in a footnote. It said the broader question about the correct test should await another case, but the parties could not use this remand to inject arguments they had failed to pursue in the Supreme Court. Its reference to forfeiture and law-of-the-case principles limits an overly broad statement that everything remained open below. Id. at 7 n.2.

## Majority reasoning on the assigned kit issue
### Two statutory requirements must be satisfied
Subsection (A) requires a weapon and a specified relationship to firing: present ability, design, or ready conversion. The majority did not eliminate the noun “weapon” and ask only whether some assortment could eventually be transformed into a gun. It concluded that certain kits satisfy both requirements already. Id. at 7–8.

This structure explains why the completeness of the record example matters. A product with the relevant components and an obvious function can differ legally from a general assortment of materials. The majority was rejecting the categorical proposition that no kit can qualify, not identifying the exact point at which every possible unfinished item crosses the line.

### Ordinary usage can describe an unfinished human-made object
Gorsuch described “weapon” as an artifact noun: a word for an object created by people and identified substantially by its intended function. Ordinary speakers may call an unfinished manuscript a novel or an unassembled furniture purchase a table. In a suitable context, incompleteness does not erase the object's identity. Id. at 10–11.

The opinion applied that point to the record product, whose intended function and substantially complete collection of components made it recognizable as a weapon. This is a claim about ordinary usage informed by context, rather than a freestanding rule that all artifact nouns always include all incomplete precursors. The majority expressly retained limits. Id. at 10–13, 17 n.4.

### The statute itself confirms that operational completeness is unnecessary
The starter-gun parenthetical reinforced the majority's reading. Congress expressly included an object that ordinarily does not fire live projectiles without conversion. That inclusion provides a statutory example showing that present combat readiness is not required. The additional references to design and ready conversion likewise would be unnecessary if Congress meant to reach only fully operating guns. Id. at 11–12.

The Court used the starter-gun example as a benchmark for the amount of conversion effort contemplated by the statute. On the evidence before it, the relevant kit required no greater burden of time, expertise, or specialized equipment. That comparison supplied an application of the statutory test; it did not prescribe a universal numerical deadline for all products. Id. at 12–13.

### Other references to combinations of parts do not compel categorical exclusion
The challengers noted that Congress expressly regulated combinations of parts in provisions concerning other devices. The majority accepted that subsection (A) is narrower than those provisions. It does not reach every separate component or every possible kit. But a difference in breadth does not establish that its language reaches no kit at all. Id. at 14–15.

The majority also rejected a proposed requirement that every covered weapon contain a fully functioning frame. The statute contains no such categorical requirement, and some kits could satisfy even that proposed condition. Likewise, the fact that certain other criminal provisions would rarely apply to kits did not justify narrowing the definition across the whole Act. The statute expressly includes components and silencers that also cannot meaningfully participate in every firearm-related offense. Id. at 14–17.

### Acknowledged boundary problems do not decide this facial challenge
The majority recognized a continuum of completeness. An unfinished product can eventually become so incomplete that the relevant noun no longer fits. It compared the difficulty to determining when removing grains means a heap no longer exists. That problem was real but unnecessary to resolve at every margin here. Some products clearly fell within the statutory language, defeating the categorical position the lower court adopted. Id. at 13.

## Additional majority reasoning omitted from the kit-only excerpt
### Unfinished frames and receivers under subsection (B)
The majority applied ordinary-language reasoning to the component terms as well. It also examined the Act's serialization provision, §923(i), which requires serial numbers on the frame or receiver of covered firearms. Because the Act covers some incomplete or unconventional weapons and devices, the serialization provision necessarily uses those component terms in a way broader than only a prototypical finished frame. The majority favored consistent usage across the statute. Id. at 17–20.

It considered ATF's longstanding treatment of some incomplete components as further evidence of meaning. The opinion expressly maintained the court's independent responsibility to interpret the statute; it did not revive mandatory deference to any reasonable agency interpretation. The challengers' concession that they did not dispute the agency's prior practice also undercut their categorical finished-items-only position. Id. at 20–21 & 21 n.5.

### Why the omission of ready-conversion language did not control
The challengers emphasized that subsection (B), unlike subsection (A), does not say “may readily be converted.” The majority answered that the unqualified component nouns could themselves include certain unfinished objects in context. Subsection (B)'s reference to the frame or receiver of “any such weapon” also connects it to the weapons identified in subsection (A), some of which are not operationally complete. Id. at 21–22.

The Court did not hold that subsection (A)'s language can simply be copied into any other provision. It used the relationship between the subsections, ordinary usage, the serialization scheme, and prior practice together to reject the asserted categorical exclusion.

### Other statutes and unresolved factors
The challengers warned that the same reasoning might allow regulation of common semiautomatic receivers as machinegun receivers under the National Firearms Act. The majority rejected the extrapolation. The two Acts have different language, contexts, and regulatory problems, and the government disclaimed that classification. The opinion does not authorize it. Id. at 22–23.

The Court left for another case the lawful significance of tools, instructions, and similar associated materials in individual classification decisions. It likewise declined to use lenity or constitutional avoidance because text, context, and structure resolved the question before it. Possible ambiguity at other margins did not require resolving the present categorical claim for the challengers. Id. at 23–24.

## Separate opinions
### Sotomayor: compliance, evasion, and the scope of the majority
Sotomayor joined in full. She disputed the suggestion that established regulatory duties necessarily left good-faith businesses unable to determine their obligations. The duties were longstanding, and manufacturers could seek agency classifications for doubtful products. She also cautioned against using a dissent's characterization of the holding as a substitute for the majority's own language. Sotomayor, slip op. at 1–2.

Her concurrence matters because it responds both to Kavanaugh's fair-notice concern and to Alito's description of the examples as exceptionally close to finished products. It does not itself establish a new majority holding about every possible kit.

### Kavanaugh: different mental-state requirements
Kavanaugh also joined in full but recognized substantial uncertainty at the boundary. He distinguished offenses requiring willfulness from background-check offenses requiring knowledge. Under the authority he discussed, willfulness generally requires awareness that the conduct is unlawful; knowledge ordinarily concerns the facts constituting the offense. The former therefore supplies more protection against punishment of a person unaware of a disputed legal classification. Kavanaugh, slip op. at 1–2.

For background-check violations, he noted the government's representation that it likely would not prosecute certain good-faith situations and identified a possible due-process fair-notice argument. He did not hold that every violation requires knowledge of illegality or that prosecutorial discretion eliminates all notice problems. Id.

### Jackson: the scope of delegated authority
Jackson emphasized the limited nature of the APA claim. Congress had delegated rulemaking authority, and the challengers asserted that the agency exceeded its statutory boundaries. Courts must determine those boundaries, not ask whether the agency's policy choices are precisely the choices the judges would have made. Where the statute permits the agency's action, that excess-of-authority claim fails. Jackson, slip op. at 1.

Her concurrence is not an argument that agency history is controlling or that an agency may decide its own jurisdiction. It distinguishes judicial interpretation of legal limits from judicial substitution of policy preferences within those limits.

### Thomas: regulatory overbreadth and a different reading of the nouns
Thomas first objected to the majority's facial-challenge framework. A regulatory definition can contain a lawful application while still being unlawfully broader than the statute. Saving it merely because some applications overlap could make nearly any overbroad definition defensible. He would examine whether the regulation exceeds the statutory limits rather than regard one valid application as dispositive. Thomas, slip op. at 6–9.

On the merits, he read frames and receivers as the actual functional components, not objects that must first become those components. Congress expressly included conversion language elsewhere and omitted it from subsection (B), which strongly supported deliberate exclusion. He also objected to using external marketing materials or tools to change the legal identity of the object itself. Id. at 10–15.

Thomas criticized reliance on casual speech about unfinished novels or tables. Statutory drafting, he argued, requires attention to the full scheme and precise differences among provisions. He treated the serialization examples as context-specific accommodations, insufficient to redefine the component terms everywhere else. Id. at 15–20.

For kits, Thomas insisted that the statutory weapon requirement must be satisfied before ready conversion becomes relevant. In his view, a collection of unfinished pieces lacking the essential functional component was not already a weapon. The express inclusion of starter guns was a specified exception to ordinary meaning, not a warrant to generalize further exceptions. Id. at 20–24.

Finally, he would apply lenity if ambiguity remained because the statute carries criminal consequences. He drew on Thompson/Center Arms and the contrast between definitions expressly mentioning combinations of parts and those that do not. Id. at 24–26.

### Alito: agreement on examples, disagreement over the test
Alito's dissent is materially different from Thomas's. He agreed that the rule was consistent with the statute as applied to the examples the majority identified. He said he would join the Court if satisfied that the demanding facial-challenge standard governed. His disagreement was whether that standard should decide the case without fuller briefing and consideration. Alito, slip op. at 1–5.

He rejected the inference that labeling a claim facial amounts to conceding a particular constitutional facial-challenge test for an APA regulation. Respect for legislative enactments may justify that test in constitutional litigation, but the same justification does not automatically transfer to an agency definition alleged to exceed Congress's instructions. Applying it could substantially restrict courts' ability to set aside overbroad rules. Id. at 2–5.

Alito would have requested supplemental briefing or vacated and remanded for the court of appeals to address the test. He should therefore not be grouped with Thomas as simply concluding that no kit can ever satisfy the statute. His agreement on the majority's examples and his different proposed procedure are essential to the division in the Court. Id.

## Study synthesis and source limits
This case combines ordinary meaning, statutory context, consistent usage, express inclusions and omissions, agency history, and the procedural scope of a challenge. The kit-only assignment highlights the artifact-noun and starter-gun arguments. The full opinion reveals that the disposition also depends on the case's framing and produces a separate disagreement about APA review itself.

Do not convert “some products qualify, so this facial claim fails” into “every product the rule might reach is lawful to regulate.” Do not convert the majority's assumption of a framework for this dispute into a universal holding about all APA facial challenges. And distinguish Jackson's delegation-boundaries point, Kavanaugh's mens rea analysis, Sotomayor's compliance discussion, Thomas's statutory objections, and Alito's procedural dissent. They address related but different questions.
