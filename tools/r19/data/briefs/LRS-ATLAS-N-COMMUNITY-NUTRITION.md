# Community Nutrition Institute v. Young
818 F.2d 943 (D.C. Cir. 1987) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Court, stage, and disposition

On remand from the Supreme Court, the D.C. Circuit decided two unresolved challenges to FDA’s use of informal contaminant action levels. The per curiam opinion held that FDA had treated the challenged action levels as legislative rules and could not issue them without APA notice and comment. It separately rejected the challenge to FDA’s decision not to enforce the food-adulteration statute against certain blended corn. The case returned to the district court for proceedings consistent with that opinion. Judges Mikva and Edwards formed the majority on the rulemaking question. Judge Starr concurred on enforcement discretion but dissented from the legislative-rule classification. The decision is therefore 2–1 on the first issue and unanimous on the second. 818 F.2d at 944–945, 949–950.

## Facts and prior litigation

Aflatoxins are by-products of molds growing on crops, including corn. Under the Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, FDA regulated unavoidable poisonous or deleterious contaminants by announcing action levels. The challenged level for aflatoxins in corn was twenty parts per billion. The agency used these levels to describe allowable contamination and guide enforcement; it had not adopted the particular level through APA notice-and-comment procedures. FDA also occasionally permitted producers to blend contaminated corn with uncontaminated corn to bring the mixture within an acceptable concentration, particularly when weather produced a heavily contaminated harvest. Id. at 944–945 & nn.1–2, 949.

Community Nutrition Institute and associated organizations and individuals attacked the policy on three grounds. First, they claimed the organic statute required formal tolerances rather than informal action levels. Second, they claimed the action levels were legislative rules requiring APA procedures. Third, they claimed intentional blending violated the Act. The district court granted FDA summary judgment on all three. The D.C. Circuit’s first decision accepted the organic-statute argument, treated the APA issue as moot, and called for reconsideration of blending. The Supreme Court reversed that statutory interpretation in 1986, holding that FDA could use action levels under the organic statute, and remanded without deciding the APA and blending questions. This 1987 opinion resolves those remaining issues; it does not revive the statutory holding the Supreme Court had rejected. Id. at 944–945.

## Legislative rule or policy statement?

FDA did not dispute that its action levels were rules within the APA definition. The disputed question was whether they fell within the exception for interpretative rules and general statements of policy. FDA described the levels as nonbinding enforcement guidance. CNI argued that their effect on FDA’s discretion made them legislative rules. An agency’s own label received some weight, but not controlling deference. The actual language and operation mattered more. Id. at 945–946 & nn.2–5.

The majority applied American Bus’s criteria: whether the pronouncement had present binding effect and whether it genuinely left the agency and its decisionmakers free to exercise discretion. Footnote 4 clarified that the first inquiry concerns binding effect, not a literal distinction between present and future operation. An announcement of current intentions can remain nonbinding; a policy that presently creates a binding norm is legislative. The opinion accordingly should not be reduced to the proposition that guidance becomes a legislative rule simply because it applies immediately. Id. at 946 & n.4.

## Why FDA had bound itself

Three features supported the classification. First, FDA’s governing regulations said action levels defined when food would be deemed adulterated and could prohibit detectable quantities. Their language described an operative limit rather than tentative advice. The regulatory word “may” gave FDA discretion to establish an action level; it did not make a level nonbinding after establishment. The availability of an action level when circumstances made a formal tolerance subject to near-term change also connected the two regulatory devices. Id. at 946–947 & nn.6–7.

Second, FDA provided exemptions permitting marketing of food described as unlawfully contaminated. Requiring an exception made little sense if the underlying level were only a prediction that bound no one. Third, FDA’s communications approving particular blending plans and its Federal Register notice stated that corn exceeding twenty parts per billion was adulterated and could not be shipped interstate. These statements reinforced the levels’ substantive role. Id. at 947–948.

Publication did not furnish a simple answer. The particular level appeared in the Federal Register, while the authorizing regulations appeared in the Code of Federal Regulations. The court regarded that mixed pattern as inconclusive rather than treating either publication location as automatically dispositive. Id. at 947 n.8.

The majority acknowledged a significant point favoring FDA: in an enforcement suit, FDA apparently still had to prove statutory adulteration, rather than merely establish violation of the numerical level. The level did not conclusively determine liability as a conventional legislative rule would. But that was only one factor. FDA conceded that prosecuting a producer shipping corn below the level would be daunting. The agency had constrained its own enforcement discretion sufficiently to give the level substantive significance. Binding effect on the agency could therefore require notice and comment even though the policy did not conclusively bind the producer in court. Id. at 948 & nn.9–10.

The holding was expressly limited. The Supreme Court had allowed informal action levels under the organic statute, and FDA could still issue genuine nonbinding policies. Written enforcement guidance did not automatically become legislation. Here, however, FDA’s own words and conduct gave its levels present substantive effect, so the unobserved APA procedures invalidated the challenged action level. Id. at 949.

## Blending and enforcement discretion

The court agreed that intentionally blending contaminated corn made the resulting feed adulterated under the statutory provisions concerning added poisonous substances. An intentional addition was not an unavoidable contaminant merely because its concentration was diluted. FDA had conceded that point. But statutory adulteration did not require FDA to prosecute every violation. The Act authorized enforcement and expressly recognized discretion concerning minor violations. Id. at 949–950.

Under Heckler v. Chaney and the circuit’s related precedent, the gravamen of CNI’s blending claim was a challenge to FDA’s choice not to initiate enforcement. That decision lay within the agency’s unreviewable enforcement discretion in this setting. The court therefore rejected the blending challenge rather than declaring the feed unadulterated or conferring substantive legality on the blending practice. The two holdings are consistent: the agency must use required procedures when creating binding norms, yet it may decline particular enforcement actions where the statute commits that choice to it. Id. at 950.

## Starr’s separate opinion

Starr accepted the majority’s reasoning on blending and acknowledged that its multifactor approach fit recent circuit precedent. He nonetheless preferred Pacific Gas’s force-of-law criterion as determinative. A legislative rule fixes a legal standard that the agency can enforce merely by proving its application and violation; a policy statement requires the agency, when challenged, to justify the underlying standard independently under the statute and facts. Because FDA still had to prove adulteration with scientific or other probative evidence, the action level lacked that legal force in subsequent proceedings. Id. at 950–952.

Starr linked notice and comment to the deliberation and public participation appropriate before coercive governmental rules acquire legal status. That rationale also required respecting Congress’s exceptions for statements below the status of law. His proposed test aimed to make a difficult boundary clearer. He admitted that the exemption mechanism made these levels a troubling hybrid and that the majority’s result had commonsense support, but did not abandon his chosen test. Id. at 951–952 & Starr nn.1–3.

He also acknowledged the risk that agencies might cast desired regulations as guidance to avoid participation. Subsequent enforcement would still require justification, and Congress could mandate formal regulations when it wanted them. Here the Supreme Court had held that the organic statute imposed no such categorical obligation. The disagreement concerned the proper APA classification test, not FDA’s general authority to issue guidance. Id. at 952–953.

## Assigned passage and historical scope

The assigned material contains selected majority passages within commentary about the boundary between policy and legislative rules. It omits Starr’s complete separate writing, the prior Supreme Court stage, and the distinct blending holding. Its introductory statement that activists considered the levels too low conflicts with its later explanation that overly high thresholds permitted unsafe contamination; the brief does not turn that editorial inconsistency into a judicial finding. The full opinion supplies the actual parties’ legal grounds and the limited result. This account describes the 1987 decision rather than certifying later treatment of guidance or enforcement discretion. Full-source author checks are recorded in the accompanying register.
