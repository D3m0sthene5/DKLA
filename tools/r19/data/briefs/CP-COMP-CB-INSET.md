# Inset Systems, Inc. v. Instruction Set, Inc.
937 F. Supp. 161 (D. Conn. 1996) | Civil Procedure

## Court, motion, and result

District Judge Covello decided Civil No. 3:95CV-01314 (AVC) on April 17, 1996. A Connecticut software company alleged that a Massachusetts company infringed its INSET trademark through a domain name and toll-free telephone number. The defendant sought dismissal under Rules 12(b)(2) and 12(b)(3), contesting personal jurisdiction and venue. The court found repetitive business solicitation under Connecticut's long-arm statute, constitutionally sufficient purposeful contacts, and corporate residence supporting venue. It denied the motion, Document No. 7. This was a ruling permitting litigation to proceed, not a finding of trademark liability or a damages award. The sole judicial opinion contains four footnotes and no separate writing or appendix. (162–166.)

## The parties and disputed advertising

Inset Systems was incorporated in Connecticut and operated from Brookfield. It developed and marketed computer software and related services worldwide. Instruction Set, or ISI, was a Massachusetts corporation based in Natick, providing computer technology and support worldwide. It had no Connecticut office or employees and did not regularly conduct business there. (162–163.)

Inset applied to register its INSET trademark in August 1985 and received the registration in October 1986. ISI later obtained INSET.COM and used it to advertise its goods and services. Inset discovered the address in March 1995 when trying to acquire that domain for itself. ISI also advertised through 1-800-US-INSET. Inset had not authorized either use and filed this action on June 30, 1995. (163.)

The complaint sought damages and injunctive relief under federal trademark law, state unfair-competition and infringement principles, a Connecticut dilution provision, and the Connecticut Unfair Trade Practices Act. The court described a possible confusion mechanism: a user encountering an identical domain might mistake the advertiser for the trademark owner. That explanation supplied context for the alleged infringement; the jurisdictional order did not establish actual consumer confusion on the merits. (162–163.)

The opinion's technological premises reflect 1996. It described approximately twenty to thirty million Internet users worldwide and at least ten thousand connected users in Connecticut, contrasting continuously accessible online advertisements with broadcast schedules or discarded newspapers. Those figures are historical premises stated by the court, not present-day estimates. (163–164.)

## The separate statutory and constitutional inquiries

The plaintiff bore the burden of making a prima facie showing through affidavits or other evidence sufficient to establish personal jurisdiction. The inquiry had two steps: whether the defendant's conduct fell within Connecticut's long-arm statute, and whether exercising that statutory jurisdiction satisfied Fourteenth Amendment due process. Passing one step did not automatically establish the other. (163.)

For the statutory step, § 33-411(c)(2) subjected a foreign corporation to a resident's suit arising from business repeatedly solicited in the state, whether or not the resulting orders were accepted there. Note 2 reproduces the pertinent language, including the resident or usual-place-of-business requirement and the provision's application even where the corporation was not otherwise transacting business in Connecticut. ISI did not specifically contest satisfaction of this statute; Inset relied on the website and toll-free number. (163–164 & n. 2.)

Earlier district decisions had found repeated solicitation through advertisements in publications circulated in Connecticut. The court treated continuous Internet advertising as at least sufficiently repetitive: users in the state could access the same electronic advertisement repeatedly, with greater potential reach than discarded printed materials. It therefore found solicitation within subsection (c)(2). The opinion did not require evidence of completed Connecticut sales to reach that statutory conclusion. (164.)

## Purposeful contacts and fairness

ISI argued that its Massachusetts incorporation and operations, lack of a Connecticut office or employees, and absence of regular Connecticut business defeated minimum contacts. Inset responded that the website and toll-free number sought business in Connecticut. The court applied purposeful availment and reasonable anticipation of litigation, considering the total circumstances rather than a mechanical formula. (164–165.)

The decisive inference was broad: ISI directed its online advertising and telephone access toward every state, including Connecticut. Their nationwide reach did not, in the court's reasoning, exclude purposeful direction to this particular state. Continuous availability to Connecticut's Internet users supported the conclusion that ISI sought the privilege of doing business there and could anticipate being sued there. This is the historical reasoning of this decision; it should not be converted into an independently verified current rule that every accessible website establishes jurisdiction everywhere. (165.)

The court separately considered fair play and substantial justice. A defendant with sufficient contacts could still show compelling reasons why jurisdiction was unreasonable. Relevant considerations included comparative litigation burdens, the state's interest, and efficient interstate adjudication. ISI had focused on the absence of contacts and did not develop a distinct fairness objection. Inset emphasized the short journey from Massachusetts and ISI's retention of Connecticut counsel. The court itself stressed the minimal interstate distance, Connecticut's interest in its own common-law and statutory claims, and efficient resolution. It found jurisdiction fair. Retaining local counsel was not identified as an independent jurisdictional consent holding. (165.)

## Venue followed corporate residence under the applicable statute

ISI also asserted that the defendants resided in different states, relevant events occurred in Massachusetts, and litigation belonged there. Inset invoked the then-applicable venue rules for an action not founded solely on diversity. Section 1391(b)(1) permitted suit where a defendant resided if all defendants resided in the same state, while § 1391(c) deemed a corporate defendant resident in a district where it was subject to personal jurisdiction when the action began. (165–166 & nn. 3–4.)

Because ISI was a corporation subject to personal jurisdiction in Connecticut, the court treated it as resident there for venue and found § 1391(b)(1) satisfied. The analysis did not depend on finding Connecticut the sole place where relevant events occurred. Note 1 sets out § 1406(a)'s dismissal-or-transfer provision for improper venue, but the court found venue proper and ordered no transfer. These are the statutory formulations applied in 1996, not a certification of present venue law. (162 n. 1, 165–166 & nn. 3–4.)
