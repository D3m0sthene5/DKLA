**857 F. Supp. 2d 1378 (J.P.M.L. 2012) — April 23, 2012 — Civil Procedure**

Rendell, and Charles R. Breyer. Judge John G. Heyburn II did not participate, as the order’s starred note states. There is no separate opinion. ## Facts and the actions before the Panel

The bankruptcy of MF Global Holdings and related entities generated litigation by two different groups: investors in securities and holders of commodities futures customer accounts. The proceedings shared factual questions about the collapse and a reported estimated $1.6 billion customer-account shortfall. That estimate supplied the factual background for centralization, not an adjudication of liability or a damages award. Jon Corzine, Bradley Abelow, Henri Steenkamp, and Laurie Ferber moved to centralize the actions in the Southern District of New York. (1379–1380; filed order 1.)

Three actions were formally before the Panel: Henning-Carey Proprietary Trading v. Corzine in the Northern District of Illinois, and Joseph Deangelis v. Corzine and Sapere CTA Fund v. Corzine in the Southern District of New York. Footnote 1 explains why that number differs from the broader litigation picture. The motion initially encompassed twelve actions; eight had been closed when New York cases were consolidated, and an Eastern District of Michigan action had been dismissed. The Panel had additionally been notified of twelve potentially related actions: nine in the Southern District of New York and one each in Illinois, Montana, and the Eastern District of New York. Some New York actions had already been consolidated with Deangelis. Those were potential tag-along actions, rather than twelve additional actions expressly transferred by this order. (1379 n.1; filed order 1 n.1 and Schedule A.)

## The parties’ differing positions

The Sapere commodities customer plaintiff opposed centralization altogether. Customer plaintiffs in Illinois and Montana opposed combining commodities-customer litigation with securities litigation and proposed their respective districts for the customer actions. Other defendants, including Michael Stockman, Edith O’Brien, Christine Serwinski, and JPMorgan Chase, supported centralization of all actions in New York. Convertible-note underwriters and Jefferies supported centralization of securities actions there. Securities lead plaintiffs—the Queen in Right of Alberta and Virginia Retirement System—supported centralizing all actions in New York. Several customer plaintiffs also supported New York centralization while opposing actual consolidation of securities and customer actions before the transferee judge. Thus, agreement on location did not necessarily mean agreement on the extent of combined proceedings. (1380 & nn.2–4; filed order 1–2.)

The principal objection was that securities-law claims and Commodities Exchange Act and related customer claims were legally distinct. The opponents argued that a shared MDL would be inefficient and inevitably slow the customer litigation. The question was whether different claims justified separate centralization proceedings despite substantial shared facts. (1380; filed order 2.)

## Rule and reasoning

Section 1407 centralization requires common factual questions and a determination that coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings will serve convenience and the just and efficient conduct of the actions. The Panel held that disparate legal theories do not defeat transfer where the cases share substantial factual questions. These actions arose from the same corporate collapse and account shortfall and would focus on many of the same events, defendants, and witnesses. Separate statutory theories did not eliminate that overlap. (1380–1381; filed order 2.)

The Panel also declined to prescribe the precise organization of proceedings. Its usual practice was to avoid separate MDLs for different claims arising from a common factual background and to leave the degree of coordination or consolidation to the transferee judge’s discretion. That distinction answered part of the efficiency objection: centralization created a common judicial setting without requiring every pretrial task or claim to proceed identically. The decision did not announce that legally different claims had become one claim or that all claimants were one certified class. (1380–1381; filed order 2.)

Considering the submissions and hearing, the Panel found that centralization would avoid duplicative discovery, reduce inconsistent pretrial rulings—including class-certification rulings—and conserve the resources of litigants, counsel, and the judiciary. Potential common class-certification issues were a reason to coordinate; the Panel did not itself certify a class. (1381; filed order 2.)

New York was an appropriate location because numerous parties supported it, MF Global Holdings and several defendants were headquartered there, and the related bankruptcy proceedings were underway there. Coordination with bankruptcy litigation added practical value beyond simply combining securities and customer actions. Judge Victor Marrero had MDL experience, already presided over both kinds of actions, and had taken steps to streamline their resolution. These circumstances supported the selection of both district and judge. (1381; filed order 3.)

## Exact order and assigned significance

The Panel ordered the Schedule A actions pending outside the Southern District of New York transferred there under § 1407 and, with that court’s consent, assigned to Judge Marrero for coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings with the listed New York actions. Schedule A identifies only one action outside New York: Henning-Carey, No. 1:11-08717. The listed New York actions were Deangelis, No. 1:11-07866, and Sapere, No. 1:11-09114. The order neither resolved substantive wrongdoing nor expressly transferred every potential tag-along action. (1381; filed order 3–4.)

The assigned account accurately focuses on shared facts notwithstanding different legal theories and the transferee judge’s discretion over coordination. Its reference to three actions and twelve tag-along actions pending across four districts compresses footnote 1’s distinction between the three actions formally before the Panel and twelve potentially related actions. The textbook’s questions about administrative costs, counsel control, fees, and settlement leverage are editorial questions, not findings the Panel made about these parties.
