# Weiss v. United States
510 U.S. 163 (1994), No. 92–1482, with Hernandez v. United States (January 19, 1994) | Legislation and the Regulatory State | Assigning military judges and protecting impartiality without fixed terms

## Two convictions challenged the judges' legal authority

Weiss, a Marine, pleaded guilty at a special court-martial to larceny. His sentence included three months' confinement, partial forfeiture of pay, and a bad-conduct discharge. Hernandez, also a Marine, pleaded guilty to cocaine possession, importation, and distribution, and conspiracy. His sentence included twenty-five years' confinement, forfeiture of all pay, reduction in rank, and a dishonorable discharge; the convening authority reduced confinement to twenty years. The Navy-Marine Corps Court of Military Review separately affirmed their convictions. 510 U.S. at 165.

Weiss argued that military trial and appellate judges lacked constitutional authority because Judge Advocates General selected them without a new appointment under the Appointments Clause. He also argued that judges' lack of fixed terms denied due process. The Court of Military Appeals rejected both claims. On appointments, two judges considered the existing military commissions sufficient, one regarded the Clause as inapplicable to the military, and two dissented separately. Relying on that decision, the court also affirmed Hernandez's conviction. The petitioners sought joint Supreme Court review. Id. at 165–166.

The Supreme Court affirmed both judgments, 36 M.J. 224 and 37 M.J. 252. All nine Justices agreed with the result. Chief Justice Rehnquist's opinion was joined entirely by Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, and Ginsburg. Scalia and Thomas joined Part I on appointments and Part II-A on the history of military judicial tenure, and concurred in the judgment on separate reasoning. Souter and Ginsburg each wrote concurrences. The decision rejected structural challenges to these judges; it did not retry the guilty pleas or revise the sentences. Id. at 164, 181–199.

## Military judging was an assignment within an existing commission

Trial judges had to be commissioned officers and members of an appropriate state or federal bar. The relevant Judge Advocate General selected and certified them, and they acted as judges when detailed to courts-martial. Appellate judges could be commissioned officers or civilians and were assigned to the service Courts of Military Review. All judges involved in these petitioners' cases, however, were already commissioned military officers appointed by the President with Senate consent. The different question concerning civilian appellate judges was expressly outside the case. Id. at 167–170 & nn.2–5.

The parties agreed that military judges exercised the responsibilities of constitutional officers and that the Appointments Clause applied to military officers. The question was whether those already validly appointed officers required a second appointment before undertaking judicial duties. Special qualifications, such as bar membership, did not establish a separate office by themselves: pilots and military lawyers likewise needed qualifications beyond those of an ordinary officer. Id. at 169–171.

The statutory vocabulary reinforced that conclusion. Congress expressly required additional presidential appointment and Senate confirmation for several senior military positions, but used assignment and detail for military judges. The Court declined to infer that Congress had intended a separate appointment yet simply omitted it from the governing provisions. Buckley, Freytag, and Morrison established that the appointees were officers; they did not themselves resolve when an existing officer required a second appointment. Id. at 171–173.

## Shoemaker's germaneness principle was satisfied, with its reach left qualified

Shoemaker permitted Congress to give existing officers duties germane to their offices. The Court distinguished that case's assignment to two identifiable incumbents from the military system's indefinite number of judges selected among many qualified officers. Congress had not itself selected a particular individual for the new work, and the Court found no comparable circumvention through legislative appointment. Id. at 173–174.

The majority nevertheless tested germaneness while expressly assuming, rather than definitively deciding, that the principle governed this situation. Military judging was germane to military office. Commissioned officers traditionally maintained discipline, made arrests, imposed authorized nonjudicial punishment, served on courts-martial, and reviewed sentences. Military judges also lacked an independent general judicial authority apart from the court-martial or appellate court to which they were assigned. They could return to other duties and, with permission, perform nonjudicial tasks while serving as trial judges. Those features differentiated their role from a full-time civilian judge's office. Id. at 174–176.

The result therefore was not an exemption of military personnel from Article II. They had complied with constitutional appointment requirements through their existing commissions, and neither the statute nor the Clause required another appointment for these duties. Id. at 169–176. The assigned textbook's statement that the duties satisfied germaneness should be read with the actual opinion's reservation about whether that test was independently necessary here.

## Due process did not require a fixed judicial term

The petitioners sought some fixed term, not Article III life tenure. They alleged no actual or apparent bias by the judges in their cases. Instead they argued that the absence of tenure necessarily deprived military judges of sufficient independence. The Court recognized that impartial adjudication was a basic due-process requirement, while giving particular deference to Congress's authority over the armed forces. It adopted Middendorf's inquiry: were the factors favoring the requested safeguard so extraordinarily weighty as to overcome Congress's chosen balance? It did not apply Mathews or Medina without adjustment for the military setting. Id. at 176–178.

History mattered. Anglo-American military courts had operated without tenured judges, and Congress's revisions had never required fixed terms for these trial and intermediate appellate judges. A 1983 commission had considered tenure and recommended against guaranteeing terms. The majority did not say that every historically accepted military procedure necessarily remained constitutional; it treated the history as a factor alongside present protections. Id. at 178–179 & n.6.

Those protections separated judges from the convening authority's control over individual cases. Judge Advocates General supervised judges, while the Code barred command authorities from evaluating their judicial work or censuring them for findings, sentences, or other adjudicative acts. Judges could not adjudicate matters in which they had previously participated; accused persons could challenge a judge for cause and learn the judge's identity before electing trial by judge alone. The civilian Court of Military Appeals, whose judges had fifteen-year terms, enforced these safeguards. Its decisions prohibited evaluating, decertifying, or transferring judges because a superior disagreed with findings or sentences. The Court therefore rejected the claim of unfettered removal discretion and found the factors favoring fixed terms insufficient to overcome Congress's balance. Id. at 179–181 & n.7.

## The concurrences explain the constitutional qualifications

Souter joined on the understanding that military judges were inferior officers. Presidential appointment and Senate confirmation do not themselves make every commissioned officer a principal officer; Congress may use that more demanding appointment method for inferior offices. But assigning principal-officer responsibilities through a lower official's selection from a large pool could improperly bypass the Constitution's specific principal-officer process. Souter emphasized that appointment safeguards prohibit both one branch's aggrandizement and abdication by the branches that hold the responsibility. Id. at 182–191 & nn.1–6.

He considered the military judges' status debatable, especially at the appellate level, but found relevant limits in de novo review of trial rulings, reassignment authority, and absence of a fixed term. In a close classification question, he would tolerate a fully rational judgment of the political branches; he therefore concluded these were inferior officers. His discussion did not definitively classify Tax Court judges, and his constitutional analysis was a concurrence, not an additional holding announced for the Court. Id. at 191–194 & nn.7–9.

Ginsburg stressed that servicemembers retain constitutional protections and that the Court's close examination of the safeguards respected that principle. Modern military justice was more protective than its historical predecessors, but the petitioners' challenges still deserved judicial scrutiny. Id. at 194–195.

Scalia, joined by Thomas, regarded germaneness as necessary whenever a person outside the Clause's permitted appointing authorities could assign duties amounting to a new office. A Judge Advocate General was not such an appointing authority. Scalia upheld the assignments because the duties were germane, criticizing the majority's suggestion that germaneness might be unnecessary merely because many possible officers were available. His starred note regarded the principal/inferior issues raised by Souter as inadequately presented for decision. Id. at 195–197 & starred note.

On due process, Scalia and Thomas considered the settled military tradition sufficient. They rejected judicial rebalancing of independence and accountability as unnecessary: the challenged military system supplied at least the protections historically accepted in that setting. Scalia expressly distinguished civilian judicial tenure, so his view should not be converted into a general license for civilian criminal judges to serve at an executive's pleasure. Id. at 197–199.
