# Shaumyan v. O’Neill

**987 F.2d 122 (2d Cir. 1993), decided March 3, 1993. Civil Procedure.** Judge Miner wrote the single panel opinion for Judges Kearse, Miner, and Mahoney. The assigned account is CivPro physical page 171, printed 295, Note 1, ending before Note 2. It contrasts this home-repair contract dispute with Doehr’s intentional-tort setting. The complete report affirms an expressly narrow as-applied holding, rather than sustaining Connecticut’s attachment statute for every debt or every property owner.

## Repairs, attachment, and two federal decisions

Sebastian and Maria Shaumyan contracted with Sidetex in November 1986 to repair their New Haven home. The $14,800 agreement covered painting and installation of siding, storm windows, doors, and shutters, with payments due as work progressed. A disagreement arose over the windows’ quality. Sidetex replaced them and charged another $1,800 for materials and labor under a signed change order. The homeowners then refused to pay the $3,900 balance on the original contract and the additional $1,800. 987 F.2d at 123–124.

Attorney Steven Rolnick applied for an ex parte prejudgment attachment for Sidetex. Its president’s affidavit described the contract, asserted completed performance and repeated demands for the $5,700 balance, and denied setoffs, defenses, or counterclaims. A state judge ordered a $6,300 attachment on July 30, 1987. Sidetex brought its breach-of-contract action, while the Shaumyans filed a federal § 1983 action seeking a declaration of unconstitutionality, an injunction, damages, and fees against Sidetex and Rolnick. The other named defendants later left by stipulated dismissal, and the proposed class was never certified. Id. at 122–124 & n.1.

Sidetex released the ex parte attachment in November after the federal suit was filed, then obtained a $6,500 attachment following a December plenary hearing. That attachment was recorded in January 1988. The state court eventually awarded Sidetex $5,700 in June 1989. Shortly afterward, the federal district court granted summary judgment against the constitutional challenge. The Second Circuit remanded for reconsideration following Connecticut v. Doehr. District Judge Nevas again upheld the procedure as applied, and the present appeal reviewed that post-remand judgment. Id. at 124.

The later state judgment is part of the factual chronology and the panel’s discussion of the readily documented debt. It should not be treated as a general rule that eventual recovery retroactively cures any unconstitutional attachment.

## The statute and the issues on appeal

The challenged version of § 52-278e(a)(1) allowed attachment of real property without prior notice or a hearing and without a plaintiff’s security bond. It nevertheless required a probable-cause affidavit reviewed by a judge. After attachment, notice informed the owner of a right to seek vacation or modification, substitute a bond, or assert an exemption; a requested hearing had to occur within seven business days. The Shaumyans challenged both the absence of a predeprivation hearing and the absence of a creditor’s bond. Id. at 124–125.

The panel declined to decide facial invalidity. Due process varies with circumstances, and Doehr itself had decided the statute as applied. The inquiry here therefore concerned the homeowners’ dispute with a contractor seeking payment for improvements to the particular attached property. Id. at 126.

## Applying Doehr’s three considerations

The first consideration favored the homeowners. Attachment of their home clouded title, restricted sale or other alienation, impaired credit and borrowing prospects, and could affect an existing mortgage. Continued physical possession did not make that interference trivial. The panel recognized the same significant property interest that Doehr had recognized. Id. at 126.

The second consideration—risk of erroneous deprivation and the value of additional safeguards—pointed the other way. An intentional-tort claim can turn on disputed events, credibility, and uncertain damages that a one-sided affidavit poorly predicts. Here the contractor sought a sum certain for work under written agreements, and the underlying facts were readily documented. The court considered that setting more amenable to accurate ex parte assessment. Judicial affidavit review and the available postattachment hearing thus supplied adequate safeguards on these facts. This was a contextual conclusion about the repair debt, not a pronouncement that every breach-of-contract defense is simple or that documentary claims can never require prior hearings. Id. at 126–127.

The third consideration was Sidetex’s interest in the property. Unlike Doehr’s tort plaintiff, seeking an unrelated asset solely to secure a potential judgment, Sidetex had placed labor and materials into the very home attached. Those contributions could not be reclaimed after incorporation. The panel characterized the resulting interest as substantial and pre-existing even though Sidetex had not perfected a mechanic’s lien. Both parties initially acknowledged the availability of that lien remedy. Id. at 127–128.

Note 2 addresses the homeowners’ later attempt to withdraw that acknowledgment. They argued that the mechanic’s-lien statute did not cover remodeling or improvement. The panel found their cited cases construed an older version and their quotation omitted language added in 1974. It concluded that Sidetex’s work constituted an improvement qualifying for a lien, while acknowledging that Connecticut’s courts had not yet authoritatively construed the added language. That analysis supports the specific property-interest analogy; it does not establish that Sidetex actually perfected a lien. Id. at 127–128 n.2.

## Why no creditor’s bond was required here

Only four Justices in Doehr had reached the bond question. The Second Circuit’s earlier Pinsky opinions had rejected a constitutional bond requirement by a two-judge alignment, relying in part on Connecticut’s vexatious-litigation remedy. The panel revisited that reasoning after DeLaurentis clarified the state remedy. Double damages required an action without probable cause; treble damages additionally required malicious intent. Probable cause involved a good-faith belief in the facts and claim, so the remedy did not automatically compensate every unsuccessful attachment. The panel acknowledged that clarification could limit the owners able to recover. Id. at 128.

It nevertheless rejected the bond challenge in this case. Doehr’s plurality had linked the proposed bond requirement to the substantial risk of error in intentional-tort claims. The panel had found no comparable substantial risk here. It also relied on the mechanic’s-lien analogy: Sidetex could have achieved similar protection through a lien procedure without a bond. Together with the state remedy and Pinsky reasoning, those considerations made the omission constitutionally permissible for this dispute. The holding does not turn Doehr’s four-Justice bond discussion into a Court-wide rule or make vexatious-litigation damages unconditional. Id. at 128–129.

## Exact result and limits

The panel **affirmed the district court in all respects**. It expressly endorsed the district court’s restriction to a breach-of-contract action attaching the property on which the attaching party had installed materials and performed labor. Id. at 129. There is no separate opinion or appendix in the actual appellate report.

The assigned account accurately highlights documentary reliability and the contractor’s pre-existing interest. The full opinion adds the substantial homeowner interest, the post-remand posture, the unperfected mechanic’s-lien explanation, and the separate bond analysis. Those features explain why the result differs from Doehr without converting a confined 1993 decision into a general validation of all Connecticut attachments or a certification of current law.
