# Yakus v. United States
321 U.S. 414 (1944), Nos. 374 and 375, decided March 27, 1944 | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Court, posture, and judgment
The United States Supreme Court affirmed the First Circuit’s affirmance of criminal convictions for willfully selling wholesale beef above maximum prices fixed under the Emergency Price Control Act of 1942. Chief Justice Stone wrote the Court’s opinion, announced by Justice Roberts. Roberts nevertheless filed his own dissent from the judgment; Justice Rutledge filed a separate dissent joined by Justice Murphy. The Court sustained the delegation, held that the statutory review route excluded a regulation-validity defense in these criminal prosecutions, and rejected the defendants’ due-process and jury-trial objections on this record. The Court did not itself adjudicate whether the particular price regulation complied with the Act or was confiscatory. 321 U.S. at 418–419, 423–448, 460, 489.

## Facts and statutory scheme
Yakus and the petitioners in the companion Rottenberg case were prosecuted in the District of Massachusetts for willful sales of wholesale beef cuts above the ceilings in Revised Maximum Price Regulation No. 169. Issued December 10, 1942, that regulation fixed specific prices for beef and veal and included a statement explaining the considerations supporting its issuance. Its ceilings were slightly above those prevailing March 16–28, 1942 and approximated September 15, 1942 levels. The Administrator’s explanations connected the chosen base to earlier stabilization measures and explained why fixing prices at the statute’s October 1941 reference period was impracticable. 321 U.S. at 418–423 & nn.1–2.

Congress enacted the statute as a temporary wartime measure to stabilize prices and prevent inflation, profiteering, hoarding, speculation, and the disruption of production, fixed incomes, defense appropriations, and postwar values. The original June 1943 termination date was extended to June 1944. It authorized an appointed Price Administrator to establish ceilings when prices had risen or threatened to rise inconsistently with the Act’s purposes, and required the resulting prices to be generally fair and equitable and to effectuate those purposes. So far as practicable, the Administrator had to consult industry representatives, consider prices during a designated October 1941 base period or an appropriate nearby representative period, and adjust for relevant general factors such as costs, profits, and speculative fluctuations. The parties did not contest Congress’s underlying power to impose wartime commodity-price controls. 321 U.S. at 419–423.

Sections 203 and 204 supplied a specialized review system. A person subject to a regulation generally had sixty days after issuance to protest with specific objections and supporting written evidence; later protests were allowed for grounds arising later. The Administrator had to take initial action within prescribed periods and state the grounds and officially noticed material for a denial. An aggrieved protestant could then complain within thirty days to the Emergency Court of Appeals, a tribunal composed of designated federal district or circuit judges. That court could set aside a regulation as unlawful or arbitrary and capricious, with Supreme Court review by certiorari. It could not grant a temporary restraining order or interlocutory injunction; a permanent injunction’s effectiveness was postponed thirty days and, if certiorari was sought, until final Supreme Court disposition. Section 204(d) gave this route exclusive regulation-validity jurisdiction and withheld that power from other federal, state, and territorial courts. 321 U.S. at 427–430.

## Proceedings and arguments
The petitioners never filed an administrative protest or sought Emergency Court review, and the sixty-day period had expired when they were indicted. At their criminal trials, they offered proof that the ceilings failed the statutory standards and deprived them of property without due process because efficient wholesalers could sell only at a loss. The district court excluded the proof as irrelevant to the permitted issues, rejected their constitutional and statutory objections, and entered convictions on guilty verdicts. The First Circuit affirmed, 137 F.2d 850, and the Supreme Court granted certiorari. The jury found that the petitioners knowingly violated the regulation within eight days after issuance; they did not contend that they lacked notice. 321 U.S. at 418–419, 430–431, 435.

The petitioners attacked the delegation, argued that the Act should not exclude a defense contesting a regulation’s validity, and challenged the adequacy of the separate review process. They stressed the short protest period, restricted evidentiary process, inability to obtain a judicial stay, possible punishment before validity was resolved, and the forced choice between selling at a loss, ceasing business, or risking accumulating penalties. They also contended that removing validity from the criminal trial violated the Sixth Amendment and impermissibly interfered with judicial power. The Government defended the standards and the exclusive, prompt review system as necessary to maintain uniform wartime controls. 321 U.S. at 418, 430–447.

## Delegation analysis
Congress preserved the essential legislative function by stating a policy, choosing maximum-price regulation as the means, and defining the conditions and standards governing its use. The Administrator could ascertain complex facts, draw inferences, exercise judgment, and formulate subsidiary policy within that framework. Congress did not have to determine every fact or choose the means involving the least administrative discretion. The relevant question was whether the statutory field was sufficiently marked to permit a court to determine whether the legislative will had been obeyed, rather than whether Congress could have frozen prices mechanically at a single date. 321 U.S. at 423–426.

The majority distinguished Schechter’s invalid industrial-code delegation: that law’s very broad rehabilitation purpose lacked defined permissible code provisions and adequate standards, and entrusted formulation to private industrial groups rather than a responsible public official. Here the anti-inflation objective, fair-and-equitable constraint, base-period considerations, and required statement of considerations provided sufficiently definite guidance for the Administrator and a means for Congress, courts, and the public to check compliance. The Court compared these standards with previously sustained authority over reasonable rates, public-interest transactions, broadcasting, competition, and agricultural and tariff adjustments. This was a substantive nondelegation holding, not merely a conclusion that war automatically displaced constitutional limits. 321 U.S. at 424–427.

## Exclusive review, due process, and criminal trial
The text of section 204(d) was broad enough to exclude challenges to a regulation’s validity as defenses in criminal enforcement, not just separate injunction suits. Legislative history supported a deliberate distinction: enforcement courts retained authority to consider the constitutionality of the statute itself, but regulation validity belonged to the prescribed review route. The Court extended Lockerty’s conclusion concerning exclusive equity jurisdiction to this defense setting. 321 U.S. at 427–431.

Due process required a reasonable opportunity to be heard and present evidence, but these petitioners had never used the process and had not shown it incapable of affording that opportunity. The Court would not presume that the Administrator would deny an appropriate hearing, manipulate the record, refuse suitable additional evidence, or unreasonably delay. Rules permitted additional time for evidence and an oral hearing in appropriate cases; the Emergency Court could correct legal and procedural errors and authorize additional evidence; Supreme Court review remained available. The sixty-day protest period was not unreasonably short given wartime urgency. 321 U.S. at 432–437 & nn.3–5.

The prohibition on interim judicial relief also survived. Fragmented litigation and stays could cause disruptive regional disparities and delays that defeated nationwide anti-inflation policy. The statute did not compel anyone to sell a commodity, and offered a means of testing legality without necessarily incurring penalties. Although courts could not suspend a regulation during review, the Administrator could modify, rescind, or suspend it, including through amendment procedures. The petitioners could not assume that relief would have been denied without applying. Further, an injunction is not an absolute entitlement even where irreparable private harm threatens; Congress could make a public-interest choice that equity courts could themselves make. The majority analogized to other urgent public measures permitting initial action followed by judicial review. 321 U.S. at 431–443 & nn.6–8.

The Court also sustained separating regulation review from trial of the violation. A constitutional objection may be forfeited through failure to raise it timely before a competent tribunal. Comparable administrative schemes required prior review of challenged orders or rates. With an adequate separate opportunity to test validity, Congress could make willful violation criminal and assign validity and violation to different tribunals. The Emergency Court’s equity proceeding did not require a Seventh Amendment jury, and the local criminal jury properly decided whether the petitioners had committed the charged willful violations. 321 U.S. at 443–448 & n.9.

The Court reserved two important situations: a regulation unconstitutional on its face, and a defendant tried and convicted while diligently pursuing statutory review of the regulation. Neither was presented. It also recognized that a criminal ruling excluding a showing that the accused had lacked an opportunity to establish invalidity could be reviewed on constitutional grounds. The decision therefore does not hold that all constitutional regulation defenses may always be excluded regardless of access to review. 321 U.S. at 446–447.

## Separate opinions
Roberts thought the Act delegated legislative power without real boundaries. In his analysis, its multiple broad economic purposes let the Administrator choose conflicting policies and balance them according to personal judgment; the fair-and-equitable language and base-period directions did not establish an enforceable constraint. He also considered review ineffective: the protestant had to submit objections and evidence before learning the full administrative basis, while the Administrator could add economic studies and noticed material, and the court’s deferential review presumed validity. His discussion of Emergency Court decisions illustrated why he regarded the nominal review as unable to restrain the Administrator. He rejected the idea that wartime power could authorize surrender of legislative functions or suspension of constitutional limits. 321 U.S. at 448–460 & nn.1–3 (Roberts, J., dissenting).

Rutledge, joined by Murphy, agreed with the Court on the substantive issues, including the delegation, and accepted exclusive review and denial of interim injunctions. His objection concerned their combination with criminal enforcement that withheld validity defenses. Broad power to withhold jurisdiction did not authorize Congress to confer criminal jurisdiction while compelling courts to enforce potentially unconstitutional regulations without considering their validity. He argued that the summary civil protest process, written evidence, short limitations period, and uncertain protection against punishment could not replace the constitutionally protected criminal trial. Dividing essential questions between tribunals differed from ordinary forfeiture within successive stages of the same proceeding. 321 U.S. at 460–485 & nn.1–39 (Rutledge, J., dissenting).

Rutledge did not conclude that the defendants’ particular confiscation proof necessarily established invalidity. Note 40 explained that neither the Fifth Amendment nor the Act guaranteed each individual dealer a profit, and the offered proof concerning them or Boston wholesalers hardly established that the trade generally lacked a fair margin. Nonetheless, he considered the denial of a full criminal defense impermissible as a matter of constitutional procedure. Other enforcement tools could keep controls effective without excluding the defense. His final note allowed that carefully safeguarded specialized proceedings might foreclose some complex economic issues if they supplied the substantial equivalent of a criminal defense and a genuine opportunity to waive it; racial or religious discrimination issues should remain available in the criminal forum. He would reverse. 321 U.S. at 486–489 & nn.40–41.
