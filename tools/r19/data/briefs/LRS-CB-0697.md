# Vermont Yankee Nuclear Power Corp. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.

**Course:** Legislation and the Regulatory State
**ID:** LRS-CB-0697
**Review:** Full opinion and textbook comparison; two footnote locators corrected.

## Citation

Vermont Yankee Nuclear Power Corp. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc., 435 U.S. 519 (1978), decided with Consumers Power Co. v. Aeschliman. Justice Rehnquist delivered the Court's opinion. Assigned excerpt: casebook pp. 697–702, October second scan, PDF pp. 119–124. Full judicial opinion: [Justia reproduction](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/435/519/); [original U.S. Reports scan](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep435/usrep435519/usrep435519.pdf). The opinion runs from reporter pp. 523–558 and addresses both the fuel-cycle rulemaking and the companion construction-permit proceeding. The printed casebook page is 697, not 597.

## Parties

Vermont Yankee Nuclear Power Corporation sought to preserve an operating license for its nuclear plant in Vernon, Vermont. Natural Resources Defense Council and other challengers contested the treatment of nuclear-fuel reprocessing and radioactive-waste disposal in the agency's environmental analysis. The federal regulator was the Atomic Energy Commission, whose licensing functions were transferred to the Nuclear Regulatory Commission; the opinion refers to both as the Commission. 435 U.S. at 525–530 & n.2.

In the companion case, Consumers Power Company sought to preserve a construction permit for two reactors in Midland, Michigan. Intervening groups, identified as Saginaw and Mapleton, opposed the project. The issues reaching the Supreme Court included energy-conservation alternatives and the adequacy of an advisory safety report. These were distinct agency proceedings, although the Court of Appeals had connected their treatment of fuel-cycle effects. Id. at 530–537 & n.9.

## Material Facts

The nuclear-licensing scheme involved separate authorization at construction and operation. Applications received staff review, review by the Advisory Committee on Reactor Safeguards, and the applicable environmental analysis. Licensing boards conducted adjudicatory hearings, followed by administrative review. Vermont Yankee received a construction permit in 1967 and later applied for an operating license. In that licensing proceeding, the board excluded the environmental effects of fuel reprocessing and waste disposal, and the Appeal Board initially sustained that exclusion. Id. at 525–528.

The Commission then undertook a generic rulemaking on fuel-cycle effects. It published alternatives based on an Environmental Survey and made the survey and background documents available. Participants could submit written statements, make oral presentations as time allowed, appear through counsel, and supplement the record after the hearing. The Commission questioned speakers, but participants were not given discovery or cross-examination. The resulting 1974 rule supplied numerical environmental-impact values for use in individual licensing cost-benefit analyses. The Commission concluded that the effects were relatively small and that earlier licensing determinations need not be reopened on that account. Id. at 528–530.

Consumers Power's separate application underwent extensive safety and environmental review. The advisory committee referred to certain generic safety issues addressed in other publicly available reports. Saginaw submitted numerous environmental contentions but declined to participate meaningfully in later evidentiary hearings or identify supporting record findings. Its energy-conservation arguments were not developed into specific, adequately supported alternatives. After the administrative record closed, evolving guidance and another Commission decision addressed conservation more expressly. The Commission declined to reopen the completed proceeding, explaining the showing needed to justify further inquiry. Id. at 530–535.

## Procedural History

The D.C. Circuit set aside the fuel-cycle rule after finding the rulemaking procedures insufficient to allow adequate exploration of the issues, even though the agency had supplied the procedures required by APA § 553 and additional opportunities. It consequently remanded the Vermont Yankee licensing determination. The parties later disagreed about whether the lower court had found a procedural defect or inadequate substantive support. After examining its opinion, the Supreme Court concluded that procedural insufficiency was at least a principal ground. Id. at 535, 539–542.

In the companion case, the D.C. Circuit found the environmental impact statement defective for failing to examine conservation alternatives and required further explanation of the advisory committee's generic safety concerns. It also remanded the fuel-cycle issues under its Vermont Yankee ruling. The Supreme Court reviewed these rulings together. Subsequent interim rulemaking did not moot Vermont Yankee's challenge because the lower court's judgment still affected whether its license required renewed evaluation and possible revocation. Id. at 536–537, 535–536 n.14.

## Issue

May a reviewing court require an agency conducting informal rulemaking to employ additional procedural devices, such as discovery or cross-examination, because the subject is technically complex or the court believes those procedures would produce a better record, when the agency has satisfied applicable statutory requirements? If those additional procedural demands are impermissible, may the reviewing court nevertheless test whether the rule is adequately justified under the APA?

The companion issues are whether the Commission improperly declined to reopen the construction-permit proceeding for inadequately developed conservation proposals and whether a court could require the Commission to return its expert advisory report for a more detailed, lay-readable treatment of generic safety issues. Id. at 523–525, 539–558.

## Rule and Holding

For ordinary informal rulemaking, § 553 generally establishes the procedural requirements courts may impose. Agencies may voluntarily provide additional opportunities, but reviewing courts ordinarily may not add their own preferred procedures beyond those required by the Constitution, the APA, and other governing statutes. Technical complexity or public importance alone does not authorize a court to require trial-type devices. The Court preserved constitutional constraints and referred to exceptionally compelling circumstances; it did not establish that no additional procedure can ever be legally required. Id. at 524–525, 542–548.

The D.C. Circuit had exceeded that limit in setting aside the fuel-cycle rule for its procedural format. Whether the rule had adequate substantive support remained for APA review on remand. In the companion proceeding, the Commission permissibly required sufficiently clear and material conservation contentions to warrant further inquiry and was not obliged to reopen on the basis presented. The court also lacked authority to prescribe the additional advisory-report process it demanded. Id. at 549–558.

## Judgment

The Supreme Court reversed both judgments and remanded for further proceedings. For the Vermont Yankee rule and operating-license dispute, the Court expressly left open whether the fuel-cycle rule could survive review of its substantive justification under the APA. The Court of Appeals remained free to find the rule arbitrary and capricious on that proper ground. The Supreme Court therefore did not finally approve the fuel-cycle rule or establish that the license was unconditionally valid. It also reversed the challenged conservation and advisory-report rulings in the Consumers Power case. Id. at 549, 555–558; id. at 535–537 n.14.

## Reasoning

1. Congress allocated procedural authority through the APA. Section 553 prescribed notice, an opportunity to submit material, consideration of relevant submissions, and a statement of basis and purpose. The Court read the statute and its history as preserving agency discretion to add useful procedures, rather than delegating to reviewing courts a general power to redesign rulemaking. Prior decisions likewise recognized agencies' comparative ability to adapt procedures to their regulatory tasks. Id. at 523–525, 544–546.

2. The lower court's operative demand concerned procedure even without a detailed procedural command. The D.C. Circuit said it would not dictate the precise format, but faulted the opportunities to test and develop the technical evidence and discussed further devices or more sensitive use of existing ones. The Supreme Court concluded that the decision effectively conditioned validity on additional procedure. Framing that demand as assurance of meaningful participation did not remove the need for legal authority to impose it. Id. at 539–542.

3. The constitutional and settled-practice qualifications did not apply here. The Court distinguished proceedings exceptionally affecting a small group on individual grounds, in which due process may require additional safeguards. NRDC did not maintain a constitutional claim before the Supreme Court, and the generic fuel-cycle rulemaking did not present that individualized situation. The Court also left open the possibility of correcting a wholly unjustified departure from established agency procedures, but a few earlier proceedings using additional devices did not establish the necessary longstanding practice. Id. at 542–543 & nn.16–17.

4. Judicial selection of optimal procedures would make review unpredictable. An agency choosing a procedure before a hearing could not reliably know what a reviewing court would later deem necessary after seeing the resulting record. The incentive would be to use full adjudicatory safeguards in every rulemaking as protection against reversal. That result would erase the statutory distinction between informal rulemaking and trial-type adjudication and sacrifice the benefits of the former. Id. at 546–547.

5. The adequacy of support and the form of the proceeding are different inquiries. Informal rulemaking may draw on written submissions, studies, agency expertise, and other properly considered material without creating a trial transcript. Additional cross-examination does not automatically establish a better legally relevant record. A court may find the agency's substantive explanation or evidentiary support insufficient, but ordinarily must allow the agency to determine how to address that deficiency. Id. at 544–545, 547–549.

6. NEPA did not supply a general authority to supplement APA procedures. The environmental statute required the specified consideration and disclosure of effects and alternatives. The Court found no statutory language giving courts power to impose an expanded rulemaking procedure because environmental questions were involved. It nevertheless required compliance with the procedures actually mandated by the relevant statutes. The restriction on judicial innovation therefore preserved, rather than displaced, enforcement of genuine statutory duties. Id. at 548–549 & n.21.

7. Fuel-cycle environmental effects were within the Commission's authority. Vermont Yankee argued that its license could issue without considering the effects of reprocessing and waste disposal. By the time of review, the Commission itself accepted consideration of those effects. The Court held that it acted within its statutory authority: the radioactive wastes were environmental consequences of plant operation, and their persistence implicated the commitments of resources addressed by NEPA. The Court resolved the authority to consider them without issuing a substantive determination that the agency's numerical estimates were correct. Id. at 538–539.

8. Proper substantive review remained necessary. A judge on the appellate panel had considered the rule insufficiently justified on the record, and the other judges may also have harbored that concern. The Supreme Court therefore remanded for ordinary APA review of the agency's contemporaneous explanation. A finding that the agency's conclusion could not be sustained on its record could still require vacatur and further agency consideration. This reservation is essential to understanding the judgment. Id. at 549; id. at 535–537 n.14.

9. Conservation alternatives had to be assessed in their historical and procedural setting. The Court recognized NEPA's requirement to address alternatives, while explaining that the universe of alternatives must be bounded by feasibility and what was reasonably known at the time. Conservation guidance developed after the Midland environmental statements and hearings. The agency was not obliged to anticipate every later development or reopen indefinitely each time a new policy idea emerged. The record had thoroughly addressed conventional demand for the plant's output, and the intervenors had not developed a contrary feasible alternative. Id. at 550–555.

10. Participants had responsibilities even though the agency retained an independent environmental duty. The Commission did not require an intervenor to prove a full civil-litigation prima facie case. It required a timely, specific, material showing sufficient to prompt reasonable further inquiry. Saginaw had received opportunities to clarify and support its contentions and did not use them. The Court considered that threshold a permissible means of conducting an intelligible proceeding, rather than an abdication of the Commission's responsibilities under NEPA. Id. at 553–554.

11. The advisory-report remand lacked a statutory basis. The Advisory Committee on Reactor Safeguards primarily supplied expert technical advice. Its references concerned generic issues documented in other publicly available reports. The lower court required elaboration understandable to lay readers and required the licensing board to seek it on its own initiative, although no member of the allegedly confused public had requested that remand. The Supreme Court found the imposed step unsupported by the statutes and disproportionate to the supposed defect in an otherwise extensive review. Id. at 556–558.

## Separate Opinions

There were no separate concurring or dissenting opinions in the Supreme Court. Justice Rehnquist's opinion was joined by all participating Justices. Justices Blackmun and Powell took no part in consideration or decision, so the decision was unanimous among seven participants, rather than a nine-Justice vote. 435 U.S. at 558.

The opinion's discussion of Judge Tamm's concurrence concerns the D.C. Circuit, not a separate Supreme Court opinion. His view that the rule lacked adequate record support remained pertinent to the issue left open on remand. Id. at 549.

## Notes

The assigned excerpt concentrates on the fuel-cycle rulemaking and ends by preserving substantive APA review. The full opinion adds the construction-permit issues, the agency's authority to consider fuel-cycle effects, the mootness discussion, and the complete qualifications concerning constitutional procedure and established practice. Those additions are identified separately so that the companion case is not mistaken for part of Vermont Yankee's operating-license record.

A useful application requires identifying the source of the asserted procedural duty. An express statutory requirement, a constitutional hearing requirement, an agency's own binding procedural rule, and a reviewing judge's preference raise different questions. This opinion rejects the latter as a general justification for supplementing informal rulemaking. It does not excuse noncompliance with a statutory command or eliminate review of the agency's substantive reasoning. Id. at 542–549.

Read alongside State Farm: a court can require a reasoned treatment of an important alternative without prescribing the evidentiary format for producing that treatment. That comparison rests on State Farm's express discussion of this decision, 463 U.S. at 50–51, separately briefed in this edition. The historical observations about nuclear technology and conservation describe the record and legal setting considered in 1978; they are not current scientific or regulatory conclusions.
