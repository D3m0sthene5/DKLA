# Meyer v. South Dakota Department of Social Services
581 N.W.2d 151, 1998 SD 62 (1998) | Contracts

## Court, stage, and result
The South Dakota Supreme Court unanimously affirmed the circuit court’s affirmance of an administrative decision denying Harold Meyer Medicaid benefits. Justice Gilbertson wrote; Chief Justice Miller and Justices Sabers, Amundson, and Konenkamp concurred. The decisive conclusion was that the purported gifts and loans within the family did not validly encumber Meyer’s ranch. The court declined to decide whether the instruments independently constituted a Medicaid-qualifying trust. This is an administrative-benefits appeal, rather than a foreclosure action determining competing creditors’ rights. (¶¶1, 9, 18, 25–27.)

## Facts and chronology
Meyer was seventy-seven and had owned more than 6,000 ranch acres in Corson County, some outright and some jointly with his wife, who had died by the time of the litigation. After his 1986 automobile accident, a certified public accountant, Neil Cahill, advised the parents, their four children, and the children’s spouses on estate and income-tax planning. The resulting plan used four transactions during 1989–1991. The parents wrote checks marked as gifts; the children immediately endorsed them back; the parents deposited the uncashed checks into their own account and executed mortgages, generally accompanied by notes, for the supposed amounts borrowed from the children. (¶¶2–3; n.1.)

The first two transactions each involved $80,000. In December 1989, the children endorsed eight $10,000 checks back to their mother; the parents deposited them and executed the corresponding note and mortgage on December 29. A similar January 1990 transaction produced another $80,000 note and mortgage. The first instruments provided annual repayments of at least $6,800 through 2009 and did not require insurance on the premises; the second used the same terms. A third $80,000 arrangement began in January 1991, with its mortgage executed in June. The fourth involved $200,000 in June 1991 and a note signed only by Meyer’s wife. Two earlier, ordinary mortgages—a $62,500 Small Business Administration mortgage and a $160,000 Farm Credit Bank mortgage—were undisputed. The court’s objection concerned the family arrangements, rather than all liens on the ranch. (N.1.)

Meyer entered a nursing home on June 20, 1991, and applied for Medicaid five days later. His first application failed for insufficient resource information. It reported $9,040 in rental or lease income, although a son leased the ranch for $25,000 annually in 1991, 1992, and 1994. Seven subsequent applications also failed, most because of excess resources. His lawyer twice told the department that no money had changed hands in the family transactions. Cahill later disputed that description, asserted that the documentation showed consideration, and persuaded the department to accept the mortgages as valid liens. Meyer consequently received benefits from January 1995 until October 1996, when excess resources again led to termination. The department maintained that its earlier approval had been erroneous but sought no reimbursement for those benefits. (¶¶4–8; n.2.)

Following a February 1997 denial, an administrative law judge found that the family transactions had not created valid encumbrances. The judge also invoked Medicaid-trust rules and public policy. The department adopted the decision; the circuit court affirmed; Meyer appealed. (¶9.)

## Issues, arguments, and governing rules
Meyer argued that his notes and mortgages validly reduced the ranch’s available equity and that the department therefore wrongly counted the property as a resource. He also contested the trust classification, the public-policy objection, and treatment of the real estate as available to him. The appellate court gave substantial weight to agency factual findings and reviewed them for clear error, while reviewing legal questions fully. (¶¶9–10.)

A valid gift requires intent, delivery, and acceptance. Donative intent must presently relinquish dominion, and delivery must be complete and unconditional. Mortgages and contracts require valid consideration; formal documents cannot supply a bargained exchange that the parties never intended. Medicaid, under the provisions applied in this historical dispute, assessed available income and resources and required applicants to exhaust other resources. Those benefits rules supplied the setting in which the court tested the alleged debts. (¶¶12–15, 20–22.)

## Reasoning
The court upheld the agency’s finding that Meyer lacked actual donative intent. Cahill acknowledged that Meyer lacked the liquidity to make the purported gifts and obtained unsecured loans to cover the checks. The children knew the plan before receiving them and were expected immediately to return them. The evidence supported the conclusion that they neither expected the checks’ proceeds nor intended to exercise the notes’ and mortgages’ rights upon default. Demanding payment on the supposed gifts would have made their parents insolvent. Conditional receipt followed by an obligatory return therefore failed to transfer genuine control over the funds. (¶¶12–14.)

That failure also defeated the alleged loans. The parents were effectively purporting to borrow funds over which they had never surrendered control. The administrative judge found both an absence of bargained exchange and illusory or conditional promises. The Supreme Court agreed that the supporting notes and mortgages lacked consideration. Its reasoning concerns the substance and intended exchange, rather than the adequacy of a small but genuine price. The written checks, notes, and recorded mortgages did not transform this circular arrangement into enforceable debts reducing Medicaid resources. (¶15.)

The court separately described the agency’s alternative trust analysis. The applicable regulation covered certain pre-August 11, 1993, nontestamentary trusts or similar legal devices using an applicant’s or spouse’s funds, with discretionary distributions. The agency thought the children’s foreclosure powers and the instruments’ repayment and insurance terms resembled trust powers. The Supreme Court observed that the cited authorities did not establish the agency’s proposed constructive-trust application, but expressly reserved that issue because the absence of valid encumbrances already resolved eligibility. Thus, the affirmance does not establish that every family mortgage is a Medicaid-qualifying trust. (¶¶17–18; n.3.)

The court also condemned this particular use of illusory gift-and-loan transactions to shelter assets for heirs while obtaining publicly funded care. Cahill’s testimony showed concern about preserving assets against nursing-home costs as well as tax reduction. The court emphasized Medicaid’s role in supporting people without available resources and held, under these specific facts, that Meyer had not validly encumbered his property. It summarily rejected the remaining resource argument. (¶¶20–26; n.4.)

## Assigned significance and source scope
The assigned discussion is Note (4), “The Peppercorn and Token Payments Offered as Consideration,” beginning at printed page 59 and continuing onto page 60. Meyer’s own account appears at the start of page 60. It contrasts efforts to formalize an intended obligation with a purported exchange that is a sham. The complete opinion supports that distinction through actual donative intent and the absence of a genuine loan; it also reveals the administrative posture, prior temporary benefits, reserved trust question, and narrow factual basis omitted from the short account.

The complete reproduced judicial opinion, all twenty-seven numbered paragraphs and four footnotes, and both assigned scans were read.
