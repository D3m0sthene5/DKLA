# Ellefson v. Megadeth, Inc. — assigned problem, source-limited
2005 WL 82022 (S.D.N.Y. 2005) | Contracts

## Source status

It supplies a settlement chronology and asks whether the court should enforce the agreement; it does not reproduce the court's complete decision or give the answer. The actual opinion has not been recovered. This account preserves the supplied facts and issues without inventing a holding, judge, star-page locator, or final disposition. All factual locators below refer to assigned printed page 268.

## Settlement chronology supplied

Ellefson, a member of Megadeth, sued the band for fraud arising from a dispute over profit distribution. Counsel negotiated a Settlement and General Release, revising it repeatedly over several weeks. On May 13, 2004, defendants emailed that they would terminate the deal at 5 p.m. Pacific time on May 14 unless they had a signed agreement in hand. The agreement was finalized and sent to defendants at 4:45 p.m. May 14, but Ellefson's signature page was not faxed until 5:16 p.m. The difference between sending the text before the deadline and sending the signature afterward is central to the problem. (Assigned 268.)

On May 20, defense counsel sent fully executed copies to all parties by regular mail. On May 24, Ellefson's lawyer purported to withdraw from negotiations. Defense counsel responded that a signed agreement already existed. Ellefson received the copies mailed May 20 on June 2. These dates are the assignment's chronology, not facts independently verified from an available judgment. They must not silently be replaced with differing dates in secondary accounts. (Assigned 268.)

## Motion and questions posed

Defendants moved to enforce the settlement. The textbook asks whether to grant that motion and directs the reader to its earlier discussion of tardy acceptances. It quotes the court's observation that the motion resembled a first-year contracts examination. That quotation identifies the subject of the judicial dispute but gives neither the court's answer nor its full reasoning. (Assigned 268.)

The facts raise successive formation questions: what legal effect the receipt deadline had; what effect the late signature had; whether defendants' execution and subsequent mailing could form an agreement; and whether the attempted withdrawal preceded any legally effective acceptance. Distinguishing those events prevents assuming that a failure to meet the first deadline necessarily ended every possibility of contract formation. These are questions exposed by the supplied chronology, not holdings attributed to the unavailable opinion. (Assigned 268.)
