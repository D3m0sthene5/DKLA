# Pacific Gas & Electric Co. v. Federal Power Commission

**506 F.2d 33 (D.C. Cir. 1974) — June 26, 1974.** Judge MacKinnon wrote the panel’s single opinion, joined without a separate writing by Chief Judge Bazelon and designated Senior District Judge Christensen. One opinion disposes of Pacific Gas & Electric’s and Mississippi Power & Light’s two petitions, Nos. 73-1358 and 73-1485. Assigned in LRS November-a, physical PDF 53–54, printed 628–629.

## The shortage, agency action, and challenge

A natural-gas shortage forced pipeline companies to choose which customers would lose deliveries during periods of peak demand. Contract commitments and efficient uses of gas could point in different directions. Pipelines also worried that departing from existing contracts could expose them to civil liability. The Federal Power Commission’s 1971 Order 431 had directed pipelines anticipating shortages to file curtailment plans. Those filings varied substantially: some prioritized end uses, others contractual entitlements. The absence of a stated national approach complicated planning by pipelines, distributors, and consumers. (35–36.)

Order 467, issued January 8, 1973 without prior notice and comment, announced an end-use approach and a proposed priority schedule. Its appendix prioritized residential and small commercial needs and relegated some industrial boiler-fuel and interruptible requirements to lower positions. The Commission explained, among other considerations, that residential customers could not safely be curtailed daily and that many interruptible or boiler-fuel customers could substitute other fuels. Order 467-A added emergency procedures, and Order 467-B affirmed and partly amended the policy while denying requests for rehearing and intervention. The opinion treats these orders collectively as Order 467. (35–37; appendix 50–52.)

Customers facing possible reductions, particularly electricity generators with low priorities, sought review under §19(b) of the Natural Gas Act. They alleged three defects: the Commission had adopted a substantive rule without APA rulemaking procedures, had not developed an adequate factual record supporting its priorities, and had not complied with NEPA. The Commission characterized its pronouncement as advance guidance for later proceedings, not a final determination of any pipeline’s customers’ rights. (35–37.)

## Holdings and disposition

The panel held that Order 467 was a general statement of policy exempt from the APA’s notice-and-comment requirement. Its classification also mattered to judicial review: this particular order had no sufficiently immediate and significant impact on the petitioners, and the record did not permit meaningful review of their substantive objections. The court therefore dismissed both petitions for lack of §19(b) jurisdiction. It did not uphold the priority schedule’s substantive wisdom, decide contractual immunity, or resolve the NEPA claim. (45–49.)

## A tentative policy must remain open to challenge

The assignment reproduces the court’s central distinction. A substantive rule supplies a legal standard that generally governs later agency proceedings: the questions become application to the facts, waiver, or compliance, rather than a fresh examination of the policy itself. A general statement of policy instead discloses what the agency intends to pursue in future rulemaking or adjudication. It cannot become a binding legal premise merely because the agency published it. When the agency later applies the policy, it must supply supporting evidence and reasoning as if the statement had not already settled those matters. (38–39.)

Publishing genuinely tentative guidance can nonetheless serve important functions. It reveals agency intentions rather than leaving regulated entities to encounter undisclosed policies, permits long-range planning, and encourages national consistency. The APA’s exception recognizes that value. But an agency’s label is evidence rather than an automatic answer. The practical effect in subsequent proceedings is critical. A professed policy statement that actually establishes a binding norm cannot escape the procedures required for a substantive rule. (37–40.)

Order 467 passed that test because it established no particular pipeline’s curtailment plan and expressly contemplated further proceedings. Interested parties could challenge the underlying priorities on facts and law, not merely request exceptional relief from an otherwise conclusively valid schedule. The Commission repeatedly represented that its proposed plan was a starting point and would be adjusted when a hearing record required it. The court expected later review to police those assurances. It did not authorize the Commission to turn tentative guidance into binding precedent without the necessary support. (40–43.)

The customers argued that the schedule shifted the burden of proof from pipelines to affected customers. The court rejected that reading. A filing conforming to the policy could become effective subject to intervenors’ rights to contest preference, discrimination, injustice, or unreasonableness. Appropriate §4 proceedings would still require the pipeline to establish that its plan was fair and reasonable. A cited Transwestern proceeding had used §5, which placed a different burden on challengers, but the court did not regard that particular action as an announced decision to avoid §4 invariably. It expressly declined to determine whether Transwestern’s particular challenges should have triggered §4 because that case and its facts were not before it. (43–44 and n.28.)

## Why review was premature

The court rejected the Commission’s separate argument that petitioners lacked party status because they had requested rehearing rather than intervention. The Commission itself had denied an intervention request and said intervention was unnecessary for a policy statement. Futile intervention requests were not required; the rehearing petitions sufficed in these circumstances. The jurisdictional failure came instead from the order’s effect and the suitability of the record. (45–46.)

Earlier decisions had appeared to distinguish reviewable adjudicative orders from nonreviewable agency rules. The panel explained that intervening decisions favored a practical inquiry rather than that categorical division. Drawing on Abbott Laboratories, it considered fitness for judicial decision and hardship from withholding review. A rule could be reviewable before individual enforcement where its legal questions, evidentiary record, and controlling practical effects supported review. Conversely, calling an announcement a policy statement did not make immediate review impossible in every conceivable situation. (46–48.)

Here, later curtailment proceedings would determine whether particular contracts and deliveries were affected. Unlike the broadcasting regulations considered in CBS, Order 467 had not already caused wholesale contract cancellations or comparable direct business disruption. Moreover, petitioners’ factual-support and contract-abrogation challenges lacked an adequate record; even the contracts allegedly threatened were absent. Neither undue present hardship nor fitness for meaningful substantive review had been established. (41–42, 48–49.)

That jurisdictional conclusion also prevented a NEPA merits ruling. The court summarized the competing environmental positions but left petitioners free to pursue their NEPA claims in district court. Its discussion of potentially broader scrutiny of policy statements assumed that jurisdiction might exist in an appropriate later case; it was not an actual review and approval of this curtailment policy’s wisdom. (40 n.22, 49.)
