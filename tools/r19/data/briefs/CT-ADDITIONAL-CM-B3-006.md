# DeLancey v. Rockingham Farmers' Mutual Fire Insurance Co.
52 N.H. 581 (1873) | Contracts

## Decision and result
The New Hampshire report places this decision in June 1873. Justice Doe wrote the principal opinion directing judgment for the plaintiff on an insurance policy. Justice Hibbard concurred in the result while expressly disagreeing with the stated ground, offering a construction of the application that would avoid the statutory question. The report supplies neither a numerical vote nor a published dissent, and it does not specify the amount of the judgment. The disposition is judgment for the insured, not an order for a new trial to determine whether the insurer committed fraud. (52 N.H. at 581, 585, 595–596.)

## Property, policy, and title description
Randolph DeLancey's policy, dated November 19, 1866, purported to insure his house, clothing, and provisions. The property's title involved the estate of Moses Hobbs, whose widow, three brothers, and sister—the plaintiff's wife—held interests. The widow conveyed her interest to the siblings in February 1866. Two brothers conveyed their interests to DeLancey in April, while a third brother and his wife's interest reached him through later conveyances in April 1867. He testified, over the insurer's exception, that earlier understandings and payments explained these transfers, including his $1,700 payment for the widow's interest. The house's value when insured was $2,883.33; the application estimated $1,400 and the policy insured $1,050. The charter limited the insurance to three fourths of value. (Id. at 581–582.)

The signed application described the property as his and unencumbered. As Doe analyzed it, he held an absolute legal title to only part as a tenant in common, rather than fee-simple unencumbered ownership of the whole. The application contained covenants about descriptions and material misrepresentations and agreed to the charter and bylaws. The policy incorporated the application and annexed the charter, its 1862 amendment, and bylaws. Prominent legible print expressed the company's promise to insure; restrictive material appeared in small, compact, difficult print. (Id. at 585–586.)

An 1855 general statute prevented avoidance for an error, mistake, or misrepresentation unless intentionally and fraudulently made, while allowing the company to offset actual damage it suffered. A 1862 private act amending this insurer's charter stated that a policy would be void if the insured held less than an unencumbered fee and the true title or encumbrances were not stated. DeLancey's inaccurate title description was not intentional and fraudulent. He invoked the general protection; the insurer claimed its later special charter provision exempted it. (Id. at 586–587.)

## The insurer's arguments
The printed report distinguishes counsel's argument from the judicial opinions. The insurer argued that exact title was an express condition, that ownership of part did not satisfy insurance of the whole, and that the general legislation did not displace its later specific amendment. It also contended that reliable title information protected its assessment lien on insured property, an interest distinct from the risk of fire. An innocent error could undermine that security just as effectively as a fraudulent one. Finally, it argued that the insured agreed to the annexed charter and thereby waived any statutory protection that otherwise would apply. These are advocated positions, not the court's findings about fraud or waiver. (Id. at 582–585.)

## Legislative purpose and typography
Doe treated legislative purpose as essential to determining whether the private act silently restored the very problem the general act had remedied. He described public belief that some insurers collected premiums through technically dense documents but evaded payment through hidden conditions, fictional applications, and provisions disavowing their own agents' knowledge. Crucially, he said it was unnecessary to determine whether this public belief was factually warranted: the belief explained the legislation. The passage is therefore not an adjudication that every insurer, or this insurer, engaged in the described practices. (Id. at 587–591.)

Within that account, Doe criticized tiny crowded print and placement of important provisions among obscure appendices. He said there was ground for a premium payer to argue that the print alone was competent evidence for a jury of a fraudulent plot. The assigned note at printed page 669 extracts that observation and the criticism of typography. But the operative controversy was the construction of the statutes, and the ultimate judgment did not turn on a jury's finding of such a plot. Nor did the court announce that any small-print contract is automatically void. (Id. at 588, 595.)

## Harmonizing the acts rather than creating an exemption
Doe reasoned that it was implausible for the legislature, after years of general protection against innocent misstatements, to give one company an unexplained monopoly-like immunity from that protection. Legislative history, the remedial purpose, and constitutional principles favoring common benefit and equal rights supported a presumption against such a discriminatory exemption. He acknowledged that sufficiently explicit words could reveal the contrary legislative intent; the court's task was discovering the enacted meaning, not rewriting a law to match its preferences. When fairly available, a construction consistent with constitutional principles was favored. The opinion uses those principles to construe the statute rather than striking down the amendment as unconstitutional. (Id. at 591–593.)

The private act's title and body referred to amendment of the company's charter, not amendment of the general insurance law. It expressly repealed specified charter sections but omitted a general repeal clause and any express reference to exemption from the 1855 protection. The former charter's statement making signed policies binding “in all cases” itself necessarily operated subject to general insurance law; otherwise even policies invalid for other recognized reasons would always bind the company. The substituted provision likewise had to be read with that qualification, including its treatment of incomplete title. That reading harmonized the two acts despite less-than-ideal drafting. (Id. at 593–595.)

The waiver argument failed for the same reason. Even if the annexed charter became part of the contract, it became part according to its legal construction, which left it subject to the general protection. Section 3 of the 1862 amendment did not exempt this company from section 6 of the 1855 statute. The innocent title description therefore did not invalidate the policy. (Id. at 595.)

## Hibbard's different ground
Hibbard did not agree that Doe's ground sustained the result. He construed the application as insuring DeLancey's undivided half, which he owned in fee simple, rather than the whole house. The jury had found that interest sufficiently valuable for the amount insured. On that reading, the statutory conflict did not arise. He noted that application provisions supporting his construction were absent from the printed introductory facts and declined to reproduce them or expand his disagreement. His short concurrence is complete as printed, but those omitted application terms cannot be supplied as if recovered. (Id. at 596.)
