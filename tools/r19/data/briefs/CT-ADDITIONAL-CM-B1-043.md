# Broadnax v. Ledbetter
100 Tex. 375, 99 S.W. 1111 (Tex. 1907) | Contracts

## Court, certified stage, and answer
Associate Justice Williams wrote the Texas Supreme Court’s February 27, 1907 answer to a question certified by the Court of Civil Appeals for the Third District. The pending appeal concerned dismissal of S. H. Broadnax’s reward claim after demurrers were sustained and he declined to amend. The question was whether knowledge or notice of the reward when the recapture occurred was essential to recovery. The Supreme Court answered yes. It did not itself enter the trial dismissal or announce an appellate affirmance; its operative disposition was an affirmative answer to the certified question. The report supplies no separate writing or numerical vote. (375–378.)

## Allegations and procedural chronology
Broadnax’s petition alleged that A. L. Ledbetter was the Dallas County sheriff and that Holly Vann, convicted of first-degree murder and sentenced to death, escaped from jail on January 25, 1905, while his case was on appeal. Ledbetter publicly offered $500 to anyone who recaptured Vann and returned him to the Dallas County jail or another Texas jail or jailer. Broadnax alleged that, before revocation of the offer, he recaptured and returned Vann that evening and performed all stated conditions. He requested $500 and costs after payment was refused. These were pleading allegations reproduced in the certificate, not factual findings after a merits trial. (376–377.)

The petition did not allege that Broadnax knew or had notice of the reward while recapturing and returning Vann. Ledbetter demurred on that omission. The trial court sustained the demurrers; Broadnax declined to amend, and the court dismissed his case with costs. The appellate court certified the legal sufficiency question rather than supply a finding about what Broadnax actually knew. The decision therefore establishes the necessity of the missing allegation, not an evidentiary determination that he was affirmatively proved ignorant. (377.)

## Parties’ arguments
As reported in the original report’s counsel section, Broadnax argued that a public reward was a conditional promise binding when someone performed its stated conditions before revocation. Because the reward itself had not made knowledge an express condition, he maintained that knowledge was unnecessary. Ledbetter answered that liability was contractual and that knowledge of the offer when performing was essential to acceptance. The court acknowledged conflicting decisions in other states and selected the latter position. (375, 377.)

## Acceptance and consideration require action on the offer
The court reasoned that no general rule independently imposed payment of this privately offered reward. An enforceable obligation had to arise through contract. A promise or offer alone did not create the contract: the performer had to accept, which a reward offer permitted through the requested service. Someone ignorant of the offer could perform the same physical acts, but could not thereby act in acceptance of that offer. Performance undertaken without reference to the promise supplied neither the necessary assent nor the consideration contemplated by the offer. (377.)

The court rejected two contrary explanations. Describing a reward as a bounty or gratuity did not provide a legal basis to force someone to pay a promised gift. Nor was benefit to the offeror enough: requested acts could be valuable even without inducing a legally binding exchange, and their value was not the test of contractual responsibility. For this liability the promise had to induce action, connecting assent and consideration. (377–378.)

## Knowledge, motive, and the public-reward reservation
Knowledge of an offer and a performer’s particular motive are distinct. The court acknowledged that motive might not be controlling and explained that authorities discussing motive did not dispense with the need for a contractual obligation. It did not decide the casebook’s later hypotheticals about learning of an offer after an initial capture, releasing and recapturing a prisoner, or acting primarily to protect a friend. Those questions elaborate on doctrine; they were not facts submitted in this certificate. (378; assigned printed 205.)

The court also rejected a supposed public policy of encouraging crime detection as a basis for enforcing unknown private promises. An offer unknown to the performer could not stimulate that conduct, and the judiciary could not require individual citizens to fund a public policy without a legal obligation. It expressly reserved a different possibility: a government reward authorized by law might confer an entitlement independent of contract even if the claimant lacked knowledge. That was a hypothetical distinction, not a holding that all public rewards require or dispense with knowledge. Ledbetter’s liability as an individual promisor required a contract. The certified question was answered in the affirmative. (378.)
