# Biden v. Missouri

**595 U.S. 87 (2022) — Supreme Court of the United States — January 13, 2022.** Per curiam; **5–4**. Justice Thomas dissented, joined by Alito, Gorsuch, and Barrett; Alito separately dissented with the same three joining him. The joint instrument resolves Missouri application No. 21A240 and Louisiana application No. 21A241. Assigned account: November second casebook, physical PDF page 37, printed page 981. Pinpoints use the separately numbered official slip writings.

The Court stayed two district-court injunctions blocking a COVID-19 vaccination condition for staff of facilities participating in Medicare and Medicaid. Congress had authorized conditions protecting patients' health and safety, and the Court considered this infection-control measure within that authority. It also rejected the administrative-law objections supporting the preliminary injunctions. The order allowed enforcement while appellate proceedings continued; it was not a final merits judgment terminating those cases. (Per curiam 4–10.)

## Funding condition and litigation

The Secretary of Health and Human Services administered the Medicare and Medicaid programs and their provider-participation conditions. Congress granted general rulemaking authority for efficient administration, together with category-specific power to impose requirements necessary for patients' health and safety. Existing conditions already addressed infection prevention and control, employee qualifications and training, clinical practices, and other aspects of safe healthcare. (Per curiam 2–3, 6.)

On November 5, 2021, the Secretary issued an interim final rule requiring covered facilities to ensure staff vaccination. It required medical and religious exemptions and excluded full-time teleworkers. Noncompliant facilities risked penalties, denial of payment for new admissions, and eventual removal from the programs. The Secretary found that unvaccinated staff increased transmission risks to particularly vulnerable patients, deterred patients from seeking needed treatment, and contributed to staffing disruptions through infection and exposure. He invoked good cause to dispense with advance notice and comment, citing the Delta variant and approaching winter. (Per curiam 3–4.)

Missouri-led and Louisiana-led groups of States sued separately. The Eastern District of Missouri enjoined enforcement November 29, and the Western District of Louisiana did so November 30. The Eighth and Fifth Circuits respectively refused the Government's requests to stay those injunctions. The Government then sought Supreme Court stays, and the Court heard expedited argument. The object of these applications was the **injunctions**, rather than a request to halt the vaccination rule. (Per curiam 4, 10.)

## Patient safety and statutory authority

The States urged a narrow reading under which the statutory provisions authorized technical program administration rather than this vaccination condition. The Court rejected the depiction of the Secretary as merely a bookkeeper. Longstanding participation conditions addressed the provision of safe medical care, including examinations, transplantation procedures, delegation of clinical work, staff training, and infection control. Missouri conceded the Secretary could require gloves, instrument sterilization, and handwashing. The Court regarded protection against transmission of COVID to patients as another application of the same mission. (Per curiam 5–7.)

The absence of a previous CMS vaccine requirement did not make the policy unexpected. The pandemic's scale was unprecedented, and healthcare workers already commonly faced vaccination requirements imposed elsewhere. The Secretary explained that existing state requirements helped account for CMS's earlier practice. Support from medical and public-health organizations reinforced the characterization of the measure as a predictable health-and-safety condition, rather than a departure from the agency's purpose. (Per curiam 7–8.)

The Court acknowledged that five facility categories lacked express health-and-safety language in their particular statutes. Those facilities employed less than three percent of covered workers; their provisions could also be read to incorporate comparable authority. The Court emphasized the rule's express severability provision rather than letting those categories determine treatment of the remaining facilities. That reasoning in the sole judicial footnote should not be converted into a declaration that every category's statute used identical language. (Per curiam 5 n.*.)

## Administrative-law objections

The Court also rejected the contention that the interim rule was arbitrary and capricious. The Secretary examined evidence and explained vaccination rather than testing, inclusion of previously infected workers, and departure from encouragement alone. He considered possible staffing losses, including rural shortages. Review asked whether the agency acted within a zone of reasonableness, rather than allowing courts to select their preferred evidence or policy. (Per curiam 8.)

For good cause, the Secretary identified an immediate benefit from acting before winter: reducing infections, hospitalizations, and deaths. The Court did not view the two months spent preparing a 73-page rule as delay inconsistent with that justification. Consultation with state agencies could occur during deferred notice and comment. A statutory impact analysis for small rural hospitals applied to a notice of proposed rulemaking followed by a final rule, rather than this interim process. Finally, construing the prohibition on federal control of medical services and personnel to invalidate this condition would also undermine the many accepted participation conditions. (Per curiam 8–9.)

The opinion closed by distinguishing two principles: emergencies do not create powers Congress withheld, but neither do they justify restricting authority Congress already gave. Here the second principle controlled. This reasoning arose in deciding entitlement to interim relief and should not be presented as a final adjudication of every potential challenge. (Per curiam 9–10.)

## Two dissents

Thomas would deny the stays because the Government had not strongly demonstrated statutory authority. General administration clauses bore at most a tangential connection to requiring millions of workers to receive an unwanted medical procedure. Facility-specific statutes did not supply a single universal delegation: five lacked the relevant language, others used catchall definitions that context limited to requirements similar to their enumerated administrative conditions. Even a long-term-care infection-control provision concerned sanitation and could not authorize a rule for every facility type. Regulations already adopted by the agency could not themselves prove what Congress authorized. (Thomas 1–7.)

Thomas also invoked clear-statement principles for powers of vast economic and political importance and substantial changes in the federal-state balance. Vaccine mandates ordinarily belonged to state police power; Congress could explicitly authorize federal vaccination when it chose. His disagreement concerned legal authority, not vaccine efficacy or importance. Alito, Gorsuch, and Barrett joined that complete eight-page dissent. (Thomas 7–8.)

Alito, joined by Thomas, Gorsuch, and Barrett, agreed on the authority objection but separately challenged the procedure. Notice and comment protect regulated people by requiring agencies to hear and consider their evidence before making binding rules. CMS had to show specific harm from the delay required for that process. In his view, months of vaccine availability, two months between announcement and issuance, and an additional compliance interval undermined the claimed urgency. The Government could not shift its affirmative good-cause burden to challengers by arguing that nobody proved prejudice. He feared that accepting such justification would broadly weaken procedural safeguards. (Alito 1–5.)

## Exact order and assigned scope

The Court **granted both Government applications**. It stayed Missouri's November 29 injunction pending Eighth Circuit proceedings and timely certiorari, and Louisiana's November 30 injunction pending Fifth Circuit proceedings and timely certiorari. For each, denial of certiorari would terminate the stay automatically; a grant would continue it until the Supreme Court sent down its judgment. Four Justices joined both dissents, producing the 5–4 result. (Per curiam 10; Thomas 1; Alito 1.)

The assigned account briefly contrasts this result with the same-day OSHA order and notes that this Court opinion did not invoke the major-questions doctrine. The full source supplies the different statutory mission, funding condition, procedural rulings, category-specific footnote, and dissents. Both cases involved interim judicial relief, but in opposite directions: here the Court stayed injunctions against the rule; in the OSHA matter it stayed the rule itself. The two application dockets remain separately identified within this single report.
