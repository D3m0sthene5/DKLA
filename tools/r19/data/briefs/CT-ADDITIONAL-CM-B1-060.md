# Perry v. Command Performance
913 F.2d 99 (3d Cir. Aug. 30, 1990) | Contracts

## Decision and posture

The Third Circuit vacated summary judgment for a beauty salon and remanded Edith Perry’s action for further proceedings. The district court had treated the alleged racial refusal of service as harassment after a contract formed when an appointment was scheduled. The record did not support that assumption. Even if an appointment created a contract, evidence might show that its terms themselves were discriminatory. Anne E. Thompson, a district judge sitting by designation, wrote for the panel of Sloviter, Mansmann, and Thompson; no separate writing or divided vote was reported. Costs were charged to the appellee. (99–100, 101–102.)

## Incident and disputed evidence

On October 12, 1987, Perry’s husband called Command Performance at the King of Prussia Mall to arrange a noon wash-and-set appointment. Perry, a Black woman, had used the salon more than five times, with different operators. Helene Kugler, who had previously done her hair, was scheduled to serve her that day. After Perry arrived, Kugler said she had a bad cold and asked whether another operator could serve her. Perry agreed. (100.)

According to Perry’s complaint and deposition, Beth Abbott loudly refused when Kugler asked her to take the appointment. Abbott said that she did not do Black people’s hair and did not deal with Black people. Perry said she became distraught, cried, called mall security, and left with her husband. She claimed subsequent hives, insomnia, and psychiatric treatment. These allegations and testimony were reviewed in the summary-judgment posture, not accepted as findings after trial. (100.)

The record also contained a dispute about whether Kugler offered to perform the service after Abbott’s outburst. Command Performance later sent an apology and an offer of a free wash and set. At oral argument, uncertainty remained about whether the salon or Abbott was the proper defendant. None of these matters was resolved into an appellate determination of liability. (100 n.1.)

## Historical statutory framework and lower-court reasoning

Perry sued under 42 U.S.C. § 1981 and Pennsylvania intentional-infliction-of-emotional-distress law. In the statutory framework then governing, Patterson v. McLean Credit Union distinguished racial refusal to enter contracts, or offers on discriminatory terms, from harassment arising after formation. The district court concluded that the telephone appointment created a racially neutral contract and that Abbott’s subsequent conduct therefore fell outside § 1981. The Third Circuit reviewed the summary judgment de novo, with facts and inferences viewed favorably to Perry. (100–101.)

The appellate court did not accept Perry’s argument that Patterson’s general discussion of § 1981 was confined to employment merely because it referred to Title VII. At the same time, it expressly declined to decide Patterson’s effect on earlier discrimination cases involving service establishments, and did not decide the scope of Patterson within employment. Its discussion of discrimination in enforcement of contracts was also identified as not germane to Perry’s theory. These reservations prevent treating its cited examples as additional holdings. (101 nn.2–3.)

## Formation could not be assumed

The district court had no evidentiary basis for determining that a contract necessarily formed at the scheduling stage. An appointment could instead be an invitation to negotiate, with acceptance accompanying performance and a payment obligation arising only when service began. If so, Abbott’s refusal might have occurred during formation and amounted to a refusal to contract. The court offered this as a possible account requiring factual development, not a categorical holding that every salon appointment is legally nonbinding. (101–102 & n.4.)

The remand permitted consideration of industry practice and the parties’ expectations. Only after finding an existing contract when Perry appeared could the district court characterize the conduct as post-contract behavior. The significance of the inquiry was practical: timing determined whether the alleged discrimination implicated the particular formation protection recognized by Patterson. The court’s reference to Roberts, involving race recorded on a retail check, illustrated another transaction in which formation timing required evidence; it did not supply missing facts about this salon. (102 & n.4.)

## Discriminatory terms and other remand questions

A finding of earlier formation would not automatically end the claim. The parties also had to be allowed to present evidence whether the agreement was implicitly conditioned on the availability of an operator willing to serve a Black patron. The record could support an inference that a white patron with the same appointment would receive a replacement operator’s services or would at least not be refused because of race. Such a discriminatory term could violate the right to make contracts even under Patterson’s narrower interpretation. The appellate court described a possible factual conclusion rather than finding that the condition actually existed. (102.)

The district court had also not considered whether Abbott’s conduct was authorized or reflected a salon policy for which Command Performance could be liable. Nor had it determined intentional discrimination, which the opinion identified as necessary under § 1981. These issues remained open on remand. The court mentioned Perry’s state tort claim but did not decide its elements or make a separate merits holding awarding tort damages. (100, 102.)

## Result, assigned material, and historical limit

The order was vacated and the case remanded for proceedings consistent with the opinion, with costs against Command Performance. Perry obtained reopening of the case, not an appellate liability judgment or damages award. (102.)
