# National Labor Relations Board v. Wyman-Gordon Co.
394 U.S. 759 (1969) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Judgment and competing rationales

The Supreme Court reversed the First Circuit and remanded to the district court with directions to reinstate its judgment compelling Wyman-Gordon to supply employee names and addresses for a representation election. Seven Justices supported enforcement, but disagreed about the validity of the earlier Excelsior requirement on which the Board relied. Fortas, joined by Chief Justice Warren, Stewart, and White, regarded Excelsior's generally applicable prospective command as improperly adopted without APA rulemaking, but sustained the specific order addressed to Wyman-Gordon. Black, joined by Brennan and Marshall, considered Excelsior a valid exercise of adjudicatory power and concurred in the result. Douglas and Harlan separately dissented and would affirm the First Circuit. (761, 769–775, 780–783.)

The substantive disclosure requirement and the Board's subpoena authority received seven votes: Black's opinion expressly agreed with Parts II and III of Fortas's opinion. The criticism of Excelsior's adoption drew agreement from the four-Justice plurality and the two dissenters, but those Justices disagreed on its consequences for this case. The result should therefore not be described as seven Justices adopting Fortas's rule/order distinction or invalidating all agency policymaking through adjudication. (763–769, 769–770, 775–783.)

## Elections, the earlier Excelsior decision, and litigation

On a Boilermakers petition, the Board directed an election among Wyman-Gordon's production and maintenance employees. They could choose either of two unions or no union. The Board ordered the employer to provide the names and addresses of eligible voters so the unions could contact them. Wyman-Gordon refused. Both unions lost the election; the Board sustained their objections based on the missing list and ordered a new election. Wyman-Gordon again refused the list requirement, and the Board subpoenaed the list or personnel and payroll records containing the necessary information. (761.)

The Board sought enforcement of the subpoena or a mandatory injunction in the District of Massachusetts. The district court ordered compliance, 270 F. Supp. 280 (1967). The First Circuit reversed, 397 F.2d 394 (1968), reasoning that the order rested on an invalidly adopted general rule. The Supreme Court granted review amid a circuit conflict and decided the case on April 23, 1969. Its disposition restored the district court's enforcement judgment rather than sending the question back for a new discretionary Board decision. (762 & n.1, 769.)

Excelsior Underwear Inc., 156 N.L.R.B. 1236 (1966), had considered losing unions' objections to elections at two companies that refused to provide voter lists. The Board invited selected employer organizations and trade unions to brief and argue the general disclosure issue. It announced that employers would file eligible employees' names and addresses with the regional director within seven days after approval of an election agreement or direction of an election; the director would provide the information to the parties. Failure to comply could justify setting aside an election upon proper objections. But the Board declined to apply that requirement to the original parties. It applied only to elections directed or consented to more than thirty days after the decision. Black understood that prospective limit as protecting the companies' justified reliance on earlier decisions refusing compelled disclosure. (762–763, 773–774.)

## Fortas plurality: invalid general rule, valid particular order

Section 6 of the NLRA authorized necessary rules in the manner prescribed by the APA. Fortas emphasized the APA's definition of a rule as an agency statement of general or particular applicability and future effect, and the publication, participation, explanatory, and publication-of-adoption requirements of § 553. Selected amici and briefing in Excelsior did not substitute for notice to all interested persons through the Federal Register. Nor had the adopted command been published there. The plurality treated disclosure as substantive, outside the exceptions for interpretative rules, policy statements, or agency procedure. (763–764 & n.2.)

The Solicitor General did not deny that APA rulemaking procedures had been omitted. Instead, the government argued that the requirement was valid because it emerged from a proceeding satisfying adjudicatory requirements. Fortas answered that the Board could decide in an adjudicatory case whether the particular employer had to furnish a list, but Excelsior had not imposed that duty on its own parties. It instead purported to create a general future command. Adjudications could formulate policies and serve as precedents guiding future agency action; that did not automatically make their announced policies legislative rules binding the public without further agency action. The plurality's notes distinguished earlier precedents approving Board case decisions from approval of legislative rulemaking without the mandated procedure. (764–766 & nn.3–4.)

Wyman-Gordon, however, had received its own express direction to provide the list as part of the order for this election. That direction required delivery within seven days and made the information available to all election parties. Fortas considered it a valid adjudicatory order, notwithstanding its citation to Excelsior. Without the specific direction, Wyman-Gordon would not have been compelled by a statute or validly adopted rule to supply the information. The plurality thus enforced the particular order while rejecting the earlier command's independent force as a legislative rule. (766 & n.5.)

Harlan argued that Chenery required remand because the agency's stated ground was invalid. In note 6, Fortas refused a remand that he regarded as a useless formality. Chenery involved an agency's use of incorrect standards in a complex factual matter, with possible alternative rationales left unresolved. Here, he considered the disclosure order's substance incontestable and the result on remand certain, whether the Board used a rule or an order. This was a specific futility rationale, disputed by Harlan, not permission to affirm every agency result on a ground the reviewing court invents. (766–767 n.6.)

## Disclosure's merits and subpoena enforcement

Wyman-Gordon also contested the requirement's substantive validity. Fortas invoked the Board's broad authority to ensure a fair and free choice of bargaining representatives. Lists helped create an informed electorate and gave unions access to employees comparable to management's existing access. Weighing employees' possible burdens from union solicitation against those election interests belonged to the Board. Black expressly agreed that disclosure was valid on its merits. (767 & n.7, 769.)

The employer separately argued that the lists were not evidence subject to subpoena under NLRA § 11. That provision allowed access to evidence related to a matter under investigation or in question and authorized subpoenas for evidence in the proceeding or investigation. The Court accepted evidence as including not only proof introduced at a hearing, but books, records, and papers assisting the Board's investigation. The names and addresses fell within that scope. The First Circuit had not reached this independent objection; the Supreme Court resolved it and reinstated enforcement. (768–769 & n.8.)

## Black's concurrence: prospective relief remains adjudication

Black objected both to the attack on Excelsior and to enforcing what the plurality considered an illegally adopted requirement. He regarded such enforcement reasoning as weakening judicial oversight of statutory procedural compliance. His route to the same judgment was that no adoption violation had occurred: Excelsior genuinely adjudicated a controversy, and its prospective disposition remained an incident of that adjudication. (769–770 & n.1.)

Rulemaking and adjudication were distinct but closely related powers. Deciding a case could require a new doctrine drawn from the governing statute; precedential effects could guide future conduct much like a rule. Where a matter fit either mode, the choice lay primarily within the agency's informed discretion, as Chenery II explained. Black's procedural note specified that worker-representation certifications were exempt from the APA's formal adjudication requirements; the pertinent NLRA procedures governed those proceedings. His assertion of procedural compliance therefore did not impose all APA trial requirements on representation elections. (770–772 & nn.2–3.)

Excelsior resolved whether the losing unions' objections justified setting aside particular elections. The Board rejected that remedy because it declined to upset justified reliance, while explaining why future disclosure should be required. That was a real disposition of the pending cases rather than policy invented in the abstract. Prospectivity did not erase the adjudicatory character of the decision. An opposite rule would force the agency either to impose an unfair retroactive requirement or duplicate its completed inquiry through rulemaking. The fairness of retroactivity often became apparent only after full consideration, so Black rejected requiring the agency to commit to retroactive application before it began adjudicating. (772–775.)

## Douglas and Harlan dissents

Douglas accepted that the Board could decide particular cases conventionally, but thought it had chosen a general policy governing all future elections and was bound to follow rulemaking procedures. A thirty-day effective-date delay did not replace notice and public participation. He emphasized that broad participation exposed agencies to information beyond their own assumptions, citing revisions after public comment on highway rules and the need to hear groups poorly represented in agency deliberation. His notes discussed statutory exceptions and criticized the Board's historical avoidance of rulemaking. For him, permitting an agency to announce a general rule and then compel compliance through individual orders defeated the procedural discipline Congress required. He would affirm the First Circuit. (775–780 & nn.1–2.)

Harlan likewise distinguished formulation of an order from a future rule never applied to the original parties. A policy change substantial enough to make retroactivity unfair was precisely the sort of change benefiting from broad rulemaking participation. Calling a later enforcement action adjudication did not repair the invalid adoption. His first note treated the disclosure requirement as substantive rather than an exempt procedural rule. (780–781 & n.1.)

He also applied Chenery I to the Board's actual reasoning. The regional official refused to reconsider the employer's objections because Excelsior bound him; the Board denied review because no substantial issue warranted it. Once that premise was rejected, a court could not substitute confidence that the agency would reach the same result. At a minimum, the Board should reconsider the competing interests; Harlan would go further and require rulemaking because the Board itself had concluded its change should not operate retroactively. The Court could not know what renewed deliberation would produce. He would affirm the First Circuit rather than restore enforcement on a judicially supplied basis. (782–783 & n.2.)
