# The Estate of Lucille Osborn v. Michael J. Kemp
2009 WL 2586783; C.A. No. 3171-VCP (Del. Ch. Aug. 20, 2009) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
Vice Chancellor Parsons issued this post-trial memorandum opinion in the Delaware Court of Chancery. He found a disputed handwritten property-purchase agreement authentic, construed it to require an additional $50,000 payment after twenty years of monthly payments, and granted the occupant’s counterclaim for specific performance on specified terms. He directed dismissal with prejudice of the estate’s complaint and submission of a proposed final judgment. This is the 2009 trial-court decision quoted through Snow Phipps in the assignment, with its own evidentiary findings and interpretation. (Caption; opinion 11–13, 23–27, 31–33.)

## Facts and chronology
Lucille Osborn owned a two-apartment beach house in Slaughter Beach, Delaware, while living in Wilmington. In November 1984, electrician Michael Kemp rented the upstairs apartment for $275 monthly plus utilities. Osborn continued to use the downstairs quarters during vacation visits. Her first husband died in February 1985, leaving her responsible for two houses. According to a handwritten agreement dated April 16, 1985, Kemp undertook twenty years of $275 monthly payments plus utilities to purchase the identified property for $50,000. The one-sentence document appeared to bear both signatures and a notary’s signature and seal. Only a photocopy survived. (4–5.)

Kemp and his cohabitant, Roxanne Danburg, made the monthly payments through July 2005, sometimes late and often describing them as rent. Osborn also treated the payments as rent on her 2004 tax return and declared herself the fee-simple owner in a 1986 antenuptial agreement. Over the occupancy, Kemp claimed to have spent about $11,000 improving the house. His receipts were largely missing; the estate disputed some work and said he had been reimbursed. The court ultimately found meaningful improvements, without establishing the claimed expenditure as a precise damages amount. (6–7, 19–20, 28.)

The twenty-year period ended in April 2005. Kemp testified that he realized by July that he had completed the required payments, discussed overpayment and later utilities with Osborn, and sought a deed in August. He stopped making payments after July. In May 2006, Osborn became unable to manage her affairs following a fall and progressive dementia. Her niece, Sharon Gillespie, took over. When they met in August 2006, Kemp did not disclose the agreement; he said he regarded it as a private matter with Osborn. In January 2007, after a notice demanding overdue rent and utilities, he produced the photocopy and asserted purchase rights. Those contested conversations and explanations were evaluated as evidence, rather than treated as undisputed acknowledgments by the estate. (7–9, 30–31.)

## Procedure and arguments
Suit began in August 2007 through Gillespie as attorney-in-fact. The owner sought an injunction, a declaration defeating Kemp’s ownership claim, and restitution. Kemp added his specific-performance counterclaim in August 2008. Trial occurred October 29, 2008, followed by permitted affidavits on repairs, briefing, and argument. After Osborn died in December 2008, her estate was substituted in January 2009. (3, 11.)

The estate challenged authenticity, definiteness, fairness, and Kemp’s financial ability, and argued that his delay until after Osborn’s incapacity barred relief by laches. Kemp claimed that the monthly payments already satisfied the purchase price. Alternatively, he said he could obtain financing if another $50,000 was required. He denied unreasonable delay. (12, 26, 28–29.)

## Governing standards and authentication
Specific performance required clear and convincing proof of a valid real-estate purchase contract, the claimant’s readiness, willingness, and ability to perform, and a balance of equities favoring enforcement. The estate’s ordinary claims would generally use a preponderance standard, but their issues overlapped the counterclaim subject to the higher burden. (12–13.)

The original-document rule did not automatically exclude the photocopy. The court considered the duplicate rule and the exception for lost originals, finding that Osborn probably kept the original and supplied the copy, with no persuasive evidence that Kemp destroyed it in bad faith. Even Gillespie’s possible accidental disposal of it did not establish intentional destruction. The court admitted and authenticated the copy based on the notary’s recollection, credible testimony, the probable document-handling practice, and the parties’ long course of conduct, including the unchanged monthly rent. (14–16.)

The notary’s seal did not itself supply a statutory presumption of genuine signatures: the document lacked the jurisdiction, office identification, and required acknowledgment language. Her testimony nevertheless had evidentiary value, though she could not recall who appeared or how many signatures she notarized. The handwriting expert’s doubt was inconclusive because he had only a copy, and the notary identified her own signature and seal. Considered together with performance and improvements, the evidence met the clear-and-convincing standard for authenticity. The court did not equate notarization alone with conclusive validity. (17–21.)

## Formation, price, and ability to perform
The court found intent to be bound, definite essential terms, and consideration. The property was identified, settlement followed the twenty-year performance period, and the two competing price readings did not make the transaction wholly indefinite. For consideration, existence mattered independently of a judicial assessment that the deal was financially optimal. At formation, Osborn obtained a long income stream, a tenant capable of maintaining the house, and continued downstairs use while retaining legal ownership. Those benefits supported enforcement; asserted inadequacy alone did not establish inequity or overreaching. (21–23.)

The court rejected Kemp’s contention that $66,000 in total monthly payments, less implied interest and carrying charges, had already purchased the property. Such a reading deprived the separately stated $50,000 price of meaningful work. Historical mortgage-rate evidence also weakened the claim that the monthly stream equaled a $50,000 financed purchase. The court additionally treated the price term as ambiguous and construed it against Kemp, the undisputed drafter. Note 97 explains that both proposed readings seemed reasonable given the informality, absence of counsel, and his limited education, but the court resolved that perceived ambiguity in favor of another $50,000 at purchase. This describes the trial court’s reasoning at this stage. (23–26 & nn. 95–97.)

The estate objected that financing was not already arranged. The court took judicial notice that financing probably could be obtained using the property as collateral and gave Kemp a reasonable opportunity to obtain it. This was conditional relief, not a finding that a lender had made a binding commitment. Closing was to occur within ninety days, absent another agreement connected with the final judgment. Note 103 states that failure to obtain financing within the period would exhaust specific performance and cause the contract to be deemed expired. (26–27 & n. 103; 33.)

## Equities and laches
The additional price mitigated the estate’s interest-related concerns, and the evidence did not show incapacity at formation or undue influence after the husband’s death. The owner had received the promised stream without objection for almost the entire twenty-year period, while the property had become Kemp’s long-standing home and he had made more than nominal improvements. The equities thus favored performance. The later dementia did not itself establish that the 1985 bargain was invalid. (27–28.)

Laches required inequitable, unreasonable delay with an adverse change in circumstances. The court rejected the defense. Cessation of payments after the twenty-year term gave at least indirect notice of the claimed purchase position while Osborn remained competent. She could have investigated the missing payments but did not. The later delay in exposing the precise dispute was attributable equally to both sides. The court did not find its suggested explanation—that she recognized matured purchase rights—to be the only possible explanation for her silence. (29–31.)

## Remedy and exact disposition
Specific performance placed both sides as nearly as possible in the position of the contemplated April 16, 2005 purchase. The estate could not collect additional rent for subsequent use merely because it had unsuccessfully disputed the purchase rights. But it would have received the $50,000 purchase funds in 2005, so the buyer had to pay that principal plus interest at the legal rate as of April 16, 2005, compounded quarterly until settlement. He also had to reimburse the same utilities for which he had previously been responsible. Danburg’s July 2005 payment was credited first to utilities and then, if a balance remained, to interest. The interest adjustment thus compensated the seller’s delayed receipt of the price within a remedy benefiting the buyer; it was not an additional rent award. (31–32.)

Kemp agreed to pay deed preparation and recording costs. With the contract silent, the estate owed transfer taxes under the cited statute; other closing costs followed the stated Kent County customs. The opinion required closing within ninety days unless otherwise agreed in connection with the final judgment, directed specific performance for Kemp and dismissal with prejudice of all complaint claims, and required his counsel to propose a final judgment within ten days. Each side bore its own fees and expenses except prevailing-party costs available to Kemp under Rule 54(d). No separate concurrence or dissent accompanies Parsons’s opinion. (32–33.)
