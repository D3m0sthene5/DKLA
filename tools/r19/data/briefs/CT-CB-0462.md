# Cundick v. Broadbent
383 F.2d 157 (10th Cir. 1967) | Contracts | Mental capacity, expert evidence, and alleged overreaching

## The sale of a rancher's property

Darwin Cundick, a fifty-nine-year-old Wyoming sheep rancher, had previously sold lamb crops to J.R. Broadbent. In September 1963 they met to discuss another lamb sale, but the meeting produced a one-page handwritten agreement selling all of Cundick's ranching properties. Only the two men attended, and neither testified at trial about what happened. The later dissent emphasized that Broadbent wrote the document; the majority declined to treat suggestions about the meeting as proof of misconduct. The sale included livestock and equipment, base range land, and shares in a development company. (383 F.2d at 159, 162, 165.)

Mr. and Mrs. Cundick took the handwritten agreement to their Salt Lake City lawyer, who expanded it into an eleven-page document. The parties signed in his office with Mrs. Cundick present. The lawyer testified that he explained the terms and that everyone apparently understood them. On September 23 the lamb crop was delivered; Cundick arranged the delivery, worked the scales with Broadbent, and tallied weights. In October he wrote proposing a date to deliver the remaining sheep. When Broadbent requested a postponement, he agreed to pay the resulting expenses. At the eventual delivery, the men and their workers sorted and counted ewes by age. These acts later became central to the majority's assessment of his capacity. (Id. at 162.)

An October 18 office memorandum recorded a subsequent accommodation: Cundick would waive a year's interest on the unpaid balance, while Broadbent would add $5 per acre to the land price and preserve specified oil, gas, mineral, and related revenues for the seller. The lawyer prepared a supplemental agreement signed on October 24. Cundick continued signing assignments, receipts, proxies, and other implementing documents until February 1964, by which time the price had been paid and the sale was substantially completed. (Id. at 162–163.)

The memorandum also reported that Broadbent offered to cancel the entire transaction and that Cundick refused. The full opinion's second majority footnote qualifies that account. Witnesses reported Mrs. Cundick objecting to the deal, Broadbent offering to return it while the outfit remained intact, and her responding that they would proceed. Neither Mrs. Cundick nor Broadbent testified personally. Stipulations established that she would deny an offer to rescind the whole agreement and describe an offer limited to the base lands, while he would say the offer was made. A precise account must preserve that conflict rather than present a comprehensive cancellation offer as admitted. The court also rejected an objection to the lawyer's use of his memoranda because the identified materials did not embody protected confidential communications. (Id. at 162–163; majority nn.2–3.)

## The rescission suit and medical evidence

In March 1964, Irma Cundick, appointed guardian ad litem for purposes of suit, brought this diversity action to set the sale aside. She alleged that her husband lacked capacity and that Broadbent knowingly exploited his condition by falsely representing the price as fair. The complaint offered restoration, which Broadbent refused. This litigation appointment did not mean Darwin had previously been adjudicated incompetent or placed under a general guardianship governing the sale. The majority expressly noted the absence of such an adjudication. (Id. at 159–160.)

After a bench trial, Judge Kerr found that the documents were prepared by Cundick's lawyer, signed with his wife's approval, and performed over several months without any contemporaneous complaint about incapacity. He found competent conduct, no deception or overreaching, and an insufficiently prompt election to rescind. The resulting judgment dismissed the action. On appeal, Irma argued alternatively that total incompetence made the contract void and incapable of ratification, or that lesser mental infirmity combined with knowing overreaching made it voidable and still subject to rescission. (Id. at 159.)

The medical proof strongly supported her position. A family doctor had diagnosed depressive psychosis in December 1960 and referred Darwin to a psychiatrist, apparently receiving information about shock treatment. After a gap of more than two years, the doctor treated him repeatedly for physical illnesses, including a March 1964 heart attack, without further discussion or treatment of a mental condition. Examinations ordered after the lawsuit revealed frontal-lobe atrophy attributed to premature arteriosclerosis. Two neurosurgeons retrospectively concluded that in September 1963 he had poor judgment and could not manage his affairs. A psychologist likewise considered him incapable of important business transactions. No medical witness contradicted them. Lay witnesses described personality changes and difficulty making ranching decisions. (Id. at 161–162.)

## Capacity and the weight of competing proof

Chief Judge Murrah distinguished complete inability to understand from weakness making a person susceptible to exploitation. The majority described the modern tendency away from treating every incompetent person's agreement as absolutely void and incapable of ratification. For a person outside guardianship, equitable principles govern avoidance; even an agreement invalid for an inability to understand can be ratified by the representative or during a lucid interval. Mere physical or mental weakness does not establish legal incapacity. The relevant question is whether the person had sufficient reason to understand the nature and effect of the particular transaction, including the property, its disposition, the recipient, and the consideration. Average intelligence or sound business judgment is not itself required. (Id. at 159–160.)

If a person can contract but mental weakness makes him vulnerable to an unscrupulous counterparty, the claim instead requires proof of fraud or overreaching. The challenger bears the burden of establishing the material incapacity or fraud at the time of the bargain. Accordingly, the existence of a diagnosis did not alone settle either theory. The appeal attacked the support for findings made after trial, and the majority accepted that supported findings bound the appellate court. (Id. at 160–161.)

The majority did not declare that a trial judge may simply ignore uncontradicted experts. It said such testimony must be honored in the absence of countervailing expert or nonexpert evidence. But observed conduct and transaction circumstances can supply that counterweight. Its first footnote differentiated direct observations at or near the relevant time from opinions based entirely on someone else's account. A trial judge ordinarily occupies the best position to assess the combined record. (Id. at 161–162 and majority n.1.)

The majority found sufficient contrary evidence here: lawyer-assisted negotiations, explanation and execution of detailed terms, deliveries, weighing and sorting sheep, requests changing delivery dates, a favorable amendment, and months of implementing documents. The lack of discussion of incapacity among family and friends, particularly in Broadbent's presence, also supported the finding. The court concluded that the medical evidence, although positive, did not compel a finding that Darwin failed to understand what property he was selling, to whom, and on what terms. (Id. at 162–163.)

## The price and the disputed inference of exploitation

The property was worth substantially more than the price paid according to valuation testimony. Nevertheless, the majority relied on the negotiated increase, a finding of adequate consideration, and evidence that the stock price exceeded prices at which it had sold from 1957 through 1962. It also rejected a proposed inference of fraud from a substantial company dividend paid within the following year. Although Broadbent was a director, the record did not establish that relevant corporate information was unavailable to Cundick or other shareholders. Under the Wyoming law the court applied, a director's purchase of a shareholder's stock did not by itself establish a fiduciary relationship or compel an inference of fraud from nondisclosure. (Id. at 163–164.)

The majority therefore sustained the trial court's conclusion that the amended agreement was not unconscionable, unfair, or inequitable. Because incapacity and overreaching had not been established, it expressly found no occasion to decide whether a voidable contract had actually been ratified. The decision should not be converted into a holding that continued performance necessarily ratified this sale. (Id. at 164.)

## Hill's dissent

Judge Hill found the contrary lay evidence trivial beside the undisputed medical proof. Sorting sheep did not establish competence for a complex sale, and some witnesses admitted they had no occasion to observe Darwin's ability to handle business affairs. The lawyer testified by deposition, so the trial judge enjoyed no special advantage from seeing him testify. Hill read that deposition as showing Mrs. Cundick and the lawyer guiding every material act; their understanding could not substitute for Darwin's own capacity. He also faulted the absence of a specific finding addressing competence on the original contract date. (Id. at 164–165 and dissent n.1.)

The price disparity independently convinced Hill of overreaching. The 2,252 acres sold for $39,510, while an expert valued them at $86,189. The development-company interest sold for $46,750; even the purchaser's witness valued it at $73,743.45, and the seller's witnesses gave valuations exceeding $105,000. A $20,000 dividend became payable within a year, and Broadbent's directorship placed him in a position to know company affairs. Hill would have closely scrutinized this combination of mental weakness and inadequate consideration, even without total incompetence or direct proof of the buyer's awareness. He would reverse and direct judgment for the appellant as requested. (Id. at 165–166.)

## Judgment and source scope

The Tenth Circuit affirmed dismissal, two to one. Murrah wrote the majority joined by Breitenstein; Hill dissented. The opinion issued September 14, 1967; rehearing was denied October 27. Some website headers use the rehearing date as the decision date, so those events must be distinguished. No rescission, restitution award, or new trial was ordered. (Id. at 159, 164–166.)
