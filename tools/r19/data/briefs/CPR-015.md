# Mas v. Perry

**Course:** Civil Procedure

## Citation

Mas v. Perry, 489 F.2d 1396 (5th Cir. 1974), cert. denied, 419 U.S. 842 (1974). United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit. Judge Ainsworth wrote the opinion. Assigned excerpt: casebook, pp. 309–311; complete following citizenship notes, pp. 311–318 (through their conclusion on repository PDF 183). “Perry” in the assignment is this case. Entire judicial opinion, including both footnotes. Full-opinion paragraph locators use the OpenJurist reproduction’s numbered paragraphs 1–18; no unverified reporter-page map is supplied.

## Parties

Jean Paul Mas, a citizen of France, and Judy Mas, an American whose established domicile was Mississippi, were the plaintiffs and appellees. Oliver H. Perry, their landlord and a Louisiana citizen, was the defendant and appellant. The Mases sought damages for Perry’s observation of them through concealed two-way mirrors. On appeal, Perry challenged the federal court’s subject-matter jurisdiction rather than the substantive basis of the verdict. Casebook, pp. 309–310.

## Material Facts

Jean Paul and Judy married at her home in Jackson, Mississippi. Before the marriage, both were graduate assistants at Louisiana State University in Baton Rouge. Jean Paul had been there for approximately nine months and Judy for approximately one year. They pursued coursework and performed teaching duties. Their Louisiana presence therefore involved education and employment, but the citizenship inquiry still required examination of their domicile and intentions. Casebook, p. 309.

After the marriage they returned to Baton Rouge to resume their graduate-assistant work and remained there approximately two more years. They later moved to Park Ridge, Illinois. At trial they intended to return to Baton Rouge while Jean Paul completed his doctorate, but had not decided where they would live afterward. The complete opinion’s footnote 2 expressly states that the complaint was filed within several days of discovery of the surveillance, well before the move to Illinois. The later-move testimony did not determine citizenship at filing; it remained relevant as evidence of a lack of intent to stay permanently in Louisiana. These filing facts appear in the complete opinion, although the assigned excerpt omits the footnote. Casebook, p. 309; full opinion, paragraph 2 and footnote 2 to paragraph 10.

The couple rented an apartment from Perry after returning to Baton Rouge following their marriage. They discovered two-way mirrors in the bedroom and bathroom. Perry had watched them through the mirrors during three of the first four months of their marriage. A jury later awarded $5,000 to Jean Paul and $15,000 to Judy. These are the actual verdict amounts, not the amounts initially placed in controversy. Casebook, p. 309.

Judy’s established domicile was Mississippi. Although she testified that she did not intend to return to her parents’ Mississippi home after marrying, the court found that she had not established a new domicile in Louisiana. She and Jean Paul were there as students and lacked the intention to remain needed for that change. The court’s conclusion relied on the continuation of an existing domicile until a new one is acquired. It did not find that an adult must continue living with, or plan to return to, her parents. Casebook, pp. 310–311.

## Procedural History

The Mases sued in federal district court and tried the case to a jury. At the close of their case, Perry made an oral motion to dismiss for lack of jurisdiction. The district court denied the motion. The jury returned the awards described above, and final judgment was entered. Perry appealed solely on jurisdictional grounds, arguing both that complete diversity had not been established and that Jean Paul’s claim failed the required amount in controversy. Casebook, pp. 309–310. Full footnote 1 clarifies that counsel made the motion immediately before the Mases’ last witness testified; by stipulation approved by the court, it was treated as made at the close of their case. Full opinion, footnote 1 to paragraph 4.

The Fifth Circuit affirmed. It held that Jean Paul’s claim fit the alienage grant and Judy’s claim fit the different-state-citizens grant. Full-opinion addition: the complete decision also rejects the amount challenge; the casebook expressly omits that discussion. Both spouses initially claimed $100,000, and the applicable statutory threshold was then more than $10,000. The eventual $5,000 award to Jean Paul did not show that the original good-faith amount was insufficient. Full opinion, paragraphs 11–16.

## Issue

Was Judy Mas a Louisiana citizen for diversity purposes because she lived and studied there, or had her Mississippi domicile continued? Did marriage to a French citizen automatically change her domicile or citizenship? With Jean Paul a French citizen and Perry a Louisiana citizen, did the parties meet § 1332’s citizenship requirements when the complaint was filed? The complete opinion also asks whether Jean Paul’s recovery below the then-applicable statutory minimum defeated jurisdiction. Casebook, pp. 309–311; full opinion, paragraphs 11–16.

## Rule and Holding

For a natural person to be a citizen of a U.S. state under § 1332, the person must be a United States citizen and domiciled in that state. Domicile, not mere residence, controls. Changing domicile requires taking up residence in a new place and intending to remain there. Until a new domicile is established, the old one persists. Citizenship is determined under federal law at the time of filing; subsequent changes in the parties’ citizenship do not themselves destroy an existing diversity basis. The party invoking jurisdiction bears the burden of pleading it and, when properly challenged, proving it. Casebook, pp. 310–311.

Judy remained a Mississippi citizen. Her Louisiana presence as a graduate student, without the necessary intention to remain, did not change her domicile; neither did marriage to an alien. Her claim against the Louisiana defendant qualified under § 1332(a)(1), and Jean Paul’s claim qualified under § 1332(a)(2). The Fifth Circuit upheld jurisdiction over both. Casebook, pp. 309–311.

Full-opinion addition: the amount claimed in good faith controls unless the claim is legally certain to fall below the jurisdictional amount. A lower verdict does not itself eliminate jurisdiction. The appellate court, like the district court, concluded after examining the record that the required amount had been in controversy. Full opinion, paragraphs 11–16.

## Judgment

The Fifth Circuit affirmed the district court’s judgment. The $5,000 award to Jean Paul and the $15,000 award to Judy were not set aside on the jurisdictional grounds Perry advanced. Casebook, pp. 309–311.

## Reasoning

The two plaintiffs had to be analyzed separately. Jean Paul’s French citizenship gave his dispute with the Louisiana defendant an alienage basis. Judy was an American, so her state citizenship had to be determined under the domicile rule. The availability of jurisdiction over her husband’s claim did not automatically make her own citizenship irrelevant. The court specifically found an independent citizenship basis for each spouse. Casebook, pp. 309–311.

Living in a state does not necessarily make an American a citizen of that state. Judy had lived in Louisiana for substantial periods and worked there as a graduate assistant. Those facts established residence but did not, on this record, establish the intent required to replace her existing domicile. The court considered the temporary, educational character of the residence and the absence of an intention to remain. The relevant question was not where she happened to sleep or work most recently. Casebook, pp. 310–311.

The existing domicile remains until both elements of a change are present. Judy’s lack of an intention to return to her parents’ house in Mississippi did not itself establish a new domicile somewhere else. To replace Mississippi, the evidence needed to show a new residence combined with the intention to remain. The court found the necessary intention absent in Louisiana. This rule prevents a person from losing an old domicile merely through departure or an unsettled future plan. Casebook, pp. 310–311.

The historical derivative-domicile rule could not resolve the case mechanically. The opinion acknowledged the then-used generalization that a wife’s domicile followed her husband’s, but refused to extend that fiction to these facts. Treating Judy as domiciled in France would not make her a French national entitled to sue as an alien; it instead could leave her outside the state-citizen category despite retaining U.S. citizenship. Treating her as domiciled in Louisiana would destroy diversity from Perry. The court rejected a result based solely on marriage rather than the relevant domicile inquiry. Casebook, p. 310.

Nationality and domicile are not the same thing. The court observed that an American woman did not lose United States citizenship simply by marrying an alien and concluded that her state citizenship and domicile likewise did not automatically change on that basis. Jean Paul’s foreign citizenship and Judy’s American citizenship thus required distinct applications of § 1332. The decision should not be summarized as making both spouses French or both spouses Mississippi citizens. Casebook, pp. 310–311.

The filing-date rule stabilizes the citizenship inquiry. The court identified the time when the complaint was filed as the relevant point for determining diversity and stated that later citizenship changes do not disturb jurisdiction. This means evidence of later moves must be used carefully: it can illuminate a person’s earlier intentions but cannot simply replace the citizenship inquiry at the legally relevant time. The casebook’s facts about Illinois and the expected return to Louisiana do not authorize treating trial-date residence as the statutory test. Footnote 2 supplies the filing chronology and expressly distinguishes the later moves’ evidentiary relevance from their inability to change jurisdiction already established. Casebook, pp. 309–310; full opinion, footnote 2 to paragraph 10.

The amount objection was different from the citizenship objection. The complete opinion treated the $5,000 verdict as an insufficient reason to dismiss Jean Paul’s claim. What mattered was the amount genuinely at stake when suit began, assessed under the good-faith and legal-certainty standard. This is a distinct jurisdictional inquiry; Judy’s larger award was not used simply to add the spouses’ recoveries together until they crossed the threshold. Full-opinion addition; paragraphs 11–16.

Efficiency was supportive, not a substitute for jurisdiction. The opinion ended by observing that the spouses’ claims arose from the same operative facts and involved heavily overlapping proof, making joint adjudication sensible. But it had already found that the claims rested on separate statutory citizenship bases. The closing discussion cannot be treated as a general rule that spouses may enter federal court whenever one spouse has a federal claim. Casebook, p. 311.

## Notes

Historical language and the modern rule. The casebook’s note expressly states that modern decisions have rejected automatic derivative domicile even when both spouses are American citizens. Mas’s refusal to apply it in an alien-spouse case should not be turned into an implication that married women today ordinarily acquire their husbands’ domiciles. Each spouse may have a separate domicile. Casebook, pp. 311–312.

Students can acquire a new domicile. The result is tied to the finding that the Mases lacked the required intention to remain in Louisiana, not to a universal legal disability attached to student status. For an application problem, ask whether residence is accompanied by the required intent; do not stop at the label “student.” The casebook’s ensuing domicile problem emphasizes the fact-intensive nature of that inquiry. Casebook, pp. 310–312; application.

Americans domiciled abroad. A United States citizen who truly acquires a foreign domicile is not thereby an alien and is no longer a citizen of a U.S. state for ordinary diversity purposes. The later casebook notes explain the resulting gap in § 1332. This is different from temporarily living abroad while retaining a state domicile. Casebook, p. 316.

Current statutory comparison. The modern § 1332(a)(2) exclusion for a lawfully admitted permanent resident domiciled in the same state as the American opponent must be considered in a present-day analogue. Mas does not supply facts establishing Jean Paul’s immigration status for that modern question. It is therefore improper to assume the modern exception applies or does not apply merely from his French citizenship. Casebook, p. 315; 28 U.S.C. § 1332(a)(2), statutory reproduction at https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/28/1332 (checked 4 October 2026).

Later statutory framework. Mas predates § 1367. For a present-day problem involving related claims of co-plaintiffs, use the current supplemental-jurisdiction statute and the later authorities discussed in that entry. Shared facts can matter, but they do not erase complete-diversity restrictions or create an original-jurisdiction claim where none exists. Application; §§ 1332, 1367, including § 1367(a)–(c), statutory reproduction at https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/28/1367 (checked 4 October 2026).
