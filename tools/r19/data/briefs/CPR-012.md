# Wyman v. Newhouse

**Course:** Civil Procedure
**ID:** CPR-012
**Review:** Complete opinion and assigned account independently compared after factual and procedural repairs.

## Citation

Wyman v. Newhouse, 93 F.2d 313 (2d Cir. 1937). Decided December 6, 1937. Judge Manton wrote for a panel with Judges Learned Hand and Swan; the report contains no separate opinion. The assigned caption also records certiorari denied, 303 U.S. 664 (1938). The casebook account begins on printed page 276 and continues through page 278, with a following editorial note on the same printed page. Repository PDF pages 163–164.

## Parties and procedural history

Wyman obtained a Florida state-court default judgment against Newhouse after a jury assessed damages. The underlying claims sought recovery for money loaned, money advanced for Newhouse, and seduction under a promise of marriage. She then sued on that judgment. Newhouse's answer asserted that she had fraudulently induced him to enter Florida solely to serve him. Wyman appealed the judgment dismissing her enforcement complaint before trial. (313–314.)

It was Wyman who moved to strike that defense and for summary judgment under Rule 113 of the New York Rules of Civil Practice. Her motion was denied. For purposes of that motion, the answer's alleged facts were taken as true under Rules 109 and 112. Both parties submitted affidavits; Newhouse then moved to dismiss the complaint, and that motion was granted. The Second Circuit affirmed the dismissal. This sequence distinguishes the unsuccessful plaintiff's motion from the successful defendant's motion; the appellate opinion does not present Newhouse's dismissal request as the Rule 113 motion. (313–314, 315.)

## Material facts

Newhouse was a New York resident who had never lived in Florida. The parties had known one another for years and maintained a sexual relationship outside marriage. Both had been married, although Wyman's husband died before the suit. The facts in Newhouse's affidavits were deemed true for reviewing the dismissal. Wyman did not deny making the representations described below. (314.)

On October 25, 1935, while Newhouse was in Salt Lake City, Wyman sent a telegram asking him to visit on his return because of illness and her planned departure. After he returned to New York, she wrote that her mother was dying in Ireland, that she would leave the United States permanently, and that she needed to see him once more and discuss her affairs. In a distressed telephone conversation she repeated those representations. He promised to visit Florida within a week or ten days. A further affectionate letter and exchanged telegrams arranged accommodations in Miami, including a telegram received during his stop in Washington. (314.)

At six in the morning, Newhouse arrived at the Miami airport and saw Wyman and her sister about seventy-five feet away. A deputy sheriff identified and served him in an action demanding $500,000; a photographer attempted to take his picture. A stranger offered to take Newhouse to the stranger's own home, saying he knew a lawyer acquainted with Wyman's attorney. That lawyer came to the stranger's home, appeared to know about the case, and invited Newhouse to his office. One of Wyman's lawyers was waiting there. Newhouse retained no Florida lawyer and returned to New York that evening. His New York counsel advised him to ignore the Florida summons; he did so and defaulted. Within days, Wyman and her Florida counsel met him at his New York attorney's home. (314.)

The court concluded that these circumstances and reasonable inferences convincingly established a fraudulent inducement to enter Florida for service. The professed affection conflicted with the airport arrangements, and Wyman never made the claimed trip to Ireland. The court questioned whether her mother had been ill at all; its conclusion concerned the scheme to procure Newhouse's presence, rather than adjudication of the underlying loan or seduction claims. (314.)

## Issue and holding

Could Wyman enforce the Florida default judgment despite fraud that manufactured Newhouse's presence in the jurisdiction for service? Did Newhouse have to challenge the judgment or defend the underlying claim in Florida before resisting enforcement elsewhere?

The Second Circuit held that this fraud affected jurisdiction over Newhouse's person. A judgment procured through that jurisdictional fraud was void and could be challenged collaterally in the enforcement action. Newhouse's equitable defense was sufficient; he did not have to seek relief in Florida or establish a defense to the original action's merits. The judgment dismissing Wyman's complaint was affirmed. (314–315.)

## Reasoning

Wyman relied on Article IV, § 1 and its then-applicable implementing statute, 28 U.S.C.A. § 687, which required authenticated judicial proceedings to receive the faith and credit they had in the rendering state. The court first asked how Florida would treat this judgment. It believed the fraudulent service would have been vacated there. Neither a Florida statute governing such service nor a controlling Florida decision had been supplied, so the court applied the forum's law, under which the service would be set aside as fraudulent. That stated route through rendering-state effect and forum law is part of the opinion's analysis. (314–315.)

The defect concerned acquisition of jurisdiction, rather than merely an irregularity after jurisdiction existed. Fraud inducing entry into the state for service was treated as equivalent to lack of personal jurisdiction. The court relied on authorities allowing a defendant to raise that jurisdictional fraud when the judgment was offered for enforcement in another state. It acknowledged contrary authority, but considered the weight of authority opposed to enforcement. (315.)

Jaster v. Currie did not resolve the case for Wyman because it had found no fraudulent inducement into the jurisdiction. Other cited cases concerned procedural irregularities that did not void a judgment. Likewise, rules requiring a merits defense when challenging fraud after valid personal and subject-matter jurisdiction had attached did not apply here: the Florida court had not duly acquired jurisdiction over Newhouse. The enforcement court could therefore consider that question collaterally. (315.)

## Assigned note and limits

The textbook asks readers to compare Wyman with Tickle v. Barton, consider a defendant already in Florida who is tricked into emerging from hiding, and assess whether long-arm jurisdiction and out-of-state service reduce incentives to use deception. It also asks whether deterrence justifies the rule even when such schemes are rare. These are editorial questions; Wyman does not decide the proposed already-present-defendant hypothetical or announce a general rule approving that form of trickery. (Assigned PDF 164, printed 278.)
