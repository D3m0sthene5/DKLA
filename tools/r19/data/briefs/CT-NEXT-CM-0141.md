# Snow Phipps Group, LLC v. KCAKE Acquisition, Inc.
2021 WL 1714202 (Del. Ch. Apr. 30, 2021) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
Vice Chancellor McCormick decided this dispute after a five-day trial involving the seller of DecoPac and buyer KCAKE and related Kohlberg funds. She entered judgment for the plaintiffs on specific performance of the stock purchase agreement (SPA), obligating the buyers to close. The court requested supplemental submissions within five business days about the closing deadline and entitlement to prejudgment interest, and a joint identification of any remaining trial-level matters. It did not adopt plaintiffs' proposed fifteen-day deadline or finally award interest in this opinion. This is a single-judge trial decision, with no separate writing or appellate vote. Slip op. 3, 123–125.

## Transaction and allocation of risk
DecoPac supplied cake-decorating products and technology to supermarket bakeries. Snow Phipps acquired it in 2017 and decided to sell in 2019. Kohlberg proposed $580 million in February 2020, raised its offer to $600 million, and promoted its familiarity with the business, speed, and closing certainty. Before signing it demanded a reduction to $550 million. The court found that pandemic-related volatility and Kohlberg's negotiating leverage, rather than solely quality-of-earnings adjustments, drove the reduction. The parties signed March 6, 2020. These are post-trial findings; the parties had disputed their negotiations and expectations. Slip op. 4–21.

The negotiations occurred as COVID-19 spread. Kohlberg investigated supply-chain, market, and demand risks, although it underestimated the pandemic's eventual consequences. Plaintiffs sought an express pandemic/epidemic exclusion from the material-adverse-effect (MAE) definition, and Kohlberg rejected that addition. The existing definition nevertheless retained broad exceptions, including effects arising from or related to changes in government laws and orders, qualified by materially disproportionate effects on DecoPac relative to comparable businesses. Attorneys treated the proposed additional language as additional protection; failure to add it did not eliminate the existing carveouts. The eventual decision principally relied on unambiguous contractual language rather than rewriting the bargain from disputed negotiating recollections. Slip op. 10–21, 63–67, 78–82.

The SPA contained representations about MAEs and top-ten customers, an ordinary-course covenant tied to DecoPac's past custom and practice, and closing conditions. Representation inaccuracies excused closing only at the contractual MAE level; covenant compliance required performance in all material respects, a distinct standard. A termination for breach required notice and an opportunity to cure. The buyer promised reasonable best efforts to obtain financing, including definitive agreements on terms no less favorable than its debt commitment letter (DCL), and restricted changes that would jeopardize funding. The SPA expressly said debt financing was not a condition to closing, but its specific-performance provision separately conditioned that remedy on funded debt, or debt that would fund with substantially contemporaneous equity. A $33 million termination fee was the exclusive monetary remedy, and the seller could not receive both it and specific performance. Slip op. 21–27, 64, 84, 92–94, 114–115.

The heavily negotiated DCL committed lenders to $365 million in facilities. It included quarterly leverage testing, a detailed EBITDA definition, and documentation principles. It expired May 12, 2020. The SPA had an outside termination date rather than automatic expiration, and withheld that termination right from a party whose own failure caused the failure to close. Slip op. 25–27 & nn.132–146.

## Performance decline and financing conduct
DecoPac's regular sales fell sharply as shutdown orders spread, while preorders and total sales showed a different trajectory. Management expected recovery and adjusted spending to reduced activity. Kohlberg initially modeled a downturn it believed the company could withstand, but senior personnel developed buyer's remorse and considered alternative investments. After consulting litigation counsel, it produced progressively pessimistic forecasts without meaningful management input. It abandoned a less severe downside model its deal team viewed as a reasonable starting point. Slip op. 28–36.

Management responded to requests with a detailed forecast using customer information, actual orders, and its experienced budgeting process. Kohlberg dismissed it seventeen minutes after receipt, did not incorporate it, and did not send it to lenders. Its own March 26 model predicted a severe decline and covenant breach using largely unsupported assumptions about cancelled celebrations and prolonged shutdowns. It paired that model with demands for a larger revolver, extraordinary lost-revenue EBITDA addbacks, and a covenant-testing holiday. The lenders refused those new terms but remained willing to fund under the agreed DCL. None declared an MAE. Slip op. 36–49.

Kohlberg reported financing unavailable, briefly investigated alternatives through Houlihan Lokey and Madison Capital, and stopped after four days. It rejected the seller's offer of financing assistance and refused to close April 8. Plaintiffs sued April 14. On April 20 Kohlberg purported to terminate for representation and covenant failures and lack of funding. Plaintiffs disputed those grounds and reaffirmed readiness to close. The DCL later expired by its own terms, after the court initially declined an extremely compressed trial schedule and subsequently expedited the matter. An October dismissal ruling rejected a duplicative implied-covenant count and addressed contractual remedy limits; the present decision followed trial on the remaining dispute. Slip op. 49–59.

DecoPac's sales recovered. Its completed 2020 results showed a fourteen-percent revenue decline and a twenty-five-percent adjusted-EBITDA decline from 2019, followed by a positive outlook. Debt markets also reopened, and plaintiffs presented financing assistance and a lender indication of interest. Those developments supplied context but did not convert the pending opinion into an adjudication that a new lender was already contractually obligated to fund. Slip op. 31, 60–62.

## MAE and ordinary-course defenses
The MAE analysis allocated successive burdens: buyer to show a qualifying effect, seller to prove an exclusion, then buyer to prove a materially disproportionate effect restoring coverage. An objectively reasonably expected MAE did not require that harm already be fully realized, but required a factual and legal basis for the serious prediction. Materiality and duration depended on the transaction; ordinarily the relevant horizon for long-term earnings was years rather than a short earnings interruption. Slip op. 65–70.

The dramatic weekly decline did not establish a durationally significant MAE. Recovery had begun before termination; credible contemporaneous forecasts projected further rebound. The buyer's expert claims about permanent shifts to thaw-and-sell cakes and online shopping did not adequately account for DecoPac's participation in those channels or improving real-time performance. The court rejected the March 26 model as a reliable objective forecast. Its comparisons with prior MAE decisions illustrated the difference between short-lived reversals and sustained deterioration, rather than creating a universal percentage threshold. Footnote 410 flagged, without deciding, the broader question of different horizons for leveraged private-equity transactions. Slip op. 70–78 & n.410.

Even assuming an MAE, the government-order exception applied. Its broad relation language did not require government orders to be the exclusive cause. Regression evidence tied most of the decline to relevant orders. Kohlberg also failed to show disproportionate industry harm: supermarkets as a whole were an overbroad comparison, while decorated-cake and in-store-bakery proxies better reflected the business and did not demonstrate disproportionate impact. The top-customer representation argument failed the same MAE-level threshold. Slip op. 78–84.

The ordinary-course analysis was separate and less demanding than an MAE inquiry, but the alleged deviations still failed. DecoPac's $15 million revolver draw followed prior draws and a portfolio-wide counterparty-risk precaution. It disclosed the draw, never used the proceeds, offered repayment, and eventually repaid. The deviation was not material in the required contractual sense. Moreover, easy repayment made contractual notice and cure meaningful, and Kohlberg failed to honor that protection. Cost reductions followed historical adjustments to sales and varied in expected or minor ways; the buyer also waived that belated theory by failing to raise it until pretrial briefing. The court did not hold that all pandemic responses necessarily satisfy every ordinary-course covenant. Slip op. 84–91.

## Financing breach and prevention doctrine
The court read the financing provisions together. The DCL supplied a floor protecting the buyer while efforts and preservation obligations protected the seller's closing. The buyer could seek improvement, but could not insist on new terms and refuse the agreed financing when improvement was unavailable. A larger revolver and testing holiday changed negotiated terms. The requested addbacks were not vested entitlements under the limited unusual-loss clause or general documentation principles; mutual negotiation did not confer a unilateral right. The court also identified waiver of the late entitlement theory, while examining its merits. Slip op. 91–103.

Acceptable-to-buyer language did not create unlimited discretion to block financing. Truly open terms could be resolved acceptably to Kohlberg subject to its efforts duties. Even assuming some addbacks concerned open terms, its reliance on an unsupported model, disregard of management's forecast and improving actual sales, and failure to work constructively with counterparties violated those duties. The court found breach of Section 6.15(a). Its alternative-financing discussion noted shortcomings but initially described that separate inquiry as unnecessary because the prior efforts prerequisite had failed. In the remedies section it additionally recognized entitlement to specific performance of the alternative-financing obligation, likely eclipsed by the closing order. These different passages should be read together, rather than presenting every four-day search as a per se violation. Slip op. 103–113, 123.

The remaining remedial obstacle was the debt-funding condition. Under prevention principles, a party's breach materially contributing to a condition's nonoccurrence excuses that nonoccurrence. Plaintiffs did not have to prove strict but-for inevitability; the buyer bore the burden of showing the condition would have failed regardless. Its refusal to proceed while lenders remained ready materially contributed to absence of funding. Allowing the DCL clock to expire did not cleanse that breach. Slip op. 115–117.

No additional showing of subjective bad faith was necessary on these facts. Express financing duties supplied the wrongful conduct, unlike precedents where an implied good-faith obligation defined wrongfulness. The court distinguished the authorities on which Kohlberg relied and did not make subjective intent an independent prerequisite. With the condition excused and clear and convincing evidence favoring performance, the court ordered closing. Slip op. 117–123.

## Significance and source scope
The case illustrates enforcement of a specifically negotiated remedy where the buyer's breach materially defeats its own remedial condition. It does not erase financing conditions generally, guarantee all acquisition financing, or decide all pandemic disruptions alike. Contract language, trial findings, efforts obligations, and causation drove the result. Prejudgment interest and the exact closing deadline remained for further submissions. Slip op. 123–125.
