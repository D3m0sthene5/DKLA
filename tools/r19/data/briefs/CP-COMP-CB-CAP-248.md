## Facts and procedural course

Leame's declaration alleged that Bray drove his single-horse chaise into Leame's two-horse curricle on the highway. Leame was riding with his servant, who drove the curricle. The impact threw the servant to the ground, the horses ran away, and Leame jumped from the curricle to preserve his life, fracturing his collarbone. The action was trespass, alleging an injury caused with force and arms. Bray pleaded not guilty. (593.)

At trial before Lord Ellenborough at Westminster, the evidence showed that the collision occurred on a dark night because Bray drove on the wrong side of the road and the parties could not see one another. There would have been ample room to pass if Bray had kept to his proper side. The report says no other blame appeared concerning the manner of his driving. Bray objected that the injury was negligent rather than willful, making case the proper action. Leame was nonsuited. He then obtained a rule calling for the nonsuit to be set aside; the reported proceeding concerns whether that rule should be made absolute. (593.)

## The arguments

Gibbs and Park opposed setting aside the nonsuit. They conceded that trespass could cover some injuries immediately caused by a person whose will did not accompany the harmful result, but characterized this injury as consequential. The carriages and horses, rather than Bray's immediate personal act, produced the damage after receiving an impulse for the lawful purpose of travel. They compared ship collisions and argued that the apparent distinction between willful injury and negligent management explained cases such as *Ogle v. Barnes*. (593–597.)

The judges challenged this argument during the reported exchange. Ellenborough asked why setting the horse in motion was not an act of force attributable to Bray. Lawrence gave examples of accidental direct injury, including a suddenly turning person knocking down someone he had not seen. The dialogue also addressed servants: vessel owners could be liable in case for negligent execution of orders by seamen even if no immediate personal act by the master could be proved. These questions were part of the analysis of action forms, not additional findings about this road accident. (594–597.)

Erskine and Hovell supported the rule. They relied on the direct-versus-consequential distinction in *Reynolds v. Clarke* and *Day v. Edwards*. A log striking someone while thrown supported trespass; a person later falling over the same log supported case. They disputed the premise that *Day*'s allegation of furious driving necessarily meant willful injury. They distinguished *Ogle* because the ship owners' personal involvement at the collision was not necessarily alleged; negligence might have occurred earlier, or the owners might not have been aboard. Their contention was that immediacy, rather than willfulness, controlled the present action. (597–598.)

## Holding and the four explanations

Ellenborough held that trespass was proper where injury was the immediate result of force personally applied by the defendant. Willfulness was unnecessary. In *Ogle*, he considered case sustainable because the declaration did not necessarily charge an act of force by the defendants themselves: management might have occurred through employees, and the owners were not alleged to be aboard. Here, Bray was personally present and used the ordinary means of propelling his horse, from which the collision followed. The moving horse and carriage did not break the attribution of the applied force to their driver. (598–599.)

Grose agreed. His review of the reported precedents yielded the principle that an injury produced by the party's own contemporaneous act, or of which that party was the immediate cause, could be pursued in trespass even if accidental or unfortunate. He did not create a new finding that Bray intended the collision. (599.)

Lawrence also agreed and explained both doctrine and procedural convenience. Trespass avoided a potential trap in case: if testimony suggested willfulness, a plaintiff who sued in case risked nonsuit. He denied that the fundamental distinction turned on lawful versus unlawful action or the defendant's design to injure. In explaining his earlier language in *Ogle*, he said his main concern was the absence of an established personal act by the ship owners. Wind or tide might have counteracted their efforts, or they might not have been aboard. Negligence allegations could also be proved through a servant's conduct in the master's absence. These were ways to understand the earlier record and the law of forms, not findings that a particular external force caused Bray's collision. (599–601.)

Le Blanc agreed with the immediate-versus-consequential rule and stressed that neither the amount of violence nor intent supplied the boundary. Quietly placing a log on another person's foot could be trespass, while someone later falling over a violently thrown log could require case. If Bray had merely left his chaise in the road and Leame later struck it, the injury would have been consequential to that earlier act. Instead, Bray was driving when the injury occurred. Le Blanc noted possible complications from wind and waves in ship collisions but expressly declined to determine their effect. He treated force applied to horse and carriage as analogous to pressing a gun's trigger: the intermediary instrument did not make the resulting injury merely consequential. (601–602.)

## Disposition and significance

The court **made the rule absolute**, setting aside Leame's nonsuit. It held that the negligence evidence did not defeat the trespass form used here. The report contains concurring explanations from Ellenborough, Grose, Lawrence, and Le Blanc and no dissent. It does not report a final damages award or a subsequent trial outcome; this ruling should not be described as an award of compensation. (602.)

The assignment accurately emphasizes the court's persistence with the immediate-injury approach and quotes Lawrence's explanation of *Ogle*. The complete report adds the actual trial posture, Leame's escape injury, the practical danger of choosing case, and the judges' distinction between a defendant's personal application of force and liability for a servant's negligence. Its illustrations concern the historical choice of writ; they should not be presented as a verified statement of modern tort pleading or liability.
