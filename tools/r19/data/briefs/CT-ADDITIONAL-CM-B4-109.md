# Wasserman Theatrical Enterprise, Inc. v. Jed Harris
137 Conn. 371, 77 A.2d 329 (1950) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and result
Connecticut’s Supreme Court of Errors decided this appeal on December 26, 1950. Chief Justice Brown wrote; Justices Jennings, Baldwin, Inglis, and O’Sullivan concurred. A theatrical promoter had sued for damages after cancellation of a contracted performance. The trial court entered judgment for the defendant, and the appellate court found no error. It awarded the plaintiff no compensation for its preparations or expected profit. (371–373, 376–377.)

## Contract and canceled performance
On October 30, 1946, Wasserman contracted with Harris for a performance of The Apple of His Eye starring Walter Huston in Worcester on December 16. The agreement made its obligations subject to customs governing uncontrollable circumstances, including illness of a chief artist, and barred compensation or damage claims by either side on such an occurrence. Wasserman had produced Worcester performances for about eleven years, expected a profit, and incurred preparation expenses and effort. It remained ready and able to perform. On December 12 Harris canceled Worcester on account of Huston’s illness and also canceled the following bookings at Ithaca, Springfield, and Rochester. Huston was the leading man and had no understudy. (372–373.)

The show had toured since early fall, including four weeks of eight performances a week in Boston. Huston subsequently performed all four New Haven shows on December 12–14 and all of the later Chicago performances beginning December 25. The question was therefore not answered simply by observing that the actor had performed before and after the canceled engagement. Harris pleaded the contract’s illness exception and asserted impossibility because Huston was essential. The court focused on whether the actor’s apprehension of serious harm was reasonable when cancellation occurred. (373–374.)

## Governing personal-service principles
Unless a contrary intention or assumption of risk appears, a promise requiring a particular person’s personal services depends on that person’s physical ability to perform at the appointed time. The principle also applies when the promisor arranges for a third person to provide those services. The express illness clause substantially reflected that implied condition rather than introducing a wholly different allocation of risk. (373–374.)

The court also applied the Restatement’s rule concerning reasonable apprehension of serious harm. A promisor without contributing fault or a contrary undertaking may decline to begin performance when existing grounds reasonably indicate serious danger to life or health. Reasonableness depends on the apparent probability and seriousness of the threatened harm, compared with the consequences to the promisee of nonperformance. Actual physical ability to perform is not conclusive: protection of human welfare may excuse performance even if it remains practicable. Later discovery that no harm would have occurred does not retroactively make the original apprehension unreasonable. The apprehended harm must nevertheless be serious and the apprehension reasonable; ordinary reluctance or unsupported anxiety does not suffice. (374–375.)

## Application to Huston’s condition
The findings as corrected showed recurring and worsening throat symptoms for approximately two weeks before December 12. His throat tightened two or three times during some performances. Medication afforded temporary relief, but the problem continued in New Haven. He feared he might be unable to finish and wanted a definite diagnosis, although the fear did not interfere with ordinary offstage conversation. The court recognized that lozenges probably would have enabled him to complete Worcester if the tightening occurred. That possibility did not eliminate the concern that delay in medical attention could seriously damage his health or voice. (375–376.)

During November and December he consulted his physician friend, Dr. Loyal Davis. Davis heard the symptoms without examining him and advised a thorough examination and treatment in Chicago. As episodes became more frequent, Huston believed he could finish New Haven but could not responsibly continue the following week. He insisted on canceling that week’s engagements to obtain immediate attention. In Chicago he underwent a minor throat operation and tissue sampling. A report finding no malignancy relieved his concern, and he resumed the scheduled performances. His forty-five-year theatrical history and sincere desire to fulfill obligations further supported the court’s assessment; the report states that his only personal request to cancel a performance had been Worcester. The producer’s cancellation of several bookings is distinct from that statement about the actor’s own prior request. (375–376.)

On these findings, the court upheld the conclusion that Huston reasonably feared serious harm without prompt expert care, especially to his voice. That reasonable apprehension justified Harris’s cancellation and defeated the promoter’s damage claim. The holding did not require a subsequent finding of cancer or proof that performing one more show was literally impossible. (376–377.)
