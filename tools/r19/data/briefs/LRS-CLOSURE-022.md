# National Mining Association v. McCarthy
758 F.3d 243 (D.C. Cir. 2014) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Court, posture, and divided disposition of the claims

On July 11, 2014, Judge Kavanaugh wrote for a unanimous D.C. Circuit panel with Judges Griffith and Srinivasan in Nos. 12-5310 and 12-5311. EPA, the Army Corps of Engineers, and intervening environmental organizations appealed district-court judgments invalidating two measures affecting Appalachian surface-coal-mining permits. The court reversed and remanded with instructions to enter judgment for defendants on the Enhanced Coordination Process and to dismiss the challenge to EPA's Final Guidance. It decided the coordination process's legality but found the guidance presently unreviewable, rather than deciding that guidance's statutory merits. There were no separate opinions. (246–248, 253.)

## Mining effects and the distinct permit regimes

Surface mining removed soil and rock above coal and relocated the material after extraction. It supplied electricity-generating coal but altered land and affected water bodies. The Surface Mining Control and Reclamation Act required mining permits under Interior-approved programs. Those permits were background, not the permits challenged here. Clean Water Act projects discharging material into navigable waters also needed two different permits. (246–247.)

The Corps issued § 404 dredged-or-fill permits, while EPA could disallow disposal sites having specified unacceptable adverse effects. Section 402 NPDES permits addressed other pollutant discharges; state authorities issued the permits relevant here, with EPA reviewing them for compliance with state water-quality standards and other Clean Water Act requirements. These standards combined designated water uses with criteria assessing water-body health. EPA's roles in the two permitting processes differed, and the court analyzed the two challenged measures separately. (247.)

In June 2009 EPA and the Corps adopted an Enhanced Coordination Process for 108 § 404 applications stalled by litigation. EPA screened application information against the guidelines governing Corps permits, flagged potential problems, and discussed them with the Corps and interested parties over a 60-day period subject to extensions. The Corps retained the final permitting decision. In 2011 EPA separately issued guidance responding to peer-reviewed studies that mining increased water salinity and conductivity, threatening aquatic life. It recommended that Appalachian conductivity levels not exceed 300–500 microSiemens per centimeter and advised EPA staff to ask state permitting authorities to evaluate that issue. (248.)

West Virginia, Kentucky, mining companies, and trade associations challenged the coordination process as beyond EPA's authority and as an improperly issued legislative rule. They attacked the guidance under both the Clean Water Act and the mining statute. The district court granted summary judgment invalidating both measures. The appellate court reviewed those judgments de novo. (248.)

## Congress did not prohibit interagency consultation

Plaintiffs argued that Congress's express assignments of EPA participation at specified § 404 stages implicitly excluded participation elsewhere. The coordination process, however, changed neither the statutory criteria nor either agency's substantive responsibilities. The Corps still decided whether to issue permits, and EPA retained its disposal-site role. No statutory provision prohibited their consultation. (248–249.)

The court declined to infer a consultation ban from silence. Coordination among executive agencies was ordinary and desirable, and a prohibition would raise significant concerns under the President's executive and Take Care responsibilities. The court expressly put independent agencies aside; both agencies involved were subject to the single President's direction. Its reasoning rejected the claimed implied prohibition, rather than holding that Article II authorized an agency to alter statutory permitting criteria. (249.)

The process was also a procedural rule exempt from APA notice and comment. It changed the way agency consideration occurred without itself altering private rights or interests. The sole footnote rejected the claim that the coordination period violated Corps processing regulations: the 60-day target had an exception for needed information, and the regulations established aspirational goals rather than mandatory deadlines. The note used then-applicable deference to the agency's reading of its own regulation. (250 & n. 1.)

## The guidance failed the legal-consequences requirement for final action

EPA conceded that the Final Guidance consummated its decisionmaking, satisfying Bennett's first condition. The remaining question was whether it determined rights or obligations or produced legal consequences. Its title did not answer that question. Under the circuit's precedents, a genuine general policy statement was not subject to pre-enforcement review, although legislative rules and sometimes interpretive rules could be. The parties framed the issue as legislative rule versus policy statement. The court acknowledged that those categories were often difficult to separate rather than announcing that every guidance document fit a simple test. (250–252.)

Actual legal effect was the most important factor. This guidance imposed no obligations or prohibitions, no requirements for obtaining permits, and no enforceable basis for liability. State authorities and applicants could ignore it without legal consequences. EPA conceded that it had no legal impact; EPA could not rely on the guidance as a defense to a challenge to a later permit denial. Its recommendations therefore described intended exercise of existing discretion rather than creating new binding permit criteria. (252.)

The document's wording also mattered. Unlike Appalachian Power's mandatory instructions accompanied by one closing disclaimer, this guidance repeated its nonbinding caveats throughout and lacked relevant commands. The sparse record of implementation did not show EPA treating it as binding. The court recognized that applicants might feel practical pressure to follow recommendations because they anticipated what EPA would favor. That pressure, without an order compelling compliance or an associated legal penalty or disability, did not supply finality on this record. Applicants might still obtain permits without following the recommendations. (252–253.)

Future review remained available. If EPA denied a permit, the applicant could challenge that denial; EPA then had to justify its policy on the governing law and record as though the policy statement had never been issued. The court expressly did not decide plaintiffs' attacks on the guidance's substantive legality. Dismissal at this stage thus differed from a merits judgment sustaining EPA's preferred conductivity criteria. (253.)

## Judgment and assigned accounts

The final order reversed summary judgment and directed judgment for defendants on the coordination-process claim, while directing dismissal of the guidance challenge. Only the former claim received a merits disposition. The guidance was not vacated by the appellate court. (253.)
