# Lake Land Employment Group of Akron, LLC v. Columber

101 Ohio St. 3d 242, 804 N.E.2d 27, 2004-Ohio-786 (2004)

## Court, author, and source scope
The Supreme Court of Ohio resolved a certified conflict about consideration for a noncompetition agreement signed after an at-will employee had begun working. Chief Justice Moyer wrote the majority opinion. The court divided four to three, with separate dissents by Justices Resnick and Pfeifer. The assigned excerpt appears at printed pages 85–91. Pinpoints use the numbered paragraphs of 2004-Ohio-786, avoiding the damaged reporter citation in the scanned casebook's extracted text. This is an account of the 2004 decision, not a current survey of noncompetition regulation.

## Parties and material facts
Lake Land employed Lee Columber from 1988 until 2001. In September 1991, after the employment relationship had begun, Columber signed an agreement restricting competitive activity for three years after termination within a fifty-mile radius of Akron. He received no salary increase, additional benefits, or other remuneration in connection with signing, and his employment status did not otherwise change. The parties continued their at-will relationship for approximately ten more years. ¶¶ 1–5, 26.

Columber remembered little about how the agreement had been presented. He vaguely recalled being told to read and sign it, acknowledged that he read it, and had not consulted a lawyer or anyone else. He could not recall whether the employer expressly told him that continued employment depended on execution. A brief should therefore distinguish the court's legal characterization of presenting the agreement as a proposal for continued employment on new terms from a factual finding that a particular ultimatum was spoken. ¶¶ 2–4, 19–20.

After Lake Land discharged him in 2001, Columber formed a corporation engaged in a similar business. Lake Land alleged that his activities violated the covenant and sought damages and injunctive relief. Columber admitted the employment relationship and execution of the agreement but raised lack of consideration and challenged the restrictions as excessive and unduly burdensome. Whether his particular activities violated an enforceable, reasonable restriction was not finally adjudicated in the decision under review. ¶¶ 1–5.

## Procedural history
The trial court granted summary judgment for Columber. It relied on the absence of any increased compensation or change in employment status when the covenant was executed and concluded that the agreement lacked consideration. Because that conclusion disposed of the claim, the court did not decide whether the three-year and fifty-mile restrictions were reasonable. The court of appeals affirmed but certified a conflict with other Ohio appellate decisions. ¶¶ 4–6.

The certified question asked whether continued employment alone can provide consideration for a covenant not to compete entered into by an at-will employee after employment has already begun. The Ohio Supreme Court answered affirmatively in the circumstances it described, reversed, and remanded for consideration of the agreement's reasonableness and further proceedings. Its judgment did not itself enjoin Columber, award Lake Land damages, or approve the covenant's duration and geographic reach. ¶¶ 6, 20–27.

## Issues
Does an employer's continuation of an employment relationship that it could lawfully terminate without cause supply consideration for an existing at-will employee's assent to a newly proposed noncompetition agreement? If consideration exists, does that conclusion establish that the restraint is enforceable without a separate inquiry into reasonableness and other contract defenses? ¶¶ 6, 14–25.

## Rules and holdings
The majority held that sufficient consideration exists when the employer presents the noncompetition agreement as a new condition of continuing an at-will relationship, the employee assents, and the employer continues employment that it could otherwise legally terminate. The employee's acceptance of the restraint is exchanged for the employer's forbearance from exercising its termination right. The majority did not require a separate raise or benefit as an indispensable consideration element. ¶¶ 18–20, 26.

The court expressly preserved separate scrutiny of the restraint's reasonableness. Under the Ohio precedent it discussed, a covenant must protect legitimate employer interests without being greater than necessary, imposing undue hardship on the employee, or injuring the public. Courts can address excessive restrictions through the controlling reasonableness framework, and other grounds of invalidity remain available. Sufficient consideration alone does not establish an enforceable restraint in every respect. ¶¶ 21–25, 27.

## Actual judgment
The judgment of the court of appeals was reversed, and the case was remanded. Summary judgment could not stand on the stated ground of no consideration; the trial court still had to determine whether the noncompetition agreement was reasonable. The employer won the consideration issue and the opportunity to pursue its case, rather than a final judgment granting all requested relief. ¶¶ 26–27.

## Majority reasoning
### At-will employment permits prospective renegotiation
The majority starts with each party's legal ability to end an at-will relationship. An employee generally cannot insist that future employment continue on yesterday's terms simply because those terms governed past service. An employer may propose reduced compensation, leaving the employee to accept the new terms or quit; an employee may demand more compensation, leaving the employer to negotiate or end the relationship. Earned wages remain governed by the agreed terms for the work already performed. ¶¶ 14–18.

The majority applies that model to the restrictive covenant. Presenting the agreement proposes a changed set of terms for continued employment. Assent followed by continued employment establishes an exchange: the employee assumes the restriction, and the employer refrains from immediately ending a relationship it was free to end. The court therefore identifies a legal act of forbearance even though the overall employment remains indefinite and no extra money changes hands. ¶¶ 19–20.

This reasoning depends on the relationship's at-will character. If an employer were already obligated to provide employment for a fixed term, continuation of the very same owed performance would raise a different consideration problem. The opinion's premise is a termination right that actually exists, not a general permission to make unlawful threats or terminate for prohibited reasons. ¶¶ 17–20.

### Earlier employment does not exhaust the possibility of a new exchange
The court distinguishes the initial hiring transaction from a later change in the ongoing relationship. Some decisions reject continued employment as giving the worker only what the worker already had; others require a substantial subsequent period or tangible benefits. The majority recognizes that split but reasons that an at-will worker has no existing entitlement to future employment for a specified duration. The employer's decision to continue rather than terminate is therefore capable of supplying consideration at the point of assent. ¶¶ 7–13, 18–20.

Approaches dependent on an undefined substantial period of future service create uncertainty: neither side knows at signing whether enough time will later have passed to validate the agreement. The majority's stated rule does not set a numerical minimum. Columber's ten additional years support application to the actual facts, but the opinion should not be rewritten as a rule that precisely ten years, or any other fixed period, must follow. ¶¶ 12–13, 26.

### Consideration and restraint-of-trade scrutiny perform different work
The court refuses to turn consideration into a broad judicial comparison of the value each party received. It treats adequacy as ordinarily a matter for the parties' bargain. Nevertheless, it recognizes the reasons courts examine noncompetition agreements carefully: they can protect confidential information and productive cooperation, yet can also constrain livelihood and competition. Those concerns belong in the established reasonableness inquiry and other defenses, which remain fully operative. ¶¶ 7–9, 21–25.

That division of labor explains the limited remand. The trial court had resolved the case at the consideration stage and consequently never examined the temporal and geographic restraints. The Supreme Court corrects the first ruling without deciding the second question on a record and posture in which it had not been addressed below. ¶¶ 4–5, 24–27.

## Resnick's dissent
Justice Resnick would affirm. She argues that the employer has surrendered nothing because it retains exactly the same power to discharge the employee immediately after obtaining the signature. The employee likewise receives no new entitlement to future work. Calling the unchanged relationship forbearance, renegotiation, or mutual promises does not, in her view, create the necessary exchange. The only concrete benefit for the employer is the new restraint; the employee retains the old insecurity. ¶¶ 28–33.

The dissent tests the majority's rule with an employee who has already acquired all useful training and information and is fired the day after signing. If that covenant is enforceable, Resnick reasons, the promise of continued employment is effectively empty. Her objection concerns the existence of legally sufficient consideration rather than simply the monetary inadequacy of a conceded benefit. She rejects the agreement on that threshold ground and therefore would leave the employee's judgment intact. ¶¶ 32–35.

## Pfeifer's dissent
Justice Pfeifer agrees that threatening an at-will employee with termination unless the employee signs should not supply consideration and characterizes that situation as coercive. He also identifies a different difficulty in the majority's logic. If the employer truly gives a promise of continued employment in exchange for the restraint, that promise seems to alter the employment's at-will character for some period. A court would then have to determine the reasonable duration of the promised forbearance, despite the absence of an express term. ¶¶ 36–38.

His critique should be distinguished from Resnick's. Resnick emphasizes that nothing changes and the employer can still fire immediately. Pfeifer argues that recognizing a genuine promise of continued employment logically requires some change, creating uncertainty about its duration. He suggests that separate monetary consideration would allow the covenant to stand apart from the employment's at-will status. Neither dissent's proposed rule became the court's holding. Id.

## Voting and limits
Moyer's opinion was joined by Justices Lundberg Stratton and O'Connor and by Judge Handwork, sitting by designation for Justice O'Donnell. Resnick, Justice Francis E. Sweeney Sr., and Pfeifer dissented. The report provides Resnick's and Pfeifer's writings, not an additional separate opinion by Sweeney.

The central study comparison is with an illusory promise and with actual requested forbearance. The majority locates consideration in continuing a relationship that could be terminated; the dissent questions whether that changes any enforceable obligation. The case does not establish that every signed employment restriction is reasonable or that Ohio's 2004 rule describes every jurisdiction. Its express reservation of reasonableness is as important to its proper use as its affirmative answer on consideration.
