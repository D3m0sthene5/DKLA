# Rucho v. Common Cause
Nos. 18-422 and 18-726, 588 U.S. ___ (2019); assigned citation 139 S. Ct. 2484 | Supreme Court of the United States | June 27, 2019 | LRS-SUP-0001

## Facts: two States and opposing parties

The consolidated appeals challenged congressional maps drawn to favor Republicans in North Carolina and Democrats in Maryland. Both involved admitted partisan objectives. The question was whether federal courts could adjudicate claims that the resulting partisanship violated the federal Constitution. The Court did not deny that the maps were highly partisan. Court opinion, slip pp.1-2.

In North Carolina, Republican legislators instructed their mapmaker to produce a ten-Republican, three-Democrat delegation. Representative David Lewis defended that objective because he preferred Republican victories and did not think an eleven-to-two map possible. The legislature adopted the 2016 plan by a party-line vote. Republicans won ten of thirteen districts in 2016. In 2018, they won nine seats and Democrats three; a thirteenth apparent Republican victory was subject to a new election after fraud allegations. That unresolved seat should not be counted as a completed 2018 Republican victory in the Court's historical account. Id. at 2-3.

The dissent adds that the 2016 plan followed a judicial finding that two districts in the earlier plan were racial gerrymanders. Thomas Hofeller used detailed election data and technology to pursue the partisan objective while creating a remedial map. The distinction is important: a successful racial-gerrymandering challenge did not establish justiciability of a subsequent partisan-gerrymandering claim. Kagan dissent, slip pp.3-5.

In Maryland, Governor Martin O'Malley and Democratic legislators pursued a seven-Democrat, one-Republican delegation by converting the Sixth District, then long held by a Republican. Although population equality required removing only roughly 10,000 residents, the plan moved about 360,000 voters out and 350,000 in. It reduced registered Republicans by approximately 66,000 and increased registered Democrats by approximately 24,000. Adopted by a party-line vote, the map flipped the Sixth District in 2012, and a Democrat held it through the period described by the opinion. Court opinion, slip pp.5-6; Kagan dissent, slip pp.5-7. These are the opinion's historical facts, not assertions about subsequent election results.

## Procedure and claims

North Carolina litigation began in August 2016. The Democratic Party, Common Cause, individual voters, and later the League of Women Voters and additional voters challenged the plan. They alleged equal-protection vote dilution, First Amendment retaliation and associational injury, infringement of Article I §2's right of the people to choose representatives, and misuse of Elections Clause authority. A three-judge court found constitutional violations after trial; Judge Osteen dissented from the First Amendment ruling. Defendants appealed directly under 28 U.S.C. §1253. Court opinion, slip pp.3-4.

While that appeal was pending, Gill v. Whitford required a vote-dilution plaintiff to show a particular injury in a packed or cracked district. The Supreme Court remanded the North Carolina case for reconsideration. The district court again found standing and justiciability, invalidated twelve of thirteen districts under equal protection, reaffirmed other constitutional conclusions, and enjoined use of the plan after the November 2018 election. It rejected geography and incumbent protection as neutral explanations for the map's effects. Defendants again appealed. Id. at 4-5; 318 F. Supp. 3d 777 (M.D.N.C. 2018).

Three Maryland voters filed their action in November 2013. After intervening litigation over procedure and preliminary relief, the three-judge court granted summary judgment to plaintiffs under the First Amendment. It found diminished ability to elect their preferred candidate based on affiliation and voting history, along with burdens on fundraising, volunteer recruitment, campaigning, and voter interest. It permanently prohibited use of the plan and required a replacement for 2020. State defendants appealed directly. Court opinion, slip p.6; 348 F. Supp. 3d 493 (D. Md. 2018). The companion Supreme Court caption was Lamone v. Benisek.

Plaintiffs defended judicial review using intentional vote dilution and retaliatory burdens, supported by evidence of entrenchment, actual outcomes, and alternative-map analysis. State defendants contended that federal courts lacked a manageable constitutional standard; they also invoked the Elections Clause's allocation of electoral regulation to political institutions. The majority rejected the strongest version of the latter argument: the Clause does not make every electoral question exclusively congressional. It nonetheless found these particular federal constitutional claims nonjusticiable. Id. at 10, 22-30.

## Issue, holding, and exact judgment

Are claims that congressional districting is excessively partisan justiciable in federal court under the asserted constitutional theories? The five-Justice majority held that partisan-gerrymandering claims present political questions beyond federal judicial reach because the Constitution provides no judicially discoverable and manageable standards for allocating partisan political power. Id. at 6-7, 15-30.

The Supreme Court vacated both district-court judgments and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of jurisdiction. This is a jurisdictional disposition, not a merits determination that the challenged maps are fair or an order commanding adoption of replacement maps. The decision preserves distinctions between these partisan claims and judicially cognizable racial-gerrymandering and population-equality claims. Id. at 10-12, 34.

## Majority: constitutional authority and the standards problem

Article III limits federal courts to judicially resolvable cases and controversies. Under Baker v. Carr, absence of discoverable and manageable standards can make a claim a political question. A question's constitutional importance does not itself establish judicial authority to decide it. The Court framed the central problem as deciding when permissible political districting becomes excessive, rather than identifying whether political motivations existed. Id. at 6-7, 12-15.

The majority traced partisan districting to the founding era and discussed the Elections Clause's assignment of initial regulation to state legislatures subject to congressional revision. Congress had exercised that authority through districting and voting legislation. This history supported caution about inventing a federal judicial role in political allocation, but did not categorically exclude courts from election disputes: one-person, one-vote and racial-gerrymandering precedents remained valid. Id. at 8-12.

Prior decisions had not produced a controlling workable standard. Gaffney permitted consideration of party balance. Bandemer found the matter justiciable through a divided Court but generated no agreed test. Vieth's plurality favored nonjusticiability; Kennedy left open the possibility of a future precise, neutral standard. LULAC again failed to settle a standard. Gill resolved standing rather than justiciability. Rucho settled the latter threshold. Id. at 13-15.

For the majority, the first obstacle was selecting a legally grounded baseline. Claims about partisan seat outcomes implicitly seek some proper distribution of political influence, but the Constitution does not require proportional representation. Increasing competition, allocating safe seats to parties, and observing traditional districting criteria can produce different outcomes. Geography may naturally concentrate a party's voters; protecting incumbents also preserves a political distribution. The Constitution did not instruct judges how to rank those goals or decide what share a party deserved. Id. at 16-19.

The second obstacle was choosing how much deviation from the selected baseline was unconstitutional. Even a defined aspiration would not tell courts how competitive a district must be, how much packing is acceptable, or when partisan advantage crosses a constitutional line. Equal-population cases supply a mathematical equality rule for individual voting weight; they do not establish an entitlement to partisan representation in proportion to support. Racial-gerrymandering claims seek elimination of a suspect classification, while some political consideration in districting is permissible. Id. at 19-21.

## Why the majority rejected the proposed tests

North Carolina's equal-protection test examined predominant partisan intent, persistent vote dilution, and neutral explanations. Predominant intent could identify racial classification in race cases, but partisan motivation did not become constitutionally impermissible solely because it predominated. Predicting durable partisan advantage and a representative's future unresponsiveness also required uncertain assessments of elections, candidates, turnout, and voter behavior. The majority invoked past gerrymanders whose anticipated durability did not materialize. Requiring a legitimate alternative explanation at the final step did not solve those problems. Id. at 22-25.

The First Amendment theories likewise failed to draw a manageable line. The maps did not formally prohibit speech or association. Treating any partisan classification as prohibited viewpoint discrimination would exclude political considerations that precedent allowed. Anecdotal declines in fundraising or engagement and a minimal chilling-effect threshold supplied no sufficiently clear measure of unconstitutional burdens. The majority's conclusion addressed these districting claims; it did not hold that First Amendment retaliation is generally nonjusticiable. Id. at 25-27.

Kagan's proposed comparison to alternative maps respecting a State's nonpartisan criteria did not persuade the majority. A federal constitutional violation should not vary with criteria selected by the mapmakers themselves. Changes in those criteria could move the median and make the same map lawful or unlawful. Even accepting that baseline, the Court found no constitutional rule identifying how far from it was too far. Other legal inquiries into degree rested on statutory, constitutional, or common-law guidance the majority thought absent here. Id. at 27-29.

Article I §2 and the Elections Clause did not independently supply the missing enforceable partisan limit. The argument that voters should choose representatives rather than the reverse resembled a republican-government objection under the Guarantee Clause, which earlier decisions had treated as nonjusticiable. Id. at 29-30.

## Kagan's dissent: rights, baseline, and administrability

Kagan, joined by Ginsburg, Breyer, and Sotomayor, treated the maps as serious constitutional injuries to equal political participation and political association. Packing and cracking diminished the effect of selected citizens' votes because of party affiliation. In her view, the majority failed to respond to identifiable injuries simply because it mistrusted the available standards. She connected individual rights to the democratic danger of incumbents insulating themselves from voters. Kagan dissent, slip pp.1-13.

She agreed that judges should not select an ideal distribution of party seats or invalidate every political influence. Her proposed limiting framework required predominant entrenchment purpose, substantial vote dilution, and absence of a legitimate nonpartisan explanation. It targeted extreme cases rather than proportional representation. Id. at 14-18.

The relevant baseline, she argued, was what the State would have done given its own physical geography, actual distribution of voters, and nonpartisan districting priorities. That approach accepted urban partisan concentration rather than correcting it. Using different state criteria was a virtue because it respected state choices and prevented judges from imposing personal ideals. She illustrated the difference with evenly distributed Republican voters in Massachusetts and regionally concentrated Republican voters in Maryland: similar statewide support need not produce similar seat outcomes absent manipulation. Id. at 22-25.

The North Carolina evidence supplied a particularly strong illustration. One expert generated 3,000 maps meeting the State's criteria except partisan advantage, using the same election-data composite as the official mapmaker. Every alternative produced at least one more Democratic seat and 77 percent produced three or four more. Another expert generated 24,518 maps under more general criteria, with over 99 percent producing at least one more Democratic seat. The dissent used these distributions as evidence that the enacted plan was an extreme departure, not as a demand that a court impose the median map. Maryland's population transfers, partisan-registration shift, and repeated electoral effects supplied simpler evidence. Id. at 18-21 & nn.3-4.

Kagan rejected the characterization of that evidence as mere judicial guesswork about the future. The same detailed data that made contemporary gerrymanders effective could identify them. Courts routinely determine intent and substantial effects without universally fixed percentages. They could at minimum recognize these extreme cases, and refine legal boundaries through ordinary adjudication. Her full discussion also explained why proving entrenchment indirectly could impose a demanding practical limit once mapmakers stopped openly admitting their purpose. Id. at 9-10, 21, 25-29 & n.5.

She doubted that political reform was an adequate substitute: beneficiaries of gerrymandering had incentives to preserve it, congressional bills were proposals rather than enacted remedies, and initiatives were unavailable in many States. State-court decisions demonstrated judicial capacity in her view, even when their constitutional language was broad. The majority answered that state law could provide standards missing from the Federal Constitution. The dissent's proposed federal standards did not command a majority and therefore do not supply Rucho's governing rule. Id. at 29-33 & n.6; Court opinion, slip pp.31-32.

## Scope, alternatives, and alignment

Roberts wrote for Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch, and Kavanaugh. Kagan's dissent had four votes. There were no additional separate opinions. The majority expressly disclaimed approval of excessive partisan gerrymandering and identified state constitutional rules, legislation, commissions, and Congress's Elections Clause authority as potential responses. Its descriptions of particular state reforms and pending bills are historical discussion as of 2019; this brief does not certify their present form or subsequent enactment. Court opinion, slip pp.30-34.

For an exam, distinguish lack of standing from this lack of justiciability, partisan claims from racial or unequal-population claims, and a jurisdictional dismissal from approval on the merits. Both opinions invoke judicial restraint and Marbury, but disagree about whether rights and workable standards already exist. The majority's choice confines the federal judiciary; the dissent's choice would permit limited review of intentional, substantial entrenchment.
