# United States v. Moser

**266 U.S. 236 (1924) — Supreme Court of the United States — November 17, 1924.** Justice Sutherland delivered the Court's opinion. The report contains no separate opinion, dissent, or numerical vote tally. The assigned Civil Procedure account begins under “Defining and Characterizing the Issue,” printed page 1299, physical PDF page 655, and concludes at printed page 1300 on physical page 656 before *Sunnen*.

## Retirement pay and the disputed status

Moser was a retired naval officer seeking the difference between a captain's and a rear admiral's pay. He relied on § 11 of the Navy Personnel Act of March 3, 1899. That provision entitled an officer with a creditable record who had served during the Civil War to retirement with the rank and three-fourths of the sea pay of the next higher grade. His retirement followed forty years measured from entry into the Naval Academy, under a separate statute permitting retirement on an officer's application after forty years' service. The dispute concerned whether his service as an Academy cadet during the war counted as wartime service for the enhanced benefit. 266 U.S. at 239–240.

This was not the first installment suit. Moser had won three previous Court of Claims judgments, each depending on that same status question. In each, the Government had argued that cadet service did not count within the benefit statute. The fourth suit sought later installments, rather than recovery a second time for the installments previously awarded. That difference in demands mattered to which form of preclusion applied. Id. at 240–242.

## The Court of Claims' changed interpretations

Between the first and second Moser suits, the Court of Claims decided *Jasper*, a different claimant's case. It construed the law against the claimant after a later statute was brought to its attention. That statute excluded cadet service but contained a proviso for officers who had received an advance of grade at or since retirement. Thus the same court had changed its interpretation in litigation involving another officer. Id. at 240.

In Moser's second and third suits, however, the court held that its first judgment made his status res judicata. In the fourth suit it again relied on preclusion and also returned to its original statutory interpretation, concluding that the proviso preserved Moser's right. The Supreme Court affirmed solely on preclusion. It found it unnecessary to decide whether the Court of Claims' renewed interpretation of the benefit statutes was substantively correct. Id. at 240–241.

## Arguments and the question presented

The Government's reported argument stressed that later installments were distinct causes of action and disputed the application of preclusion to statutory issues. It also argued that the first Court of Claims adjudication had not considered the 1906 enactment and challenged Moser's entitlement on the merits. Those were advocacy positions in the report, not findings adopted by the Supreme Court. Id. at 237.

Moser's counsel argued that the adjudicated entitlement controlled later pay, that a judgment should not be limited by accounts of what judges had in mind, and that the later Moser adjudications had considered the statute in any event. Counsel also defended the underlying statutory merits, including the treatment of Academy service and protection against retroactive loss of rank. The Court did not need to adopt those alternative merits arguments. Id. at 238–239.

The controlling question was narrower: did the earlier adjudication of Moser's individual wartime-service status bind the same parties when he sought later installments, despite the Government's challenge to the legal basis of that determination? The Court held that it did. Id. at 241–242.

## Rule and reasoning

The Court distinguished two situations. When a later suit concerns the same claim or demand, a merits judgment bars the subsequent action. When the later suit concerns a different demand, the inquiry is whether the particular point presented was actually litigated and determined in the earlier action. In modern explanatory terms, the latter branch concerns issue preclusion rather than treating all future installment demands as a single claim. The opinion itself uses the historical term “res judicata” for its broader discussion. Id. at 241.

These suits fell within the second branch. Moser requested different installments, but his entitlement still depended on the same expressly adjudicated question: whether he occupied the status of an officer who had served during the Civil War. The legal interpretation supporting that determination did not turn the adjudicated individual status into a freely relitigable abstract proposition. Id. at 242.

The Court accepted a limited qualification to the Government's law-versus-fact argument. An unmixed legal rule announced in one case does not bind the same parties in a different-demand suit merely because they were parties to the first case. But a distinctly adjudged fact, question, or right cannot be disputed again between them simply by alleging an erroneous legal view or application. Otherwise a court would nominally honor the adjudicated matter while defeating its conclusive effect through an attack on its supporting reasoning. An individual's status on which recovery depends is as conclusive as any other adjudicated matter. Id.

This does not make every legal proposition in an earlier opinion binding forever. It ties conclusiveness to the right, question, or fact put in issue and directly determined, with the prior judgment remaining unmodified and the relevant circumstances identical. The opinion's cited formulations retain those conditions. It does not decide every possible effect of future statutory or factual changes. Id. at 241–242.

## Exact judgment and significance

The Court **affirmed the Court of Claims' judgment** awarding the later pay difference. It did not separately affirm the court's latest pension-statute interpretation on its merits. No judicial footnotes, appendix, or separate writing accompany the decision. Id. at 241–242.

The practical lesson is to identify the adjudicated issue precisely. Describing it at the broad level of “what the pension statute means” would obscure the earlier determination of Moser's particular status. Conversely, describing every later installment as identical to the earlier pay claim would obscure the opinion's express different-demand analysis. The Court preserved preclusion of the recurring status issue without collapsing those distinct demands into one. Id.
