# Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin

**417 U.S. 156 (1974) — decided May 28, 1974.** Justice Powell wrote for Chief Justice Burger and Justices Stewart, White, Blackmun, and Rehnquist. Justice Douglas dissented in part, joined by Justices Brennan and Marshall. Assigned Civil Procedure account: physical PDF pages 435–436, printed pages 843–844.

**Central holding.** In this Rule 23(b)(3) action, individual notice was required for class members identifiable through reasonable effort. A plaintiff's inability to finance that notice did not excuse it. The district court could not shift 90% of its proposed notice expense to genuinely adversarial defendants because a preliminary hearing suggested that the plaintiff would probably win. The Supreme Court vacated the appellate judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss the class action as originally defined, without prejudice to attempts to redefine it. The Court did not decide the lower court's separate objections concerning manageability or fluid-class recovery. (172 n.10, 173–179 & nn.14–16.)

## The underlying claims and their practical stakes

Morton Eisen filed suit in the Southern District of New York on May 2, 1966, alleging antitrust and securities-law violations arising from odd-lot trading on the New York Stock Exchange. An odd lot contained fewer than one hundred shares. Unlike round lots, these trades were handled by special dealers acting as principals. Carlisle & Jacquelin and DeCoppet & Doremus handled 99% of this business during the relevant period. Investors paid an odd-lot differential in addition to their ordinary brokerage commission. (159–160 & nn.1–2.)

Eisen alleged that the dealers monopolized odd-lot trading and set excessive differentials, violating Sherman Act §§ 1 and 2. He sought treble damages for the overcharge, damages against the Exchange for failing to regulate the differential under the securities laws, attorney fees, and an injunction against excessive future charges. The proposed class ultimately encompassed odd-lot traders from May 1, 1962, through June 30, 1966. Eisen's own claimed loss was only $70. The Court recognized that competent counsel would not undertake this complex litigation for that individual recovery: economically, a class action was the only realistic route. That concern explains the lower courts' struggle but did not determine the meaning of the notice rule. No trial had established the alleged violations after eight years of litigation. (159–162.)

## Three appellate stages and the attempted notice solution

The district court initially rejected class treatment. In *Eisen I*, the Second Circuit allowed an immediate appeal because eliminating the class would practically end the suit—the “death knell” reasoning reported in the procedural history. In *Eisen II*, it reversed and remanded for further consideration of adequacy of representation, manageability, notice, and other pertinent issues. It rejected Rule 23(b)(1) and (b)(2) as bases for this suit; the remaining possibility was a damages class under (b)(3). It also purported to retain appellate jurisdiction during the remand. These were earlier Second Circuit decisions, not the Supreme Court's holdings on every certification appeal. (161–165 & nn.3–4.)

After evidentiary hearings, affidavits, and stipulations, the district judge found the action maintainable. To address the difficulty of distributing millions of small recoveries, the court proposed a fluid recovery: unclaimed damages would subsidize reduced odd-lot differentials for future traders. On notice, the proposed class comprised roughly six million people, institutions, and intermediaries. Computerized trading and brokerage records could identify about two million by name and address, and another 250,000 participants in special investment programs could also be identified. Individual notice to all 2.25 million would cost $225,000 under the postage assumptions then used; footnote 7 gives $315,000 after the subsequent postage increase. (165–167 & nn.5–7.)

The district court instead designed a $21,720 package: individual notice to Exchange member firms and large bank trust departments, approximately 2,000 identifiable frequent traders, and 5,000 randomly selected members, combined with newspaper publication for others. Eisen refused to finance even that reduced package. The judge held a preliminary merits hearing, found him more likely than not to succeed, and charged defendants 90%—$19,548—of the proposed cost. That allocation sought to preserve a potentially meritorious small-claim suit while avoiding automatic cost shifting in groundless cases. (167–168.)

In *Eisen III*, the Second Circuit rejected publication as a substitute for individual notice to identifiable members, disapproved the preliminary merits hearing and cost shift, rejected fluid recovery, and held the class unmanageable. It directed dismissal of the class aspect. Eisen then challenged both the court's appellate jurisdiction and its rulings. (168–169.)

## Why the notice-cost order was immediately appealable

The Supreme Court found jurisdiction under § 1291 and the collateral-order reasoning of *Cohen*. The district court had conclusively rejected defendants' asserted right not to finance notice to their opponents' proposed class. That ruling concerned a collateral issue distinct from the underlying antitrust and securities claims, comparable to the security-for-expenses issue in *Cohen*. It was therefore appealable as a final decision even though liability remained unresolved. The Court permitted review of the associated notice problems because the cost allocation was part of the district court's integrated interpretation of Rule 23(c)(2). (169–172 & nn.8–9.)

This holding did not depend on the Second Circuit's purported retention of jurisdiction after *Eisen II*. The Court expressly declined to decide that alternative theory. Nor did its discussion endorse immediate appeals from all certification rulings based on a death knell. Its operative jurisdictional ground was the conclusive collateral notice-cost order. Because notice alone disposed of this class as defined, the Court also left manageability and fluid recovery, including the jurisdictional basis for addressing those issues, unresolved. (170, 172 n.10.)

## Notice could not be reduced to match the plaintiff's resources

The historical version of Rule 23(c)(2) required the best practicable notice in (b)(3) actions, including individual notice to members identifiable through reasonable effort. Members had to be informed of the option to exclude themselves, the binding effect of judgment if they remained, and the possibility of appearing through counsel. The language and Advisory Committee explanation made individual notice mandatory rather than discretionary. *Mullane* and *Schroeder* reinforced why publication poorly substitutes for direct notice when names and addresses are ascertainable. (173–175 & nn.11–12.)

Eisen argued that full notice was prohibitively expensive and that members' tiny stakes made opting out unlikely. Neither argument authorized dispensing with the rule. A member could still choose separate litigation or participate in the action, and notice protected that choice before a binding judgment. Footnote 13 also rejected his limitations argument by referring to *American Pipe*'s class-action tolling holding. Adequate representation could not replace notice: Rule 23 demanded both. The Court did not need to rest its conclusion solely on the outer constitutional minimum because the Rule itself commanded the result. (175–177 & nn.13–14.)

The scope matters. Footnote 14 confines this notice ruling to (b)(3) classes and states that this subdivision's requirement did not apply by its terms to (b)(2) injunctive or declaratory classes. Eisen does not hold that every kind of class proceeding requires the same notice mechanism. (177 n.14.)

## Why expected success did not justify shifting notice costs

Rule 23 supplied no authority for the district court's preliminary merits adjudication as a route to class treatment and shifting notice costs. The correct inquiry was whether Rule 23's requirements were satisfied, not whether the claimant would probably win. A tentative determination of liability before those requirements were established could also prejudice defendants: it lacked the safeguards of a civil trial and could color later proceedings. (177–178.)

The usual rule placed initial notice costs on the representative plaintiff financing the suit. The district court's suggested exceptions involved pre-existing fiduciary relationships, such as derivative litigation; the Supreme Court expressly reserved the proper cost allocation in those different settings. Here the relationship was genuinely adversarial, so Eisen had to pay. This is a historical holding against this merits-based cost shift, not a claim that every later notice-related task can never be allocated differently. (178–179 & n.15.)

## The judgment and Douglas's partial dissent

Because Eisen consistently refused to finance notice to the class as defined, the Court ordered that class dismissed on remand. It vacated the Second Circuit judgment rather than simply affirming all its grounds. Footnote 16 left redefinition under Rule 23(c)(4) or Rule 15 open and expressed no view about a suitable smaller class or whether Eisen would pay for its notice. (179 & n.16.)

Douglas, Brennan, and Marshall generally agreed with the issues the Court addressed but emphasized subclass possibilities. Monthly-investment-plan participants, payroll-plan participants, frequent traders, or buyers in a particular period might form manageable groups. Existing brokerage correspondence might lower marginal notice costs. Whether Eisen belonged to a useful subclass remained unknown. Douglas favored judicial adjustment of class boundaries without unnecessarily amending the full-class complaint, preserving flexibility to expand or narrow it. He also acknowledged unresolved tolling and preclusion questions concerning investors outside a litigating subclass. His broader concern was access to remedies for diffuse, individually small injuries; these proposals were not certification findings adopted by the Court. (179–186 & all eight dissenting notes.)
