# Bereslavsky v. Caffey

**Civil Procedure — A jury demand after substitution of legal for exclusively equitable relief.** United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit, May 7, 1947. **161 F.2d 499.** Judge Frank wrote for a panel comprising Judges Augustus N. Hand, Chase, and Frank. No separate writing is reported. The assigned paragraph is in the Civil Procedure casebook, physical PDF page 554, printed page 1092.

## The claim and the challenged order

Bereslavsky was the plaintiff in a patent dispute. The original complaint sought exclusively equitable relief; the assigned account identifies that request as an injunction against infringement. His patent expired during a lengthy delay. He then amended the complaint to abandon the equitable remedy and seek only money. This change mattered because the original issues did not carry a right to jury trial, whereas the resulting claim for money did. The complete opinion does not identify the allegedly infringing product, the amount demanded, or the merits of infringement, and those facts should not be supplied by inference. 161 F.2d at 499–500; id., judicial note 4; assigned p.1092.

More than ten days had elapsed since service of the last pleading directed to the issues under the original complaint and amended answer. But Bereslavsky demanded a jury within ten days of his amendment substituting legal relief. District Judge Caffey struck the demand. The case reached the Second Circuit as a request for mandamus directed to that judge, explaining why the caption names Caffey rather than the underlying alleged infringer. The procedural question was whether the original period had foreclosed a jury on the newly created legal issue. Id. at 499–501.

## Holding and timing rationale

The panel held the demand timely. When the original pleadings were served, there was no issue triable to a jury as of right. A deadline governing such issues could not cause the plaintiff to lose a jury right he did not then possess. His amendment first created the relevant legal issue; the demand made within the ensuing ten days was therefore effective. Id. at 499–500.

The court’s reasoning is more precise than saying that every amendment restarts the jury-demand clock. The operative change was from an exclusively equitable claim to one seeking only legal relief. The decision does not address an amendment merely elaborating a previously pleaded, already jury-triable issue. Nor does it treat the missed original pleading period as a general permission to make untimely demands whenever a litigant changes strategy. It ties the new period to the first appearance of the right-bearing issue. Id. at 500.

## Procedural merger did not erase the relevant distinctions

The underlying defendant apparently argued that the Federal Rules had eliminated the difference between law and equity altogether. Frank rejected that premise. Combining their procedures did not necessarily merge their substantive principles or eliminate the distinctions relevant to a jury right. The opinion defended the flexibility associated with equitable doctrine, while expressly recognizing that this case did not decide a question of substantive equity. Its notes discussed comparative legal systems and warnings that procedural fusion could harden equity into inflexible legal rules. Those reflections explained the court’s rejection of complete obliteration; they were not a new holding about the substantive law of patents. Id. at 500, judicial notes 1–3a.

The panel also distinguished *Clark v. Wooster* and *Rice & Adams Corp. v. Lathrop*. In those cases, defendants sought to end an equity proceeding after a patent expired, while the plaintiffs had not elected to substitute an action at law. The courts could retain the equitable jurisdiction they originally acquired. Here, the plaintiff himself abandoned equitable relief and elected a claim solely for money. The prior decisions thus did not establish that the old equitable mode of trial had to persist despite his change. Id. at 500.

Frank acknowledged the panel’s view that juries were generally undesirable in patent cases. But that preference could not defeat the established right to a jury when the sole claim was for money and the demand was timely. Judicial assessments of which tribunal might handle a subject better did not determine whether this litigant had waived his legal entitlement. Id. at 500.

Note 4 added that the switch was particularly understandable because the delay during which the patent expired followed the Judge Advocate General’s suggestion. The panel expressly declined to rest its decision on that special circumstance. The rule therefore did not depend on attributing the delay to government advice or finding an equitable excuse for lateness. Id., judicial note 4.

## Mandamus and the practical result

The court held that mandamus was available in aid of its appellate jurisdiction, citing the governing statutory authority and precedents. It **directed issuance of a writ requiring Judge Caffey to vacate the order striking the jury demand**. This was a procedural victory restoring jury entitlement, not a judgment establishing infringement or awarding damages. Id. at 501 & judicial note 5.

The assigned paragraph illustrates how the character of newly pleaded relief can affect the time for demanding a jury. Its description that the amendment “renewed” the right compresses the panel’s reasoning: on the original equitable claim there had been no jury issue, and the right first arose with the legal claim. The ten-day period discussed here is the historical period applied in 1947, not a verified statement of the current deadline. No numerical vote is printed beyond the reported panel and single opinion.
