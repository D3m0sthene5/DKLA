# Appalachian Power Co. v. Environmental Protection Agency
208 F.3d 1015 (D.C. Cir. 2000) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Court, challenge, and judgment

On April 14, 2000, Judge Randolph wrote for a unanimous D.C. Circuit panel with Judges Williams and Henderson in consolidated Nos. 98-1512, 98-1536–98-1538, 98-1540, and 98-1542. Electric utilities and chemical- and petroleum-industry associations directly challenged EPA's September 1998 Periodic Monitoring Guidance. Alternatively, they challenged the 1992 Title V permit rule if it already authorized the guidance's requirements. The court found reviewable final agency action and held that the challenged instructions substantively expanded the rule without the required rulemaking. Because the document's parts were intertwined, it set aside the entire guidance. There were no separate opinions. (1017, 1023–1028.)

## Permits, existing standards, and the monitoring dispute

Title V of the 1990 Clean Air Act amendments required stationary sources to obtain operating permits under EPA-approved state or local programs. EPA could object to proposed permits; an objection prevented issuance, and unresolved objections could result in EPA issuing or denying the permit. Existing federal and state emission standards became permit terms. Those standards often already specified testing or monitoring: lead smelters had daily pressure-drop monitoring and scheduled equipment checks; particular industrial operations had instrument requirements. Monitoring was therefore a substantive component of the existing standards, not simply paperwork added to a numerical emissions limit. (1017–1019, nn. 1–6.)

EPA's 1992 rule, 40 C.F.R. § 70.6(a)(3), distinguished incorporation of applicable testing requirements from gap-filling periodic monitoring. Subsection (i)(B) required periodic monitoring where the applicable requirement lacked periodic testing or instrumental or noninstrumental monitoring. The parties agreed that a standard with no monitoring, an unspecified testing frequency, or only a one-time startup test needed supplementation. Petitioners argued that a standard already requiring testing at regular intervals should instead be incorporated as written. (1017–1020, nn. 7–8.)

The 19-page 1998 guidance required permit authorities to review whether existing monitoring adequately assured compliance and to add requirements when it did not. It presumed certain post-1990 federal standards and acid-rain requirements sufficient but otherwise called for an assessment of monitoring adequacy, rather than merely the presence of testing at regular intervals. Petitioners argued that this directed state officials to revise federal rules and state implementation plans through individual permits, bypassing the procedures for amending those underlying standards. (1019–1020, 1023–1025.)

## Finality depended on EPA's settled instructions and practical consequences

EPA argued that the guidance was neither binding nor final. The court distinguished a properly promulgated legislative rule's legal force from the practical binding effect of other agency documents. Headquarters instructions could bind in practice if field staff treated them as controlling, enforcement rested on them, or permit authorities understood that noncompliant permits would be invalid. The court focused on the challenged portions, not the label attached to every sentence of the document. EPA itself conceded that its position requiring sufficiency review was settled. (1020–1022, nn. 10–14.)

Both Bennett finality requirements were satisfied. Drafts had circulated in 1994 and May 1998 before the September version stated EPA's unequivocal position. State authorities were required to assess and augment existing monitoring. Possible future revision did not make that completed position tentative: even statutes could change. Legal consequences followed because EPA expected permit authorities and regulated sources to comply. The court did not equate every binding instruction with final action; an interlocutory order could bind without finally deciding a matter, and some final agency actions need not impose obligations on a private party. (1022–1023 & n. 15.)

A closing disclaimer denied final agency action and enforceable rights. It did not undo mandatory instructions throughout the document or prevent the creation of obligations. Petitioners reported, without EPA disputing it, that state permit negotiations were producing continuous-opacity-monitor requirements even where existing standards specified visual observation. That concrete example supported practical consequences. The court also rejected EPA's ripeness objection: the legality of directing broad sufficiency review and supplementation did not depend on a particular permit's details, and Congress provided direct review of nationally applicable EPA action. It expressed no view on petitioners' assertion that challenges to individual permits belonged exclusively in state courts. (1023–1024, nn. 17–18.)

## The guidance enlarged the regulation instead of explaining its existing gap-filling duty

The court read periodic testing in its ordinary sense: testing at yearly, monthly, weekly, daily, or hourly intervals. When an existing standard already required such testing, subsection (i)(B)'s supplementary authority was not triggered. A one-time test or omission of a monitoring frequency was different. EPA's guidance nevertheless required adequacy review and possible additional testing even when a standard already contained periodic testing. That was a substantial expansion. (1024–1025.)

EPA invoked its 1992 preamble and responses to comments. Their statements that monitoring beyond an underlying requirement might be necessary were consistent with filling missing frequencies or replacing a one-time startup test. More importantly, EPA had promised to amend insufficient federal monitoring requirements through rulemaking. That promise conflicted with the new approach of having state authorities revise the requirements through permits. Neither the rule's text nor contemporaneous explanations gave states a general commission to rework existing federal and state standards. (1025–1026, nn. 23–24.)

Calling the changes monitoring requirements did not make them insubstantial. A different compliance-measurement method could change an emissions limit's stringency; more frequent stack testing imposed additional costs. The Title V regulations themselves said the program did not impose new substantive requirements. The guidance also blurred the rule's distinction between periodic testing, instrumental or noninstrumental monitoring, and supplementary periodic monitoring. Treating them as interchangeable left unexplained why the rule separately identified the conditions triggering supplementation. A prior decision's abbreviated quotation of the regulation omitted precisely those triggering conditions and therefore did not establish EPA's broader interpretation. (1026–1028, nn. 25–26.)

Under the circuit's then-governing doctrine, a substantive addition or amendment could not avoid required notice and comment by being called an interpretation. The guidance effectively amended subsection (i)(B); EPA had to follow 42 U.S.C. § 7607(d), and potentially the small-entity procedures unless it made the requisite certification. The court did not reach petitioners' separate Compliance Assurance Monitoring challenge or decide the alternative attack on the original rule. (1024 n. 19; 1028 & n. 27.)

## Exact remedy, addendum, and assigned limits

The entire guidance was set aside because there was substantial doubt EPA would adopt the unchallenged, intertwined portions independently. State permitting authorities could not use this guidance or subsection (i)(B) to demand more frequent monitoring than an applicable standard already provided, except where the standard lacked periodic testing, omitted a frequency, or required only a one-time test. This was vacatur, not remand while the guidance remained operative. It did not eliminate states' separately recognized power to adopt more stringent standards or permit requirements through lawful channels. (1019 n. 6; 1028.)

The court appended three pages of EPA's draft decision flow chart, illustrating the expanding administrative assessment. It moves from identifying applicable requirements and current monitoring through consultation with permitting authorities, adequacy review, possible modifications, and selection of other approaches using technical and economic feasibility and source characteristics. The graphical addendum was read from the original reporter pages because extracted text omitted its contents. (1029–1031.)
