# Berry v. Reagan — district preliminary injunction

32 Employment Practices Decisions (CCH) ¶ 31,304 | Legislation and the Regulatory State | District of Columbia

## Distinct stage and source limitation

This is the separately assigned district-court stage, not the D.C. Circuit's later mootness order at 732 F.2d 949. The supplied textbook identifies the district injunction in its discussion of removal authority, printed 190–191, PDF 120–121 of the September selection. Later judicial citations identify the district decision as No. 83-3182, 1983 WL 538 (D.D.C. November 14, 1983). The complete district opinion has not been recovered. This account therefore describes what the assigned passage establishes and preserves the unresolved original-source gap.

## Controversy and requested protection

The textbook reports that President Reagan removed three Civil Rights Commissioners in 1983. The Commission was then statutorily situated in the executive branch under 42 U.S.C. § 1975(a) (1982). Civil-rights groups and members of Congress disputed the propriety of the removals, treating the Commission as operationally independent despite that designation. The district court issued a preliminary injunction blocking the removals. (Assigned 190–191.)

The later appellate caption identifies Mary Frances Berry and others opposite President Reagan. The assigned passage does not identify every plaintiff, specify damages, or reproduce the district court's findings about individual appointments or removal letters. Those details are not reconstructed here from assumption.

## Issue and supported result

The controversy concerned whether the Commission's executive-branch designation necessarily gave the President unrestricted removal authority over its members. The supported district result is preliminary injunctive protection against the removals. That result cannot be presented as a recovered final merits judgment establishing every aspect of the Commission's tenure protections. The assigned account does not supply the district court's full statutory construction, constitutional analysis, preliminary-injunction test, or separate treatment of its factors. (Assigned 190–191.)

## Textbook's interpretive use

The editors use Berry to illustrate the limits of agency labels in debates over independence. The executive designation did not foreclose independence arguments and did not prevent the district injunction. They also discuss Hannah v. Larche's characterization of the Commission's investigative functions and ask whether Congress could appoint commissioners. Those observations and questions are editorial comparisons; the passage does not establish that the Berry court decided the appointment question. (Assigned 190–191.)

The editors report a political compromise retaining two of the three commissioners and a new enabling act, Pub. L. No. 98-183, 97 Stat. 1301 (1983), expanding membership from six to eight and making the Commission independent. These are the textbook's historical statements, distinct from a reconstruction of the district opinion's findings. (Assigned 190.)

## Subsequent disposition

The complete later circuit order explains that the Commission's authorized period of existence as then constituted had expired. On November 30, 1983, the D.C. Circuit vacated the district judgment and remanded with directions to dismiss the suit as moot. It did not affirm or reject the district court's removal-power reasoning on the merits. Accordingly, this district injunction must not be described as an appellate holding that Reagan lacked removal power. The circuit stage is separately briefed. (732 F.2d 949.)
