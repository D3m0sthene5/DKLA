# FCC v. Fox Television Stations, Inc.
567 U.S. 239 (2012) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Court, consolidated cases, and precise judgment

On June 21, 2012, Justice Kennedy wrote for seven Justices in No. 10-1293, together with FCC v. ABC under Rule 12.4. Roberts, Scalia, Thomas, Breyer, Alito, and Kagan joined; Ginsburg concurred only in the judgment, and Sotomayor did not participate. All eight participating Justices agreed to vacate the Second Circuit judgments and remand. The Court held FCC had not given Fox and ABC fair notice that their specific pre-policy-change broadcasts could be actionably indecent. It set aside the agency orders on that as-applied due-process ground, without adopting the Second Circuit's broad First Amendment invalidation of the policy. (243, 253–259.)

## The old policy and three broadcasts

Section 1464 prohibited obscene, indecent, and profane broadcast language, and FCC enforced indecency restrictions between 6 a.m. and 10 p.m. Pacifica had sustained regulation of Carlin's repeated monologue, while leaving occasional expletives unresolved. FCC subsequently broadened its contextual approach beyond a fixed word list but continued emphasizing the difference between isolated and repeated material. Its 2001 guidance identified explicitness, repetition or dwelling, and pandering, titillation, or shock value as significant considerations. It included examples in which fleeting nature weighed against indecency, though some brief references were offensive because of other factors. (243–247.)

Fox's first broadcast was Cher's unscripted expletive during the 2002 Billboard Music Awards. The second was Nicole Richie's unscripted use of the F-word and S-word during the 2003 awards. ABC's distinct case concerned a February 25, 2003 NYPD Blue shower scene: an adult character's nude buttocks appeared for about seven seconds and the side of her breast for a moment when a child entered the bathroom. These broadcasts all preceded FCC's 2004 Golden Globes change. The ABC incident here was momentary nudity, not the NYPD Blue expletive complaint discussed in the separate Fox proceedings. (247–248.)

The Golden Globes order addressed Bono's 2003 remark. FCC reversed its bureau and earlier fleeting-expletive rulings, treating the F-word as inherently sexual even when nonliteral and rejecting a categorical exemption for isolated use. FCC then applied the changed approach to Fox and ABC. (248–249.)

## Agency orders and earlier appellate stages

After Fox sought review of the 2006 omnibus order, a voluntary remand allowed FCC to consider objections. The agency reaffirmed the Billboard indecency findings but imposed no forfeiture. It acknowledged that penalizing Cher's remark had not been apparent when broadcast and withheld a proposed penalty for Richie's remark in light of the remand's limited nature. The Second Circuit's 2007 APA judgment rejected the explanation for changing policy; the Supreme Court's 2009 decision reversed that conclusion and remanded for constitutional review. The 2010 Second Circuit decision then invalidated the policy in its entirety for vagueness and chilling. (249–252.)

ABC's path was separate. In February 2008 FCC found the shower scene actionably indecent and assessed $27,500 against each of 45 affiliated stations, nearly $1.24 million in total. It treated buttocks as sexual or excretory organs and the scene as pandering and titillating. The Second Circuit vacated that forfeiture in a 2011 summary order because its broad Fox judgment controlled. The Supreme Court reviewed both judgments in this proceeding. (252, 256.)

## Due process required notice before the conduct occurred

The Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause requires fair notice of prohibited conduct and standards that constrain arbitrary or discriminatory enforcement. When speech is affected, rigorous adherence also prevents ambiguity from chilling protected expression. The problem was not difficulty proving an incriminating fact; it was uncertainty about what conduct counted as a violation. (253–254.)

The Court left facial validity aside. At the time of these broadcasts, repetition was a key consideration and prior policy treated fleeting material differently. Only afterward did the Golden Globes order change course. Applying the new principle to the earlier broadcasts failed to give Fox and ABC notice that those brief incidents could violate the statute as FCC interpreted and enforced it. Such an abrupt regulatory change would present notice concerns on any subject, especially one touching protected speech. (254–255.)

## No Fox fine did not remove the constitutional problem

The Government conceded Fox had lacked reasonable notice for nonrepeated expletives but argued the absence of sanctions prevented a vagueness claim and FCC's promise not to use the findings in license renewals or elsewhere mooted the issue. The Court rejected that position. FCC retained statutory authority to consider prior offenses in future penalties, and voluntary assurances were insufficient to cure a constitutional violation. (255.)

The legal consequence was also combined with reputational injury: permanent agency findings describing wrongdoing could affect viewers' and advertisers' judgments. The Court did not treat reputational harm alone as an independent due-process claim or find that a future penalty had already occurred. Its analysis paired reputational consequences with the agency's continuing legal power. (255–256.)

## ABC had not been warned by the old nudity precedents

The Government could not rely on forbearance as to ABC because the fine was substantial. It instead cited a 1960 statement that televising nudes might raise a statutory issue. That isolated, ambiguous warning did not establish fair notice for the seven-second scene. Other agency decisions had declined to find isolated or brief nudity indecent, including one treating 30 seconds of buttocks as brief in context. Those decisions did not establish that all nudity was permissible, but they undercut the claim that ABC had affirmative notice of this particular violation. (256–257.)

FCC's comparison of the scene's length and number of shots with other broadcasts did not identify a clear preexisting line. Combined with the absence of relevant notice in the 2001 guidance, the record established ABC's lack of constitutionally sufficient notice before sanction. The agency standards were vague as applied to these broadcasts, and the orders had to be set aside. (257–258.)

## Reserved issues, concurrence, and scope

The Court made three express limitations. Because due-process notice resolved the cases, it did not decide the First Amendment implications or reconsider Pacifica. It did not decide the current policy's constitutionality. And FCC remained free to modify its policy consistent with the public interest and law, with courts free to review that policy or any replacement through its content and application. The final decree vacated both Second Circuit judgments and remanded for proceedings consistent with these principles. (258–259.)

Ginsburg agreed with the judgment but stated that Pacifica had been wrong when decided and deserved reconsideration in light of time, technology, and the agency's untenable rulings. That was her separate view, not a holding overruling Pacifica. There was no dissent. The majority and concurrence contain no judicial footnotes; the reporter's asterisk and dagger address the paired case and counsel, not additional judicial reasoning. (259; reporter front matter 239, 242–243.)
