# Krupski v. Costa Crociere S.p.A.

**Civil Procedure — 560 U.S. 538 (2010). Supreme Court of the United States, June 7, 2010. Justice Sotomayor wrote the Court’s opinion. Justice Scalia concurred in part and in the judgment, declining its reliance on Advisory Committee intentions. The judgment was unanimous, 9–0.**

## A passenger sued the wrong related company

On February 21, 2007, Wanda Krupski tripped over a cable aboard the Costa Magica and fractured her femur. Her ticket identified Costa Crociere, an Italian corporation, as the carrier. But the ticket was issued by Costa Cruise Lines, which it described as the carrier’s sales and marketing agent; its front displayed Costa Cruise’s Florida address and advertised an ambiguously identified entity called Costa Cruises. These related names and roles mattered to the later mistake inquiry. 560 U.S. at 541–543.

The ticket required written notice of an injury claim within 185 days, filing suit within one year, and service on the carrier within 120 days after filing. It also designated the federal district court in southern Florida for the category of case involved here. Krupski’s lawyer notified Costa Cruise in July 2007, and its claims administrator responded to pursue a possible prelitigation settlement without saying that Costa Cruise was not responsible. No settlement followed. Id. at 542–543.

On February 1, 2008, three weeks before the contractual filing deadline, Krupski sued Costa Cruise for negligence in diversity jurisdiction and served it three days later. Her complaint alleged that Costa Cruise owned, operated, managed, supervised, and controlled the ship and had breached its duty to prevent the accident. Those allegations described the carrier’s activities but attached them to the marketing company. Id. at 543.

After the one-year period expired, Costa Cruise identified Costa Crociere as the proper defendant in its answer, its corporate disclosure statement, and its summary-judgment motion. Krupski sought limited discovery and leave to amend, explaining that the travel documents, company website, Florida registration information, and response to her claim had led her to regard Costa Cruise as responsible. The court allowed amendment on July 2, 2008; she filed the amended complaint July 11 and served Costa Crociere August 21, within the court’s deadline. Costa Cruise was dismissed by stipulation on that same August date. Costa Crociere, represented by the same counsel, then sought dismissal as untimely. Id. at 543–545.

## Lower courts focused on her knowledge and delay

Under Rule 15(c)(1)(C), a change in the party or its name could relate back if the claim concerned the same conduct, transaction, or occurrence; the added party received notice within the Rule 4(m) period sufficient to avoid prejudice in defending; and that party knew or should have known during that period that it would have been sued but for a mistake about the proper party’s identity. The service period ordinarily applied in this 2010 decision was 120 days. Id. at 545, 547.

The district court found the same-occurrence and notice requirements satisfied: Costa Crociere had constructive notice and identified no unfair prejudice. It nevertheless concluded that Krupski had deliberately selected another defendant rather than made an identity mistake, stressing the disclosures and her subsequent delay. The Eleventh Circuit affirmed. It relied on the ticket she had given counsel before the deadline and treated her as knowing or chargeable with knowing Costa Crociere’s identity. It also regarded the delay in seeking and filing amendment as grounds to deny relation back. Id. at 545–546.

## Holding: the added party’s understanding controls the mistake inquiry

The Supreme Court rejected both reasons. Rule 15(c)(1)(C)(ii) asks what the prospective defendant knew or should have known within the Rule 4(m) period—not whether the plaintiff possessed information from which she could have identified it when filing. Knowledge that an entity exists does not establish that the plaintiff correctly understands its status or role. A plaintiff may know both companies’ names yet misunderstand which performed the activities producing the claim. Id. at 548–549.

A fully informed decision to sue one party instead of another is different: it is inconsistent with an identity mistake. But choosing deliberately on a mistaken understanding of the entities’ roles does not become an informed choice merely because both names were known. Nor is the reasonableness of the plaintiff’s mistake an independent requirement. The plaintiff’s information matters only insofar as it helps determine the prospective defendant’s reasonable understanding of whether a mistake occurred. Id. at 548–550.

This reading balances merits adjudication against repose. An unsuspecting defendant reasonably believing that the limitations period expired without an effort to sue it has a legitimate interest in finality. An entity that understood it escaped naming only because the plaintiff misunderstood its identity has a weaker claim: enforcing the deadline on that basis would confer a windfall. The rule retained meaningful notice and prejudice requirements rather than abolishing limitations defenses generally. Id. at 550.

The Court also consulted the history of the 1966 amendment, designed to address timely suits challenging federal benefits decisions that mistakenly named the government, department, or former official rather than the proper current official. Such litigants might have possessed documents identifying the correct party, yet their error was still the kind the amendment addressed. Id. at 550–551.

## An informed later change and an identity mistake remain distinct

Costa Crociere relied on Nelson, where a prevailing claimant added a corporation’s owner after becoming concerned that the corporation could not pay a fee award. Krupski explained that the initial claim there did not suggest an intended claim against the owner, and the later change reflected a newly developed collection strategy rather than an original misunderstanding of his role. That example did not establish a categorical bar whenever the original plaintiff knew the prospective defendant existed. Id. at 551–552 & n.4.

Similarly, note 2 did not purport to resolve every cited circuit decision involving unknown or misidentified defendants on its particular facts. The assigned hypotheticals involving unnamed officers therefore remain applications to analyze, not additional factual situations the Supreme Court decided here. Note 3 also explains that the rule refers generally to changes in a pleading and the party against whom a claim is asserted; the Court used a plaintiff adding a defendant as shorthand for the typical situation. Id. at 546–548 & nn.2–3.

## Diligence is not an extra relation-back requirement

Rule 15(c) specifies the conditions for relation back and mandates that consequence when they are met. It does not create an additional equitable test of whether the plaintiff moved quickly enough. That differs from Rule 15(a), under which undue delay can matter to the court’s discretionary decision to permit amendment. Permission to amend and the resulting amendment’s filing-date treatment are separate inquiries. Id. at 552–554.

Postfiling conduct may nevertheless inform the prospective defendant’s understanding of whether the original omission was a mistake. It is relevant for that limited evidentiary purpose, rather than as a separate penalty for delay. Note 5 further rejects a requirement that the plaintiff amend or serve the amended pleading within the Rule 4(m) period: the rule requires timely sufficient notice of the action, which need not be formal service of the amendment. Note 6 observes that Krupski had moved within the scheduling order’s time and need not automatically accept the first defendant’s unproved assertions at face value. Id. at 553–556 & nn.5–6.

## Applying the rule to these companies

Costa Crociere did not challenge the finding that it received constructive notice within the relevant period. The original complaint plainly sought recovery from the entity operating and controlling the ship. Costa Crociere should have recognized that those activities were its own, mistakenly attributed to Costa Cruise. The company offered no persuasive strategy that would explain a fully informed choice to seek relief exclusively from an entity unable to provide it. Id. at 554–555.

The shared corporate relationship, very similar names, and confusing ticket presentation reinforced that inference. Awareness of the ticket’s contents did not preclude misunderstanding the roles, and Krupski’s later conduct did not establish that she had made an informed initial decision to omit Costa Crociere. The Court concluded that the added defendant should have known it was not named because of an identity mistake. Id. at 555–557.

The Court **reversed the Eleventh Circuit and remanded for further proceedings**. It removed the erroneous relation-back grounds for dismissal; it did not determine negligence, order compensation, or reinstate Costa Cruise as a liable defendant. Id. at 557.

## Scalia and the assigned excerpt

Scalia agreed with the result and the opinion apart from its use of Advisory Committee intentions to establish the rule’s meaning. He accepted the Committee’s interpretive observations as useful commentary but insisted that the enacted rule’s text controlled even if those adopting it had agreed with the Committee’s intentions. His concurrence is an interpretive-method qualification, not a dissent against relation back. Id. at 557.

The assignment attributes the long quotation to 548 alone, but its retained text spans 548–551; its later delay quotations cite syllabus page 540, while the Court’s reasoning is at 553–554. The nearby reference to a service period of ninety days is the assignment’s later-rule context, not the historical 120-day period applied in this case.
