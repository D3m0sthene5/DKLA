# American Express Co. v. Italian Colors Restaurant

**570 U.S. 228 (2013) — Supreme Court of the United States, June 20, 2013.** Justice Scalia wrote for Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Kennedy, Thomas, and Alito. Thomas also concurred separately. Justice Kagan dissented with Justices Ginsburg and Breyer; Justice Sotomayor did not participate. Civil Procedure: individual arbitration, class waivers, federal statutory claims, and effective vindication. Assigned accounts: CivPro physical PDF 460, printed 893–894, and physical PDF 705, printed 1401–1402.

## Facts and the merchants' objection to arbitration

The merchants accepted American Express cards under agreements requiring arbitration of disputes and prohibiting class arbitration. They alleged that American Express used power in the charge-card market to force acceptance of its credit cards at rates about 30% higher than competing credit-card fees. A charge card required payment of the full billing-cycle balance; a credit card allowed part of the balance to remain subject to interest. The merchants described the arrangement as unlawful tying under §1 of the Sherman Act and sought treble damages under §4 of the Clayton Act. This procedural ruling did not establish monopoly power or an antitrust violation. **231 & n.1.**

American Express moved to compel individual arbitration. The merchants supplied an economist's declaration estimating that the analysis needed to prove their antitrust claims would cost several hundred thousand dollars and possibly more than $1 million. The maximum recovery for an individual plaintiff was reported as $12,850, or $38,549 when trebled. Their objection was that spending vastly more than the available recovery made individual pursuit economically irrational. They argued that enforcing the agreement would prevent effective vindication of their federal antitrust rights. **231, 235.**

## Procedural history and precise issue

The district court compelled arbitration and dismissed the suits. The Second Circuit reversed because the merchants had shown prohibitive individual-arbitration costs. The Supreme Court then vacated and remanded in light of *Stolt-Nielsen*, which prohibited compelling class arbitration without an agreement to it. The circuit adhered to its decision, saying it had not ordered class arbitration, and later reconsidered after *Concepcion*. It again invalidated the waiver, distinguishing that state-preemption decision from the federal effective-vindication question. The Supreme Court reviewed the resulting 2012 appellate judgment. **231–232.**

The question was whether courts could invalidate this contractual waiver of class arbitration because proving an individual federal statutory claim would cost more than the likely recovery. The majority answered no. This was not a California court's determination that the agreement was unconscionable under state law. **231–239.**

## The contractual rule and the federal antitrust argument

The FAA made covered arbitration agreements enforceable subject to grounds for revoking a contract. The Court emphasized enforcing agreed terms, including whom parties would arbitrate with and the applicable procedures. That principle extended to federal statutory claims unless another congressional command displaced it. **232–233.**

The antitrust statutes did not supply such a command. Treble damages helped facilitate enforcement, but the existence of that measure did not establish that Congress intended every procedural departure that might advance enforcement. The Sherman and Clayton Acts did not guarantee affordable pursuit of every claim or prohibit waiving class procedure. They predated Rule 23. Congressional approval of that rule also did not create an entitlement to a class proceeding whenever one was financially necessary: certification itself had demanding prerequisites, and *Eisen* had refused to excuse notice costs merely because those costs frustrated a plaintiff's antitrust suit. **233–235.**

## Effective vindication: the majority's distinction

The merchants also invoked a judge-made principle against arbitration terms prospectively waiving federal statutory remedies. The Court described the relevant *Mitsubishi* discussion as dictum, noting that the cited cases had recognized the principle without using it to invalidate the particular agreement. It nevertheless explained the difference between eliminating the ability to assert a statutory right and making proof uneconomic. **235–236 & n.2.**

A clause forbidding assertion of specified statutory rights would fall within the stated concern. Filing or administrative fees so high as to make access impracticable might also qualify. But the expense of establishing a claim, even when greater than the potential recovery, did not by itself eliminate the right to pursue its remedy. The class waiver restricted the proceeding to the contracting parties; the majority did not regard loss of aggregation as equivalent to a prospective substantive-right waiver. **236–237.**

Those examples were carefully qualified. The Court did not decide whether Kagan's hypothetical prohibition on economic testimony would constitute an invalid waiver. Its response distinguished a clause potentially making proof impossible even in an individual case from loss of a class mechanism. Likewise, its statement about excessive forum fees was framed as a possible application, not an adjudicated fee barrier in this contract. **236 n.3.**

The majority treated *Gilmer* and *Vimar Seguros* as reinforcing the distinction between statutory rights and procedural expense. It also found *Concepcion* strongly supportive: class arbitration altered the informality, cost, and complexity of bilateral arbitration, and low-value claims did not justify refusing to enforce the parties' agreement. Requiring pre-arbitration litigation of every theory's evidentiary needs, proof costs, and expected recovery would undermine the streamlined resolution arbitration was meant to supply. **237–239 & n.5.**

## Thomas's additional reason

Thomas joined the Court fully but supplied an independent textual explanation. He read the FAA's contract-revocation exception as addressing defects in making the agreement, such as fraud or duress. The merchants' antitrust-policy and effective-vindication arguments did not concern formation. Because they had voluntarily made a bilateral-arbitration contract and supplied no qualifying formation challenge, economic infeasibility of their desired claim did not release them from it. This formation-focused explanation was his concurrence, not a separately stated holding adopted by all five Justices. **239.**

## Kagan's dissent: evaluate the agreement's combined operation

Kagan rejected the majority's distinction between a formal right to assert a claim and a contract making actual pursuit prohibitive. For her, effective vindication prevented indirect as well as express prospective waivers. Excessive filing fees, an impossibly short limitations period, exclusion of necessary proof, meaningless remedies, or a biased arbitrator could all defeat statutory enforcement without saying that antitrust claims were forbidden. Those were illustrations, not findings about each provision in this agreement. **240–244.**

She also read *Mitsubishi* and *Randolph* differently. Arbitration of statutory claims had been accepted on the condition that meaningful enforcement remained possible. In *Randolph* the cost objection failed for inadequate evidence, rather than because prohibitive expense was irrelevant. Here she regarded the economist's evidence as concrete and essentially unchallenged. Her note 1 acknowledged that tailored arbitral proof might theoretically cost less, but explained why that possibility could not replace the factual premise on which the case reached the Court. **242–245 & n.1; 247 n.3.**

Her central objection concerned the contract as a whole, rather than a necessary entitlement to class arbitration. She described prohibitions on joinder and consolidation, confidentiality preventing a shared expert report, no shifting of costs to American Express, and refusal of stipulations that could reduce the economic-proof burden. Together, she believed, those measures foreclosed every way of sharing, shifting, or shrinking costs. A class waiver paired with an adequate alternative might not offend effective vindication. **246, 249–250 & nn.2, 5.**

The majority disputed that broader premise and the proper scope of review. Its note 4 said American Express denied the asserted other cost-sharing bars and stressed that the appellate judgment under review relied on class treatment as the only economically feasible path. Kagan relied on analysis in an earlier vacated appellate opinion that, she said, continued to inform the final judgment. The brief therefore attributes the broader contractual-bar account to the dissent rather than treating every asserted barrier as an undisputed majority finding. **237 n.4; 249–250 n.5.**

Finally, Kagan distinguished *Concepcion*: that case concerned preemption of a state rule protecting class procedures, whereas this one required reconciling two federal statutes and preserving some means of enforcing antitrust rights. The effective-vindication inquiry did not promise convenient or optimal arbitration; it demanded concrete proof of practical foreclosure. She would decline to compel arbitration on this showing. **244–248 & n.4; 251–253.**

## Judgment and textbook correction

The Court **reversed the Second Circuit's judgment**. The official mandate does not expressly add a remand. The result enforced the class waiver against the particular proof-cost objection; it did not adjudicate the alleged tying arrangement. Five Justices supported the Court's opinion, three dissented, and Sotomayor did not participate. **239; 229.**

The first assigned account's reference to a California unconscionability ruling is inaccurate. This decision reviewed the Second Circuit's refusal to enforce individual arbitration of federal antitrust claims on effective-vindication grounds. Its accompanying statement that proof costs exceeded potential recovery identifies the actual issue correctly, as does the repeated account on printed 1401–1402. The full opinion adds the qualified surviving examples, the scope dispute about other contract terms, and both separate writings.
