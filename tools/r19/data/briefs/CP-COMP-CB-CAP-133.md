# Osborn v. Bank of the United States

**22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 738 (1824). Supreme Court of the United States, March 19, 1824. Civil Procedure.** Assigned: CivPro.pdf, physical PDF pages 187–188, printed pages 329–330, the own-caption account and Marshall quotation before Notes and Questions.

## The tax, seizure, and suit

Ohio's February 8, 1819 statute imposed an annual $50,000 tax on each office of discount and deposit operated by a bank without the State's permission. It expressly targeted the Bank of the United States and directed the state auditor to arrange collection, including entry into banking rooms and vaults and seizure of money or goods. The Bank sued in the federal Circuit Court of Ohio to prevent auditor Ralph Osborn from enforcing the statute. An injunction was allowed on September 14, 1819; the subpoena bearing the order and related endorsements was served on Osborn the next morning. The subsequently issued writ was served on Osborn and collector John L. Harper on September 18. The report therefore distinguishes notice of the allowed injunction from service of the formal writ. (739–741.)

The Bank's amended bill alleged that Harper, acting for Osborn and knowing an injunction had been allowed, forcibly took $100,000 in coin and bank notes from its Chillicothe office on September 17. It added the former and successor state treasurers and sought discovery, return of the money, and protection against its use or further collections. Former treasurer Currie acknowledged receiving $98,000 as the tax proceeds. Although credited to state revenue, that money remained separate from other treasury funds. Successor Samuel Sullivan received it, understood its reported source, and kept it untouched out of respect for the injunction. Sullivan disclaimed firsthand knowledge of the seizure and claimed to hold the funds only as state treasurer. The initial statement spells the former treasurer's name Curry; Marshall's analysis uses Currie. (741–743, 833–836.)

The Circuit Court heard the cause on the treasurers' answers and the decrees against Osborn and Harper, whose allegations were taken as confessed. It ordered restoration of $100,000, with interest on $19,830 of coin held by Sullivan. The officials appealed. Their objections extended beyond subject-matter jurisdiction: counsel's authority to prosecute the suit, proof identifying Sullivan's funds, responsibility of Osborn and Harper, interest, equity jurisdiction, Ohio's immunity and absence as a party, and the validity of the tax. (743–745, 828–829.)

## Jurisdiction under the charter and Article III

The Court upheld federal jurisdiction on two separate grounds. First, Congress actually granted it. The Bank's charter expressly authorized the Bank to sue and be sued in state courts having competent jurisdiction and in any federal Circuit Court. Marshall distinguished the earlier charter construed in Bank of the United States v. Deveaux, whose general reference to courts of record or other places gave corporate capacity to sue without expressly identifying the federal courts. The present charter's specific federal-court language supplied the statutory jurisdictional grant. (817–818.)

Second, that grant was constitutionally permissible. Article III extends the judicial power to cases arising under federal law, not merely isolated federal questions. Nearly any federal-law case can also involve ordinary factual disputes, payment, release, limitations, or other nonfederal rules. Treating those issues as disqualifying would prevent the national courts from resolving whole cases. Congress could give inferior federal courts original jurisdiction over the relevant Article III class; the Constitution did not require every such case to start in state court merely because Supreme Court review might ultimately be available. (818–823.)

Marshall's central formulation was that a question within the federal judicial power could be an **ingredient of the original cause**, allowing Congress to confer jurisdiction over the cause even though it contained other legal or factual questions. The Bank furnished a particularly strong example: its federal charter created the entity and defined its capacities to acquire rights, transact business, make contracts, and sue. A suit on an ordinary contract still rested on the federally granted capacity to make that contract and seek enforcement. That ingredient existed whether the defendant actually contested it or chose to litigate only performance or payment. A previously settled issue did not retroactively make the original jurisdictional grant unconstitutional. (823–826.)

Marshall addressed the objection that this reasoning would permit federal jurisdiction over every suit involving a naturalized citizen. Naturalization makes a person a citizen; it does not prescribe all that person's capacities and transactions as the Bank's charter did. The individual thereafter stands generally on the same footing as a native citizen. The Court regarded this as a material distinction rather than treating every remotely federal source of a person's existence as sufficient. (827–828.)

The assigned quotation is Marshall's discussion of a hypothetical Bank contract, at 823–825. It explains his broader jurisdictional reasoning; the actual Ohio controversy concerned a tax and seizure, not a contract default. This is a decision about Article III's permissible reach and an express charter grant. The brief does not present the ingredient formulation as a verified current interpretation of every claim under today's general federal-question statute.

## Why the decree against the officials was permissible

**Representation and proof.** The absence of a corporate warrant of attorney from the record did not require reversal. Licensed counsel's appearance had traditionally served as evidence of authority for corporations as well as individuals. Any change to that established practice should operate prospectively; the Court also treated the record defect as formal. (829–831.) Although ordinarily one defendant's answer cannot be used against another, Currie and Sullivan were successive holders of the same specifically segregated funds. The Court explained why succession and privity supported considering Currie's admissions. It independently found Sullivan's own answer sufficient: he received and retained the money as the Bank's alleged tax proceeds with knowledge of the injunction. This was not simply an unidentified $98,000 in general public revenue. (831–836.)

**Personal responsibility and equitable relief.** Assuming the Ohio act invalid, official position supplied no justification for the seizure. Osborn's procurement of Harper's illegal act and Harper's execution of it made them jointly responsible. Equity was appropriate because the threatened annual exaction would destroy the Bank's exercise of its franchise in Ohio, rather than inflict a single casual trespass adequately repaired by damages. Repeated damages actions would leave the Bank exposed to continuing destruction of its privileges. (836–844.)

The State's immunity also made ordinary damages against its collection agents potentially ineffective: transfer of the funds to an immune principal could leave only a nominal remedy against agents unable to repay so large a sum. The amended bill furnished a further basis for relief even if the original injunction were assumed improper. Identifiable money and negotiable notes remained separately held; equity could prevent their circulation or transfer, which would destroy practical recovery of the specific property. Discovery and preservation of the deposit supported restitution. The authority note at 845 cites the comparable equitable protection of negotiable securities. (844–846, 868–870.)

**The State's interest and the Eleventh Amendment.** Ohio had a direct interest in the controversy, but was not a party on the record and could not be joined against its immunity. Marshall reasoned from the Constitution's distinction between a state's interest in litigation and its status as a party. For jurisdiction depending on party identity, he treated the parties named on the record as controlling. He accordingly confined the Eleventh Amendment limitation, in this opinion, to suits naming a State as a party. The officials' responsibility could still be adjudicated; immunity did not pass from the sovereign to agents executing an invalid law. The Court recognized the difficulty of protecting Ohio's interests through those officials, but found that consequence inherent in the State's exemption from suit. (846–859, 870.) This describes the historical opinion's reasoning, rather than certifying that its categorical party-on-the-record language states all modern sovereign-immunity law.

**The tax was invalid.** The Court reaffirmed McCulloch. Congress created the Bank as a national fiscal instrument, not merely a private commercial venture accidentally hired by government. Its lending and banking operations enabled it to perform those public functions; destroying the operations would destroy the useful instrument. The distinction between preserving corporate existence and permitting a State to tax away its essential business therefore failed. The absence of an express statutory tax exemption was not decisive: protection could follow from the law establishing the federal instrument. Marshall distinguished taxation of local property from a state's control of the protected operations. Ohio's act was repugnant to federal law enacted pursuant to the Constitution and could not authorize the officials' conduct. (859–868.)

## Johnson's dissent

Justice Johnson dissented on jurisdiction and expressly declined to decide the remaining issues. He accepted the Bank's practical importance and the desirability of protecting national fiscal operations, but thought policy could not enlarge the constitutional categories. In his statutory reading, the charter's incorporation clause enumerated the acts the artificial person could perform, rather than granting unrestricted access to federal courts. He considered the context, comparable corporate powers, and the clause's reference to both state and federal courts inconsistent with Marshall's broad jurisdictional reading. (871–884.)

His constitutional objection was to jurisdiction on a **possible**, rather than an actual, federal question. Stamp duties, federal land grants, and naturalization could otherwise become gateways to vast bodies of ordinary state litigation. Johnson did not reject deciding incidental nonfederal questions once a genuine federal case existed, or dispute Congress's power to create original jurisdiction in appropriate cases. He argued that the Court had misidentified the objection by treating it as resistance to those uncontroversial propositions. (874–875, 884–890.)

Johnson distinguished claims necessarily created by federal statute, such as patent actions, from ordinary trespass and contract claims that might later present a federal issue. In the former, the federal legal basis appeared at the outset; in the latter, the case should proceed until the pertinent federal issue actually arose, with the statutory appellate process providing protection. Corporate creation was not, for him, the proximate source of every subsequent obligation. He also rejected manufacturing a new privileged litigating person as a means of evading Article III's restrictions on party-based jurisdiction. (888–903.)

## Exact judgment and source scope

The Court **affirmed restitution of the segregated $98,000 held by Sullivan and payment of the remaining $2,000 by Osborn and Harper**, but **reversed the interest component**. Sullivan had obeyed the court's prohibition on using the money; charging interest while the court immobilized the deposit would treat its instrument unfairly. The report's closing mandate summarizes the decree as affirmed except for interest on the specie held by Sullivan. (837–838, 871, 903.) Marshall delivered the Court's opinion; Johnson supplied the sole published separate writing. The report does not give a numerical roll call, so none is invented here.
