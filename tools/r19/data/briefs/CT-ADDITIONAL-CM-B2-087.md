# State ex rel. Youngman v. Calhoun
231 S.W. 647 (Mo. App. 1921) | Contracts

## Court, proceeding, and result
The St. Louis Court of Appeals decided this original prohibition proceeding on May 3, 1921; the reporter notes denial of rehearing on June 7. Judge Becker wrote the opinion, and Presiding Judge Allen concurred. The complete reported judicial text contains no separate writing or footnote. The court made its temporary writ permanent in part: Judge Calhoun could enforce the injunction against establishing a medical office within the contracted five-mile area, but could not enforce its broader restraints on calls and treatment. This was a limitation on judicial enforcement of an injunction, rather than a declaration that the entire covenant was invalid. (647, 649.)

The relator was physician Jacob Youngman. He sought prohibition against John W. Calhoun, judge of the St. Louis Circuit Court, who had entered an injunction in a case brought by physician Louis Habig against Youngman and his wife. The respondent judge's return to a preliminary rule was taken, by counsel's consent, as a demurrer to the relator's application and the preliminary writ; the matter was submitted on a motion for judgment on the pleadings. The appellate court therefore confronted the contract and the scope of the injunction, rather than deciding a damages appeal after factual trial. (647–648.)

## The sale and the initial breach
Habig and Youngman were both physicians. Until spring 1919 Habig practiced in Illinois, while Youngman practiced in St. Louis and owned a home at 8517 Gravois Avenue, where he conducted the medical practice. Youngman and his wife sold that property to Habig. Habig moved in and took over the practice; a written agreement incorporated into the deed provided that the sellers would not establish themselves as a practicing physician and surgeon within five miles of the premises for five years from December 1, 1919. (648.)

Youngman briefly visited Florida and then returned to open an office at Grand and Lafayette Avenues in St. Louis, which lay within the prohibited area. Habig sued to prevent him from opening or maintaining a medical office and to bar practice or treatment of former patients or anyone else within the district. Before the prohibition application, Youngman admitted that the new office violated the agreement. He closed it and tendered the fees he had earned there and the litigation costs through the date of the decree. That concession concerned the prohibited office; it did not concede that every additional restriction in the injunction was authorized. (648–649.)

By the time this proceeding was heard, Youngman had opened another office at Sappington, more than five miles from the former residence. Nevertheless, the permanent injunction purported to stop him from making calls within the original district, treating district residents at Sappington, and treating any former patients or other district residents wherever they might come to him. He sought a writ preventing enforcement to that extent. (648–649.)

## Contract meaning and evidence
The appellate court first rejected the suggestion that the phrase about establishing oneself as a practicing physician was ambiguous. Counsel for the purchaser particularly denied ambiguity, and the court agreed. The language used ordinary, unambiguous words in their contractual context. Consequently, extrinsic evidence of the parties' intentions was unnecessary and inadmissible in a trial on the merits. The court did not decide meaning by resolving competing witness testimony about negotiation. (648.)

The covenant restrained trade and personal liberty and therefore could not be extended beyond its fair import. In the court's reading, the sellers promised that Youngman would not maintain an office for medical practice within the specified district. That protected Habig against a nearby competing professional establishment for the defined period. It did not promise that Youngman would stop calling on people in the area or refuse to treat people from that area when they visited an outside office. After Youngman ceased the prohibited office and moved beyond five miles, the retained freedom to make calls and accept patients remained material. (649.)

The court grounded this distinction in both the chosen words and the limited restriction the parties made. It described the purchaser as satisfied with an agreement preventing an office in the five-mile district for a fixed period. It would not read that promise as if it barred the seller from practicing medicine at any point within five miles, calling on anyone who lived there, or treating former patients elsewhere. Thus strict construction preserved the bargain's office restriction while preventing expansion into a substantially broader restraint. (649.)

## Why prohibition was available
The distinction between ambiguity and clear contractual meaning also determined the procedural remedy. If the injunction's alleged excess had depended on a factual dispute, ordinary remedies such as appeal or error could have been adequate. Prohibition was available where the facts demonstrated an excess of jurisdiction apparent as a matter of law and no adequate legal remedy existed. The court applied that principle to the unambiguous covenant and the injunction's demonstrably broader terms. It did not turn every mistaken contract interpretation into a jurisdictional defect. (648–649.)

The court allowed enforcement only insofar as the injunction barred opening or establishing an office for medicine or surgery within five miles of 8517 Gravois Avenue for five years from December 1, 1919. It permanently prohibited enforcement beyond that restriction, subject to further court order. The purchaser retained the contracted protection against a competing local office; the seller retained the freedom to make local calls and serve area patients from his outside establishment. (649.)
