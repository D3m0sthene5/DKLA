# Renner v. Kehl
150 Ariz. 94, 722 P.2d 262 (Ariz. June 25, 1986) | Contracts

## Facts and chronology
The Kehls and Moyles acquired state agricultural-development leases covering 2,262 acres of unimproved desert near Yuma. They offered their interests for sale rather than developing the property. Roy Renner, Lawrence Colton, and Kofa Farms wanted land for large-scale commercial jojoba cultivation. Their agent knew that business, and the buyers made adequate water a condition of their interest. Both sides believed the land had suitable soil and climate and enough accessible groundwater to support the venture. On June 5, 1981, the buyers agreed to pay $222,200 for the leases, paid $80,200 down, and undertook annual installments for the remainder. (150 Ariz. 96.)

Beginning that November, the buyers developed the tract and drilled five test wells. None produced water of sufficient quantity or quality for commercial jojoba cultivation. After spending roughly $229,000, they concluded the aquifer was inadequate, abandoned the project, and sued to rescind. The sellers counterclaimed for the unpaid contract balance. The mistake concerned an existing underground water supply, rather than a later reduction in profits or change in market conditions.

## Procedure and parties’ arguments
After an October 1983 bench trial, the trial court entered findings and an order in January 1984 allowing rescission for mutual mistake and failure of consideration. It ordered reassignment of the leases and awarded the buyers $309,849.84, representing the down payment and development expenditures, together with costs and attorney’s fees. The Court of Appeals affirmed by memorandum decision on December 17, 1985. On review, the sellers disputed both the entitlement to rescind and the award of consequential damages. (Id. 96–97.)

The sellers also challenged the evidence supporting the findings but supplied no trial record. Footnote 1 places the responsibility for a complete appellate record on the appellant and requires the reviewing court, in its absence, to presume substantial evidentiary support and proper exercise of discretion. The Supreme Court’s acceptance of the mutual factual premise therefore rests on that procedural constraint as well as the findings described in the opinion.

## Questions, rules, and holdings
The shared belief in adequate water was a basic assumption material enough to upset the exchange, making the contract voidable under Restatement (Second) § 152 and Arizona’s mutual-mistake doctrine. Footnote 2 specifies that failure to investigate thoroughly does not itself defeat rescission when the risk of mistake was not allocated and the mistake concerns such a material basic assumption. (Id. 96–97 & nn. 1–2.)

The court nevertheless rejected reimbursement of development costs as consequential damages. In this fault-free mutual-mistake case, the buyers were entitled to restitution of benefits conferred, rather than recovery of all expenditures incurred in reliance. Their recovery was the down payment plus the enhancement in the returned property’s value attributable to their work, less the fair rental value of their occupancy. The trial court had to determine the amounts on remand. (Id. 97–99.)

## Reasoning and remedial accounting
Water was essential to the agreed commercial project. The trial court found there would have been no transaction unless both sides believed jojoba could be grown economically on the tract. The mistake thus went to the bargain’s basis, rather than an incidental preference. The court applied § 152 without converting the buyers’ unproductive drilling into proof that they had consciously assumed all uncertainty about groundwater. Its second footnote expressly identifies the absence of risk allocation as important.

The Court of Appeals relied on Fousel, which allowed incidental or consequential damages alongside rescission, reasoning that rescission and expense reimbursement were not a double recovery. The Supreme Court rejected that analogy. Fousel concerned fraud and breach, and the accompanying Jennings authority concerned a defrauded party’s restoration. Here the trial court expressly found no fraud or misrepresentation by the sellers or their agents. Restoring all losses to the buyers on that reasoning would assign the whole risk of an innocent mutual mistake to the sellers. The opinion states its damages rule in terms of absence of proof of breach for fraud or misrepresentation; the brief preserves that historical formulation rather than expanding it to every rescission claim. (Id. 97–98.)

Restitution remained available under § 376 even though the sellers acted honestly. Its object was preventing unjust enrichment. The buyers had to return the acquired leasehold interests, with return properly conditioned on restitution, and account for their beneficial use of the property. Conversely, the sellers could not retain an increase in the returned property’s value created by the buyers’ development efforts without paying for that increase. Section 371(b) measures this side of the accounting by enhancement of the recipient’s property or interests, not by the developer’s spending. An unsuccessful or costly improvement therefore does not necessarily generate restitution equal to its cost. (Id. 98–99.)

Footnote 3 explains the court’s use of “consequential” or “incidental” damages for reliance expenses, including development costs here. The label should not obscure the substantive distinction between compensating the buyers’ expenditure and restoring the sellers’ enrichment.

## Disposition, vote, and assigned scope
Vice Chief Justice Gordon wrote for the en banc court; Chief Justice Holohan and Justices Hays, Cameron, and Feldman concurred, making the decision unanimous among the five listed members. There were no separate opinions. The Supreme Court affirmed the trial court in part and reversed in part, approved the appellate decision in part and vacated it in part, and remanded for proceedings consistent with its restitution formula. It upheld rescission but did not affirm the complete $309,849.84 award.
