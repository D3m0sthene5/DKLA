# Hochster v. De La Tour
(1853) 2 E. & B. 678, 118 Eng. Rep. 922, reproduced as [1853] EWHC QB J72 (Queen's Bench, June 25, 1853) | Contracts | Immediate action following anticipatory repudiation

## The promised tour was cancelled before it began

Albert Hochster was a courier. On April 12, 1852, Edgar Frederick De La Tour engaged him to accompany a European tour for three months beginning June 1, at £10 per month. Hochster agreed to undertake that work and be ready to start on the specified date. His declaration alleged that he remained ready and willing until De La Tour renounced the engagement. On May 11, De La Tour wrote that he had changed his mind and no longer required Hochster's services. He refused compensation. Hochster began this action on May 22, before the scheduled commencement of employment. Full report, declaration and trial account; assigned printed 897–898.

After commencing suit but before June 1, Hochster obtained another engagement with Lord Ashburton on equally good terms. That employment would begin July 4. Thus the substitute work reduced the prospective loss but did not eliminate the entire interval covered by the original engagement. The report supplies no amount of the verdict, and £10 per month is the agreed wage, not an identified award of damages. Full report, trial account; assigned printed 898.

De La Tour pleaded denials concerning formation, Hochster's readiness and willingness, and the alleged refusal and discharge. At trial before Erle J., his counsel objected that no breach could occur before June 1. Erle rejected that objection but reserved leave to enter a nonsuit on it. The remaining questions went to the jury, which found for Hochster. Counsel subsequently obtained a rule nisi to enter a nonsuit or arrest judgment. The report records argument on June 10 before Lord Campbell C.J., Coleridge, Erle, and Crompton JJ.; the reserved judgment was delivered June 25. Full report, pleas, trial account, argument heading, and note 1.

## Whether an injured party must wait and remain available

The question was whether a party who had agreed to future employment could sue when the employer unequivocally renounced the agreement before its starting date, without requiring the employee to remain ready to perform through that date. De La Tour argued that Hochster could accept termination and relinquish contractual remedies, or keep the contract alive and remain ready to perform until June 1. An announced intention not to perform, counsel maintained, was merely an offer to rescind that could still be withdrawn; physical or legal incapacity to perform was different. Full report, defendants' argument and opening of Lord Campbell's judgment; assigned printed 899.

Hochster's counsel argued that the pleaded readiness through renunciation had been proved and that decisions fixing damages at the scheduled delivery date did not necessarily prohibit an earlier action. The report also records judicial questions about possible contingencies and the meaning of rescission. Those interventions occurred during argument; the court's governing reasoning appears in Lord Campbell's subsequently delivered judgment. Full report, Hannen's argument, opposing argument, and “Cur. adv. vult.” boundary.

## Holding and judgment

Lord Campbell delivered the judgment of the court. An unequivocal renunciation of this executory engagement permitted Hochster to treat it as breached, consider himself released from future performance, and bring an immediate action for the resulting damages. He could also choose to await the performance date while treating the contract as prospectively binding. The decision did not require proof that performance had become physically impossible: an outright renunciation was enough in the circumstances presented. Full report, judgment passages beginning “The declaration in the present case” and “The man who wrongfully renounces”; assigned printed 900–901.

The court held the declaration sufficient and gave judgment for the plaintiff. It rejected the attack on the action's prematurity and preserved Hochster's success following the jury verdict. This was not a remand for a newly determined damages award, and the report does not state a numerical recovery. Its remark that a Court of Error could review the question was a statement about possible further review, not an account of a completed appeal. Full report, concluding paragraph and judgment; assigned printed 901.

## The promise already created a relationship

The court first rejected a universal rule that a breach can occur only when the promised act becomes due. Earlier decisions allowed immediate suit where a person promised to marry on a future date but married another person, contracted to execute a future lease but leased to another, or promised specified goods but sold and delivered them elsewhere. Those acts did not invariably make eventual performance impossible: a spouse might die, a lease might be surrendered, or goods might be reacquired. The examples therefore undermined the proposed absolute requirement of impossibility. Full report, judgment's marriage, lease, and specific-goods examples; assigned printed 899.

A contract for future performance also establishes a present relationship. The parties implicitly undertake not to act in the meantime inconsistently with it or to the other's prejudice. Traveller and courier were engaged to each other from hiring onward, although service had not started. An outright renunciation could breach that existing relation. The pleaded refusal was more than a passing intention that might be reconsidered. Full report, judgment passages beginning “Another reason may be” and “The declaration”; assigned printed 899–900.

## Release from further preparation reduces the loss

Requiring Hochster to remain idle and continue preparations would burden him while making the employer's eventual liability larger. Recognizing an immediate remedy allowed Hochster to seek another employer, reducing damages rather than needlessly accumulating them. It would be unfair for De La Tour to declare that he would never perform and then argue that Hochster forfeited relief by believing and acting on that declaration. Full report, judgment passages beginning “If the plaintiff has no remedy,” “But it is surely,” and “It seems strange”; assigned printed 900.

This reasoning links release from future performance, mitigation, and immediate action, but they remain distinct questions. The textbook asks whether Lord Campbell too quickly inferred that postponing suit until June would also require Hochster to remain available until then. That is an editorial criticism of a step in the argument. The judgment itself concluded that renunciation could be treated as breach and found no reason to postpone action once the corresponding condition of readiness was dispensed with. Full report, penultimate reasoning paragraph; assigned printed 901 and following note 1.

## Damages timing did not determine the availability of suit

De La Tour argued that damages could not yet be calculated. The court replied that similar uncertainty would exist in an action before September 1, when the employment would have ended. The jury could consider events that had occurred, and contingencies likely to occur, affecting the loss down to trial. Immediate suit did not mean ignoring substitute earnings or guaranteeing all promised wages regardless of intervening events. Full report, judgment's damages paragraph; assigned printed 901.

The court distinguished Leigh and Phillpotts as decisions about valuation at the time goods should have been delivered. A rule measuring a loss at a future performance date does not necessarily establish that an action cannot begin earlier. It declined to follow Parke B.'s dicta if they required continued, wasteful performance after outright renunciation. Ripley had not decided the precise question presented. Planche supported relief where a publisher ended a project before an author's work was complete, without requiring the author to finish useless work first. Full report, judgment's discussion of Leigh, Phillpotts, Ripley, and Planche; the assigned excerpt abbreviates this precedent analysis.
