# Washington Legal Clinic for the Homeless v. Barry
107 F.3d 32 (D.C. Cir. 1997) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Decision and scope of the appeal

On March 4, 1997, the D.C. Circuit reversed the District Court’s holding that District of Columbia law created a protected property interest in emergency family shelter and remanded for further proceedings. It affirmed a separate First Amendment ruling invalidating restrictions on when unsolicited advocates could visit the shelter-office waiting room. Judge Tatel wrote for himself and Judge Ginsburg; Judge Wald dissented from the property-interest and due-process ruling, but not the First Amendment result. The property issue was thus decided 2–1, while the advocate-access result drew no dissent. (32–33, 39.)

The case did not concern empty beds withheld while families completed paperwork, a judicial demand to build additional shelter, or a challenge to the total quantity of housing supplied. Shelters were operating at capacity. Nor was the adverse equal-protection judgment before the appellate court: plaintiffs had not appealed it. The due-process question concerned an asserted entitlement under the District’s existing statutory and administrative system. (35.)

## Statutes and changing allocation practices

A 1984 voter initiative guaranteed adequate overnight shelter, and a 1987 Family Shelter Act established a temporary program for eligible homeless families and required efforts to obtain available federal funding. Following costly litigation over administration, the Council amended both shelter statutes in 1990 to disclaim creation of an entitlement in any homeless person or family. The program nevertheless retained objective eligibility requirements: homelessness, payment or specified service where appropriate, and restrictions concerning recent use of shelter, taxes, and certain prior expulsions or public-housing evictions. Applicants also had to supply reasonably available documentation necessary to establish eligibility. (33–34.)

Statutes and regulations did not specify how to allocate scarce shelter among families who met those requirements. Initially the shelter office used a daily first-come, first-served system. Families camped outside, and those found eligible but not accommodated had to begin again the next morning. Some eligible families consequently never received shelter. In early 1994, the office adopted a waiting list. Families received a number and document checklist, called daily, and generally reached their numbers one or two months later. Roughly half dropped out during the wait. When a family’s number was reached, the office made the final eligibility determination; eligible families still on the list eventually received shelter. (34, 36–37.)

Denied applicants could seek internal review, a fair hearing, and judicial review. The majority acknowledged that the Clinic often secured favorable outcomes through those procedures. It distinguished the rules for establishing eligibility from the discretion to choose an allocation method among eligible families. The existence of review of mistaken eligibility decisions did not itself restrict the latter discretion. (34, 37–38.)

## Litigation and remedies below

The Clinic, staff members, a private shelter, and homeless mothers sued in 1993, alleging burdensome documentation, slow hearings, and violations of federal and District law and the Fifth Amendment. They also challenged the policy allowing unsolicited advocates into the waiting room only on Wednesday mornings and Tuesday and Friday afternoons, one advocate at a time. Advocates with existing clients were allowed whenever the office was open. (34.)

The District withdrew from the relevant federal emergency-assistance program shortly before a preliminary-injunction hearing. The District Court denied that request as moot, and plaintiffs added a declaratory claim that withdrawal violated the then-mandatory local requirement to seek federal participation. Although emergency legislation later made participation discretionary, the court declared that the Mayor had violated the previous requirement between July 1, 1993, and April 11, 1995. (34–35.)

The court also found the waiting room a nonpublic forum, sustained the one-unsolicited-advocate limit, and invalidated the three-period weekly schedule. On the shelter claims, it recognized a protected interest and, after a bench trial, found deficiencies in the definition of reasonably available documents and coordination among agency offices. It ordered quicker appeals as well. Only the District appealed the due-process and access rulings; legal conclusions and summary-judgment issues were reviewed de novo. (35.)

## Majority: eligibility does not necessarily create an entitlement

Under Roth, need or desire alone does not create property; the claimant must have a legitimate entitlement secured by an independent source such as state law. The court asked whether statutes or regulations imposed substantive limits on discretion, including mandatory directions that a specified outcome follow when relevant predicates were satisfied. Goldberg supplied the contrast: every person meeting the welfare criteria qualified for benefits. A benefit available only at officials’ unrestricted selection among otherwise eligible persons does not follow merely from satisfying eligibility criteria. (36.)

The District’s eligibility standards were objective. But the law did not mandate that every eligible family receive shelter or require the waiting-list method that currently produced that eventual outcome for families remaining on it. The office could return to its former daily system, prefer families with infants or disabled children, or select from a rolling eligibility pool; some eligible families could then never receive shelter while supplies remained limited. Supply had fallen from 495 family spaces in late 1994 to 139 in May 1995. The majority concluded that the combination of limited supply and legally unrestricted allocation prevented the asserted entitlement. Scarcity alone, without the discretion component, was not its entire rationale. (36–37.)

The court acknowledged that rules or understandings can sometimes create property interests, but rejected an administrative entitlement contradicting legislative intent. It doubted that a blanket disclaimer alone could erase an interest unequivocally conferred by the rest of a statute or regulation. Here, however, the disclaimer reinforced its interpretation of a program leaving allocation discretion intact. The majority also distinguished employment precedents concerning institutional understandings and reserved special circumstances, such as reliance creating contractual obligations, that had not been alleged here. It did not hold that every statutory disclaimer conclusively defeats due process regardless of the operative scheme. (37–38.)

## Advocate access and the unresolved funding claim

Accepting the unchallenged nonpublic-forum classification, the court required reasonable restrictions. The existing one-advocate limit already addressed overcrowding, and families’ vulnerability to solicitation did not vary with the three permitted time periods. The office’s actual nonenforcement also undermined the proffered concerns. Precedents permitting financial-solicitation bans in other forums did not justify an unreasonable restriction on offers of assistance here. The court affirmed invalidation of the weekly schedule without disturbing the one-at-a-time rule or deciding the general validity of all possible solicitation restrictions. (38–39.)

The District’s request to vacate the declaration about its past withdrawal from federal funding appeared only in a cursory concluding footnote and was omitted from its statement of issues. Plaintiffs responded comparably briefly, and the reply was silent. The court declined to resolve that inadequately presented issue. That refusal was not an appellate merits holding approving or condemning the withdrawal. (39.)

## Wald’s partial dissent

Wald considered eligible families’ interests contingent rather than nonexistent. Traditional property recognizes future interests even when the conditions for possession may never occur. For her, shortage of shelter made receipt uncertain but did not erase the legal interest; the government’s power to change or eliminate a program likewise did not negate all protection while it operated. She distinguished priority-setting from discretion to refuse an eligible family for whom an available unit existed under the current system. (39–42 & dissent nn.1–6.)

The detailed notice, hearing, evidentiary, and review rules supported her conclusion that the shelter office could not simply deny an eligible family at whim. She distinguished Tarpeh-Doe and the private-landlord discretion in Eidson and argued that Perry supported interests arising from settled institutional practices. At the minimum, an eligible family at the head of the existing waiting list when a unit became available had a protected claim to it. The mere possibility of changing the system later should not defeat that present conditional claim. (42–45 & dissent nn.7–10.)

Wald agreed that the statutory disclaimer did not itself dictate the constitutional conclusion. She read it as denying a right to force the District to fund enough shelter for everyone, not as permitting arbitrary administration of what shelter was supplied. She would affirm the rulings requiring proper documentation criteria and appeals. Her opinion demanded regular procedures for distributing available shelter, rather than shelter on demand or a judicial funding increase. (45–46 & dissent nn.11–12.)

## Final judgment and assigned qualification

The court reversed the property-interest ruling and remanded, affirmed the advocate-access ruling, and declined the cursory funding argument.
