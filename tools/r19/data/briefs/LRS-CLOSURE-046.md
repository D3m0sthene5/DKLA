# Schlesinger v. Reservists Committee to Stop the War
418 U.S. 208 (1974) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Facts, claims, and relief sought

The Reservists Committee was a national association of present and former Reserve members organized to oppose American military involvement in Vietnam through lawful advocacy. It and five members, all California residents, citizens, and taxpayers, challenged the Armed Forces Reserve membership of Members of Congress. Four individual plaintiffs were in the Ready Reserve; the fifth’s status was unspecified. Congressional reservists ordinarily could not occupy that same status under the applicable military directive. The Court’s contemporary figures distinguish active and inactive Standby members, retired reservists, and National Guard members rather than treating every congressional reservist as actively serving or receiving pay. (209–212 & nn. 1–2.)

The suit relied on the Incompatibility Clause, Article I, § 6, cl. 2, prohibiting a person holding an office under the United States from simultaneously serving in Congress. This is distinct from the neighboring Ineligibility Clause concerning appointment to a newly created or better-paid civil office during an elected term. The plaintiffs alleged that reservist status exposed legislators to executive influence and inconsistent obligations, depriving citizens of faithful legislative service. Presidential authority to call reservists to duty or discharge commissioned reservists supplied examples of the feared influence; actual distortion of a particular legislative act was not established. (209–212 & n. 3.)

They requested removal of congressional reservists from the military rolls, discharge when a reservist entered Congress, efforts to recover Reserve pay received while in Congress, an injunction against future placement on the rolls, and a declaration that Reserve membership was an incompatible office. They proposed to represent antiwar advocates, reservists outside Congress, all federal taxpayers, and all citizens. The requested order would run against the Defense and Service Secretaries, rather than directly expelling legislators from Congress. (211–213.)

## Proceedings and the issues actually reached

The District Court rejected standing as antiwar opponents, reservists, and taxpayers but accepted citizen standing. It considered the clause precise and protective against potential conflicts, stressed the vigor of the parties’ disagreement, and thought otherwise nobody could enforce it. It rejected a political-question objection and held a Reserve commission to be an incompatible office. Its partial summary judgment granted declaratory relief but denied the injunction and mandamus requests. The plaintiffs did not challenge those denials on appeal or by cross-petition. The D.C. Circuit affirmed on the district opinion, also citing Flast and Baker. (213–216 & nn. 4–6.)

The government’s petition presented standing, political-question, and constitutional-office issues. On June 25, 1974, Chief Justice Burger’s opinion for Stewart, White, Blackmun, Powell, and Rehnquist held that the plaintiffs had standing neither as citizens nor as taxpayers. The Court **reversed the appellate judgment and remanded to the District Court for proceedings consistent with its opinion**. The 6–3 decision did not determine whether Reserve membership violated the clause, whether the claim presented a political question, or the appropriate scope of declaratory relief. The reservist and antiwar standing theories were not pursued here. Stewart concurred separately; Douglas dissented with Marshall; Brennan and Marshall also filed separate dissents. (208–209, 214 n. 4, 215–216, 228.)

## Citizen standing: concrete injury before constitutional adjudication

Standing and the political-question doctrine impose distinct justiciability limitations. The Court considered standing first in this case without creating an invariant order of analysis for every case. A representative of the citizen class must possess the interest and injury of that class; here the common asserted injury was an undifferentiated interest in constitutional government. It was speculative whether Reserve membership impaired faithful legislative service. Alleged noncompliance with the clause alone therefore supplied an abstract injury, rather than an actual or threatened personal harm. (215–217 & nn. 5–7.)

The Court read Data Processing’s injury-in-fact requirement as part of Article III and stressed that recognition of more categories of injury did not abolish the need for the plaintiff to suffer one. Concrete injury gives a dispute specificity, equips the parties to explain adverse consequences from actual facts, and permits a remedy proportionate to those facts. These safeguards also prevent unnecessary constitutional adjudication and expansive injunctions confronting the political branches. Levitt’s rejection of a citizen-and-lawyer challenge to a Justice’s appointment furnished a close structural-constitutional analogue. (218–224 & nn. 8–13.)

Neither the clause’s supposed precision and self-executing character nor the plaintiffs’ earnestness and skillful advocacy substituted for the missing injury. The district court had relied on its preliminary interpretation of the constitutional provision to establish standing, putting merits reasoning before the necessary showing of personal harm. The fact that constitutional provisions ultimately benefit citizens cannot mean that every citizen may sue to enforce every provision. Likewise, inability to identify another plaintiff does not create standing; some governmental decisions remain controlled by political processes. (224–227 & nn. 14–16.)

The Court did not hold that a shared injury is always disqualifying. The deficiency was abstraction, not simply the number of affected people. Competitive economic harm, impaired enjoyment of a resource, and a concrete dilution of voting power supplied contrasting examples. Note 14 recognizes that Congress can create legal rights whose invasion supplies injury, while insisting that the plaintiff still allege a specific invasion suffered personally. Thus neither statutory interest nor an abstract entitlement to proper government automatically replaces the Article III inquiry. (223 & n. 13, 224 n. 14, 227 n. 16.)

## Taxpayer standing

Flast’s limited taxpayer exception requires a connection to an exercise of congressional taxing and spending power and a specific constitutional limitation on that power. This suit instead attacked executive conduct permitting legislators to retain Reserve status. The request to recover Reserve payments did not change the object of the suit: its premise was unlawful executive payment to persons supposedly ineligible for Reserve service, not invalidity of congressional pay statutes governing lawful reservists. The Court therefore agreed with the District Court’s rejection of taxpayer standing. (227–228 & n. 17.)

## Separate writings

Stewart’s concurrence distinguishes his Richardson dissent. Here no plaintiff alleged denial of the kind of affirmative constitutional duty to supply personally requested information asserted in Richardson. Nor did the suit challenge congressional taxing and spending. He emphasized that widespread direct injury can support standing; these plaintiffs alleged no direct, palpable injury. (228–229.)

Douglas, joined by Marshall, would affirm. He viewed the clause as a specific constitutional safeguard against executive influence, comparable for taxpayer purposes to the Establishment Clause limitation in Flast. Congress appropriated money for reservists, and military commissions were offices in his view. The citizens’ stake was preserving an operative constitutional separation of legislative and executive power, not necessarily suffering monetary loss. He saw their interest in effective antiwar persuasion as relevant and rejected the assumption that shared injury destroys standing. He distinguished Levitt’s retirement-emolument dispute as contingent, whereas the challenged congressional-military relationship was ongoing. His account treats these constitutional protections as judicially enforceable by the people whom they protect; it is not the Court’s construction of the clause’s merits. (229–235 & nn. 1–3.)

Brennan’s dissent also applies expressly to Richardson. He would accept good-faith injury-in-fact allegations and separate that inquiry from merits and reviewability. The Reservists’ taxpayer harm might be hard to prove, but neither impossibility nor bad faith was shown; the Secretary could seek summary judgment requiring proof. Because Brennan would find taxpayer standing sufficient, he did not decide their citizen-standing theory. He thought Flast’s specialized test should not be expanded to these different disputes. (235–238 & the applicability asterisk at 235.)

Marshall additionally identified a concrete First Amendment-related injury: these reservists were specifically engaged in persuading Congress to end the Vietnam War and alleged entitlement to have their advocacy considered without the military conflict of interest. Not everyone shared that political objective or engaged in those efforts. He thought it inconsistent to recognize injury to enjoyment of natural resources while refusing to hear this claimed interference with political expression. That is his characterization of the allegations, which the majority rejected as insufficiently concrete. (238–240.)
