# Cunningham v. Hamilton County, Ohio

**527 U.S. 198 (1999) — Supreme Court of the United States, June 14, 1999. Civil Procedure.** Justice Thomas delivered a unanimous opinion; Justice Kennedy also concurred separately. An attorney’s discovery-expense sanction under the historical Rule 37(a)(4) was not a final, immediately appealable decision under § 1291, even though she no longer represented a party.

## Facts and procedural history

Attorney Cunningham represented Darwin Lee Starcher in a civil-rights action arising from his son Casey’s suicide while confined at the Hamilton County Justice Center. The complaint alleged that defendants ignored their duty to care for Casey despite a known history of suicide attempts. Starcher later died, and Casey’s sister became administrator of Casey’s estate. These allegations supplied the underlying litigation; the Supreme Court did not decide liability for the suicide. 527 U.S. at 200 & n. 1.

A magistrate judge supervised discovery. After the deadline for responses to interrogatories and document requests expired, he ordered complete responses by July 12, 1996, and scheduled four depositions for July 25. Cunningham did not produce the requested documents, gave incomplete responses to some interrogatories, and objected to others. She also noticed one deposition for a different day and declined to withdraw that notice after reminders. Although the order conditioned depositions of individual defendants on complete discovery responses, she moved to compel their appearances. Id. at 200–201.

The magistrate judge granted sanctions, characterized the conduct as egregious, and directed payment of $1,494 to the county treasurer for costs and fees incurred by county counsel. He expressly did not conduct a contempt hearing or find Cunningham in contempt. Another $2,432 award for other defendants was later satisfied through settlement and was not part of this appeal. The district court affirmed the sanction and disqualified Cunningham as counsel because she was a material witness. Id. at 201 & n. 2.

While the underlying action remained pending, Cunningham appealed the sanctions order. The Sixth Circuit dismissed for lack of appellate jurisdiction, concluding that review was not completely separate from the merits and that termination of her representation did not make the order immediately appealable. The Supreme Court granted certiorari on the appealability question. It declined her separate request to review the circuit’s decision to apply its ruling to her rather than only prospectively. Id. at 202–203 & n. 3.

## Issue and holding

Is a Rule 37(a)(4) sanctions order against an attorney a final decision under § 1291, including when she has ceased representation? No. The sanction did not terminate the case and failed the requirements for collateral-order appeal. Continued participation was not a sound dividing line for appealability. Id. at 204–210.

The holding concerned the expense sanction presented, not a contempt judgment or every conceivable sanction under every rule. The Court resolved appellate timing rather than determining the sanction’s substantive correctness. Id. at 200, 207–208, 210–211.

## Finality and relationship to the merits

Section 1291’s ordinary rule defers appeal until a decision ends the merits litigation and leaves only execution. The rule respects trial-court management, limits harassment through repeated appeals, and promotes efficient administration. A small collateral-order category permits immediate review of conclusive decisions resolving important questions separate from the merits and effectively unreviewable after final judgment. Id. at 203–204.

The county conceded conclusiveness, so that condition was presumed satisfied. Separateness failed. Reviewing discovery sanctions may require examining the information’s relevance and importance or the completeness and truthfulness of responses. Here, the appellate court would have to assess Cunningham’s partial answers and objections in relation to what the case required. The inquiry overlapped the factual and legal substance of the underlying litigation. Although not every discovery sanction might involve identical overlap, the Court rejected case-specific jurisdictional classification of individual orders. Id. at 205–206.

Nonparty status alone did not establish finality. Footnote 4 points to witnesses subject to ordinary discovery orders who generally cannot appeal without contempt, and to decisions rejecting immediate appeal of attorney disqualification. Being someone other than a named plaintiff or defendant did not dispense with the final-judgment requirement. Id. at 204–205 n. 4.

## Later review, contempt, and termination of representation

Cunningham argued that because a nonparty generally cannot appeal the eventual judgment, postponement would make her sanction effectively unreviewable. She compared her position to a witness held in contempt for resisting discovery. The Court identified important differences. Lawyers undertake ethical obligations to their clients, and the resulting congruence of interests weighs against treating them like independent nonparty witnesses, even when the lawyer has a personal desire for vindication. Id. at 206–207.

The particular Rule 37(a) order also lacked the prospective coercive effect of civil contempt. It compensated expenses and addressed delaying or harassing discovery behavior, rather than forcing future production through confinement or mounting fines. Allowing immediate appeals of such sanctions could itself undermine the rule’s purpose, deter trial judges from imposing needed sanctions, and produce repeated delays. Footnote 5 explains the historical shift toward awarding expenses absent substantial justification. Id. at 207–209 & n. 5.

Ending representation did not change the result. A contrary rule would create uncertainty about when representation ended and when the appeal clock began, and could encourage strategic termination to manufacture an appeal and delay the case. The Court recognized that former counsel might have to monitor the litigation, but found those costs outweighed by the efficiencies of delayed review. Id. at 209.

The Court acknowledged possible hardship without expanding § 1291. Congress or authorized rulemaking could provide another appeal route. A trial court could also mitigate difficulty by postponing decisions about imposing, sizing, or collecting a sanction. These were possible safeguards, not relief the Supreme Court actually granted Cunningham. Id. at 209–210.

## Kennedy’s concurrence and judgment

Kennedy joined the Court but emphasized exceptional alternatives. Mandamus might address a refusal to stay enforcement producing exceptional hardship likely to cause injustice. A genuine contempt order could be appealable where there was no congruence of interests between the person sanctioned and a litigant. Those qualifications did not describe Cunningham’s order, which imposed sanctions and nothing more. Id. at 210–211.

The Supreme Court **affirmed the Sixth Circuit’s dismissal for lack of appellate jurisdiction**. It neither adjudicated the civil-rights claim nor approved the discovery sanction through a merits appeal. Id. at 210.
