# American Well Works Co. v. Layne & Bowler Co.

**Course:** Civil Procedure

## Citation

American Well Works Co. v. Layne & Bowler Co., 241 U.S. 257–260 (1916). Supreme Court of the United States. Argued May 5, 1916; decided May 22, 1916. Justice Holmes delivered the Court's opinion. Justice McKenna dissented in a reported statement. Assigned account: Civil Procedure casebook printed p. 339, repository PDF p. 193, note 3; the preceding Harms excerpt also discusses the formula.

## Parties and material facts

American Well Works alleged that it owned, manufactured, and sold a pump for which it held or sought a patent. It claimed the defendants falsely and maliciously told customers that the pump and its components infringed the defendants' patent, brought suits against users without probable cause, and threatened suits against anyone using the pump. According to the pleading, these statements and threats injured the manufacturer's business. It alleged $50,000 in actual damage and requested the same amount in punitive damages. These were allegations and demands, not findings of falsity, infringement, or loss. 241 U.S. at 258–259.

## Procedural history

The action began in state court and was removed to federal court. American Well Works moved to remand. Instead, the federal District Court dismissed, reasoning that the claim arose under the patent laws, that the state court lacked jurisdiction over it, and consequently that the court receiving the removed action also lacked jurisdiction. A proper certificate brought the jurisdictional issue directly to the Supreme Court. The unusual dismissal posture matters: the Court was not affirming a merits judgment on patent infringement. Id. at 258.

## Issue

Does a claim for injury to business from statements of patent infringement and threats of infringement litigation arise under the patent laws merely because a defense may require deciding patent validity or infringement? Id. at 258–260.

## Rule and holding

The suit arose under the state law creating the cause of action. The potential need to examine patents in a justification or defense did not turn that business-injury claim into a suit under the patent laws. Jurisdiction depended on the plaintiff's declaration, and the patent questions described here were not necessary elements of the plaintiff's affirmative case. Id. at 258–260.

## Judgment

The Supreme Court reversed the District Court's dismissal. It did not decide whether the statements were false, whether either pump infringed a valid patent, or whether American Well Works was entitled to damages. The state-law character of the claim defeated the basis for treating it as exclusively within federal patent jurisdiction. Id. at 260.

## Reasoning

Holmes identified the alleged injury as lost business resulting from language tending to persuade the public to stop dealing with the plaintiff. The means could be persuasion, threats, or falsehood. The plaintiff's case required a business capable of being damaged and actionable conduct producing that damage; whether its business rested on patents was immaterial to that inquiry. Id. at 259.

A competitor might justify persuasion through competition, good faith, or reasonable grounds. A statement of fact might be defended as true. Those justifications belonged to the defense. American Well Works did not have to prove the validity or infringement of the defendants' patent—or the validity of its own patent—as an element of the business-injury claim described in its pleading. A statement that its pump infringed could be true even if it had a patent of its own. Id.

The source of the alleged wrong therefore supplied the jurisdictional answer. State law determined whether conduct injuring the business was actionable and which justifications were available. Holmes illustrated the point through variations in state treatment of truth and malicious statements. If the state abolished such actions, the patent laws would not independently preserve this particular cause of action. The possible federal content of a defense did not create the affirmative claim. Id. at 259–260.

## Separate opinion

McKenna dissented because, in his view, the case involved a direct and substantial controversy under the patent laws. The report gives that ground in a single statement and provides no developed separate analysis. A longer rationale or individual majority roll call cannot be recovered from that statement. Id. at 260.

## Notes

The textbook uses the case to introduce Holmes' creation formula and reproduces the state-law/defense distinction. Its surrounding Harms discussion cautions that the formula is more useful for inclusion than exclusion. Preserve that distinction: this opinion resolves this state business-injury suit; it does not itself examine every later category of state-law claim containing an embedded federal question. Casebook pp. 337–340.

The complete judicial opinion has no judicial footnotes. The official PDF's opening page also contains the end of an unrelated decision before this caption; that material is excluded from this brief.
