# Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc.

**368 U.S. 464 (1962) · Supreme Court of the United States · February 19, 1962**  
**Civil Procedure · Summary judgment, circumstantial evidence, and antitrust intent**

## Facts and the alleged scheme

Lou Poller held the rights of Midwest Broadcasting Company, a dissolved corporation that had operated WCAN, a Milwaukee television station. WCAN broadcast on ultra high frequency, or UHF, and was affiliated with the CBS network. UHF broadcasting was relatively new: ordinary receivers initially received VHF signals and needed modification to receive UHF. Poller claimed that WCAN had become a successful operation worth $2 million. Its affiliation agreement nevertheless permitted CBS to cancel on six months’ notice. 465–467; 472; 481 n.8.

Poller alleged a coordinated plan among CBS, its television division and officers, management consultant Thad Holt, and Bartell Broadcasters, which owned competing UHF station WOKY. Holt obtained an option on WOKY while the Federal Communications Commission considered allowing networks to own additional UHF stations. CBS could take that option after the regulatory change, acquire WOKY, and terminate WCAN’s affiliation. According to Poller, losing network programming would leave WCAN unable to compete and force it to sell its recently improved facilities cheaply. CBS could then operate under WOKY’s license using WCAN’s superior equipment. 466–467 & n.3.

Poller alleged a $1.45 million loss to Midwest and an equipment exchange that required Midwest to acquire WOKY’s inferior facilities at excessive prices. WCAN continued operating for only ten days after CBS began its broadcasts on February 17, 1955. CBS subsequently abandoned UHF broadcasting in Milwaukee in 1959 and affiliated with a VHF station. Poller treated these events as evidence of a broader design to destroy UHF competition and protect CBS’s VHF interests. These were allegations and disputed inferences, rather than findings establishing an antitrust conspiracy. 466–467; 471–472.

## Procedure and the competing positions

Poller sued for treble damages under §4 of the Clayton Act, alleging restraint of trade and monopolization under §§1 and 2 of the Sherman Act. The district court granted summary judgment to the defendants. It reasoned that CBS could legally purchase WOKY with FCC approval and exercise its contractual right to cancel WCAN’s affiliation; Poller’s resulting economic injury therefore did not establish actionable wrongdoing. The D.C. Circuit affirmed, with Judge Washington dissenting. 465 n.1; 467.

CBS argued that no actionable conspiracy had been shown, that Holt was merely its agent, that Bartell had no improper purpose, and that a network could choose its broadcasting outlet. It also characterized the monopolization allegations as frivolous. Poller responded that acts which might be lawful independently became unlawful when combined into a scheme to suppress competition. He relied on the transaction’s circumstances, CBS’s market studies, the relationships among the participants, and the destruction of his business. 468–472.

The Supreme Court considered whether this record left a genuine dispute of material fact under then-Rule 56(c). It did not conduct the antitrust trial or decide that Poller had proved damages, the relevant market, or a Sherman Act violation. 467–468; 473–474.

## Holding and the majority’s reasoning

Justice Clark’s five-Justice majority reversed. The Court accepted that CBS’s cancellation and acquisition could be lawful when considered separately. But a contractual privilege did not authorize its use as part of an unlawful agreement or purpose to restrain trade, control a market, or monopolize. Accordingly, the defendants’ abstract entitlement to change affiliates did not dispose of Poller’s allegation that the entire course of conduct served a prohibited end. 468–469.

The majority found circumstances from which a trial could develop evidence of coordination. Holt had participated in CBS’s plans, had relationships with CBS executives, and obtained an option that contemplated CBS’s purchase. The arrangement also allowed him to retain the option if CBS did not purchase. The Court regarded a finding of independent participation as possible on this record. It therefore did not decide the defendants’ separate contention that a corporation could not conspire with its own division or subsidiary. 469–470 & n.4.

CBS’s internal studies and the broadcasting history also mattered. WCAN had operated profitably and had helped establish an audience whose receivers could receive UHF programming. CBS selected Milwaukee for its acquisition, yet its own station later sustained losses and eventually closed. The majority read parts of CBS’s market analysis as permitting an inference that its UHF investment was temporary and related to protecting its VHF position. The combination of WCAN’s initial success, the challenged purchase and disaffiliation, and CBS’s later withdrawal suggested more than an entirely fanciful accusation. The Court expressly acknowledged that the proof might ultimately fail or that a jury might accept the defendants’ account. 470–472 & nn.5–6.

This was the setting for the opinion’s caution about summary judgment in complex antitrust litigation. Motive and intent were central, much of the proof lay with the alleged conspirators, and credibility could matter. The Court required the record to be viewed favorably to Poller and concluded that affidavits should not replace a trial where the material dispute remained. The case establishes that caution in its concrete evidentiary setting; it does not hold that every antitrust plaintiff is entitled to trial merely by alleging an improper motive. 467–468; 473 & n.7.

CBS’s remaining defenses did not supply an alternative basis for affirmance. Continued television service from another station would not itself negate a restraint affecting competition. Nor could CBS again treat its contractual cancellation power as absolute despite the allegation of an unlawful scheme. Poller’s broader UHF-destruction claim remained factually disputed. As for §2, the Court found the record and relevant-market allegations insufficiently developed to decide the monopolization issues and left them for trial rather than announcing a monopoly finding. 473–474.

## Harlan’s four-Justice dissent

Justice Harlan, joined by Justices Frankfurter, Whittaker, and Stewart, would have affirmed. He agreed that trial would be required if there were a genuine factual dispute about a scheme to eliminate UHF competition. His disagreement concerned what the completed discovery actually permitted Poller to prove. The record included depositions of CBS president Frank Stanton, vice president Richard Salant, Holt, and Poller; Poller’s own affidavits; and answers to interrogatories. No one suggested that Poller had been denied access to a witness he wanted to examine. Harlan therefore rejected the implication that the case rested merely on four interested witnesses’ affidavits awaiting their first meaningful testing. 474–478 & nn.1–6.

For the dissent, allegations, disbelief of defense witnesses, and hope that courtroom cross-examination would produce a different story did not constitute affirmative evidence of unlawful intent. Harlan invoked Dyer v. MacDougall in rejecting the proposition that the possibility of disbelieving the defendants necessarily blocked summary judgment. He saw no reason for a specially restrictive antitrust version of Rule 56: costly and potentially vexatious treble-damage litigation made the rule’s legitimate use particularly valuable. 478–480.

Harlan also disputed the majority’s particular inferences. Poller demanded $2 million for WCAN, whereas CBS bought the inferior WOKY station for $335,000. Poller initiated the later equipment-sale negotiations. CBS had prepared plans to improve WOKY independently, and the sale agreement expressly contemplated WCAN’s continued operation from the smaller facilities. Poller’s large investment despite the known six-month cancellation clause could explain his loss without a conspiracy. Harlan’s note 7 read CBS’s full market report as favoring a promising longer-term UHF investment, rather than the short-term destructive venture inferred by the majority. 479 n.7; 480–483 & nn.8–9.

Finally, the dissent considered the conduct shown legally insufficient. Changing an affiliate and vertically integrating into station ownership were not themselves unreasonable restraints. FCC approval did not immunize an antitrust violation, but agency experience supported the absence of any inherent illegality in network ownership. On monopolization, Harlan viewed Milwaukee’s UHF and VHF stations as competing for viewers and advertisers; CBS was not a monopolist in that combined market. He accordingly rejected both the §1 and §2 theories on the available record. 483–486.

## Judgment and assigned scope

The judgment was **5–4 to reverse the D.C. Circuit and remand for trial**. Clark wrote for Chief Justice Warren and Justices Black, Douglas, and Brennan; Harlan’s dissent had the four joins identified above. The result reopened Poller’s claims. It did not award treble damages, establish a conspiracy, or definitively resolve the relevant-market question. 474–486.
