# Gustafson v. Alloyd Co., Inc.
513 U.S. 561 (1995) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Court, vote, and assigned scope
The United States Supreme Court decided Gustafson on February 28, 1995. Justice Kennedy wrote the five-justice majority opinion, joined by Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, and Souter. Justice Thomas dissented, joined by Scalia, Ginsburg, and Breyer. Ginsburg also wrote a separate dissent joined by Breyer. The two dissents do not represent two additional sets of votes: Ginsburg and Breyer joined Thomas and also presented their supplementary argument. The assigned Class 5 excerpt is at PDF pages 2-10. The complete decision includes the majority's additional statutory and historical analysis and both complete dissents.

## Transaction and the buyers' requested remedy
Gustafson, McLean, and Butler owned Alloyd, a manufacturer of plastic packaging and heat-sealing equipment. They arranged a private sale of substantially all the company's already-issued stock. Wind Point Partners agreed to purchase through a newly created acquisition corporation, Alloyd Holdings, which later used the name Alloyd Co. The original company's stock had been issued in 1961; the challenged agreement was a 1989 negotiated resale, not a public distribution of a new issue. (513 U.S. at 564-565.)

The buyers conducted an extensive investigation, including review of KPMG's business analysis. Because the parties did not undertake an early inventory, they negotiated using estimates and provided for a later adjustment. The price included $18,709,000 for the stock and an additional $2,122,219 reflecting estimated growth in net worth since the preceding year-end. The sellers represented that the financial statements fairly presented the company's condition and that no material adverse change had occurred. The agreement required an adjustment if the year-end audit showed a difference between estimated and actual value. (Id. at 564-565.)

The audit showed lower-than-estimated earnings. Under the agreement, the buyers were entitled to an $815,000 adjustment. The sellers paid that amount plus interest. The buyers nevertheless sued for rescission under section 12(2) of the Securities Act of 1933. Rescission would undo the transaction, a materially different remedy from the bargained-for price adjustment. They alleged that the contractual representations contained material inaccuracies and that the agreement itself was a prospectus triggering the federal remedy. (Id. at 565-566.)

## Procedural history and assumptions on review
The Northern District of Illinois granted summary judgment to the sellers. It considered section 12(2) limited to initial stock offerings and regarded the buyers' access to corporate information and inspection opportunities as distinguishing their negotiated purchase from such an offering. The Seventh Circuit vacated and remanded in light of its intervening Pacific Dunlop decision, which read the statutory definition of prospectus broadly enough to include a stock-purchase agreement. The Supreme Court granted certiorari to resolve the conflict with the Third Circuit's narrower approach. (Id. at 566.)

For purposes of deciding the statutory issue, the Supreme Court assumed that the agreement contained material misstatements and that the sellers could not carry their burden of establishing due care. Those assumptions were not findings that the sellers had committed fraud. Conversely, the sellers' victory on coverage was not a finding that every representation was true. The appeal concerned the availability of this particular remedy for this kind of communication. (Id. at 567-568.)

## Statutory framework and issue
Section 12(2), using its numbering at the time, imposed liability on a seller who sold by means of a prospectus or oral communication containing a material misstatement or omission, subject to the seller's reasonable-care defense and other statutory conditions. The provision furnished a purchaser's express remedy without requiring proof of fraud or reliance in the manner urged by the sellers. The parties' dispute centered on prospectus; the opinion recorded the lower courts' agreement that the relevant oral-communication language concerned communications relating to a prospectus. (Id. at 567-568.)

Three provisions supplied the interpretive materials. Section 2(10) defined prospectus to include any prospectus, notice, circular, advertisement, letter, or written, radio, or television communication offering or confirming the sale of a security. Section 10 prescribed information a prospectus must contain, generally the information in a registration statement. Section 12(2) supplied liability for misstatements made through a prospectus. The definition's introductory language made statutory definitions applicable unless context otherwise required. (Id. at 568-575, 585-589.)

The buyers argued that a purchase agreement was a written communication offering or confirming a sale and therefore fit the explicit definition. The sellers argued that prospectus meant a document soliciting the public to buy securities from an issuer or controlling shareholder. The question was whether this privately negotiated contract qualified under the Act, not whether stock itself was a security.

## Holding and judgment
The contract and its representations were not a prospectus within the meaning of the 1933 Act because they described a private transaction and were not held out to the public. The Court construed prospectus as a term of art referring to a public offering by an issuer or controlling shareholder. It reversed the Seventh Circuit and remanded for proceedings consistent with that interpretation. (Id. at 584.)

The result removed the asserted section 12(2) rescission theory. It did not adjudicate every conceivable state-law contract or fraud claim or eliminate other federal securities remedies. Nor should the rule be compressed to a statement that only a company's very first sale of shares can ever involve a prospectus: the Court's formulation included qualifying public offerings by controlling shareholders and recognized securities exemptions from registration within its analysis.

## Majority reasoning: the same word within a coherent scheme
Kennedy began with section 10 rather than with the definition. Section 10 generally required a prospectus to contain registration-statement information. The parties agreed that this privately negotiated agreement need not contain such information and required no statutory exemption to escape that duty. The majority reasoned that it was therefore outside section 10's category. Crucially, the reasoning was not that a defective prospectus stops being a prospectus whenever required information is omitted. It distinguished a covered document that violates a duty from a document to which that duty never attaches. (Id. at 568-570.)

The majority then applied the presumption that identical words in different parts of one Act carry the same meaning. If the agreement was not a prospectus under section 10, it was not one under section 12. Kennedy described this as finding guidance in section 10, not converting it formally into a definition. The dissents thought that explanation still effectively let a narrower substantive provision control Congress's broader express definition. (Id. at 570-573.)

The Act's architecture supported the majority's inference. Registration and disclosure duties governed specified public offerings, and adjacent liability provisions enforced those duties. Kennedy considered it more natural to link section 12(2) to the disclosure system than to read it as creating extensive independent liability for private transactions. The exception for government-issued securities also seemed more intelligible as protection for government issuers within that public-offering framework. These were inferences about statutory structure, not an additional finding that the private sellers here were governmental entities. (Id. at 570-573.)

## Majority reasoning: associated words and redundancy
Turning to section 2(10), the majority rejected reading communication in isolation. It invoked the principle against needless redundancy: if communication included every written offer, the specifically named notices, circulars, advertisements, and letters would add little. It also invoked noscitur a sociis, understanding a word by the company it keeps. Kennedy treated the list as addressing widely disseminated public solicitations, including radio and television, rather than every private conversation or negotiated instrument. (Id. at 573-575.)

The ordinary securities usage of prospectus supplied an additional anchor. In that usage it was a document inviting the public to subscribe for securities. The statutory list prevented evasion by calling a functionally equivalent public solicitation a letter or advertisement. On this view, the catchall expanded possible forms of public solicitation without broadening coverage to every kind of securities transaction. The reference to a prospectus in the definition of prospectus was a partial circularity explained by that established technical meaning. (Id. at 575-576.)

The choice of category is therefore the contested step. Letters and communications can be private. The majority inferred a public-facing common feature from the list and larger scheme; the dissents disputed that inference and the decision to let it narrow an express definition.

## Majority reasoning: other provisions, consequences, and history
The Court distinguished United States v. Naftalin, which applied section 17(a)'s fraud prohibition beyond public offerings. Unlike section 12(2), section 17(a) lacked the limiting reference to prospectus, and the majority understood its history to support broader coverage. The existence of that broader provision prevented a simple inference that everything in the 1933 Act was limited to public offerings. The majority's argument was specific to the language it considered limiting here. (Id. at 576-578.)

Kennedy also considered the consequences of allowing rescission for casual secondary-market communications without fraud or reliance. Such liability could supply buyers with an option to undo transactions after disappointment even where a negligent omission did not cause it. The majority found no basis to attribute that broad remedial choice to Congress under the language and structure before it. Thomas accepted that the consequences might be troubling but considered their allocation Congress's responsibility. (Id. at 578, 594-595.)

The full majority opinion evaluates drafting materials and commentary omitted from the assigned selection. It discounts many post-enactment writings as evidence of the enacting Congress's intention and reads the House report as focusing civil liability on the information used to solicit the public. It also rejects Ginsburg's inference from omission of a public-offering phrase found in the British model statute, reasoning that the phrase could have been considered redundant given prospectus's established meaning. Those disputed historical inferences reinforce the majority's construction; they do not furnish new statutory language. (Id. at 578-584.)

## Thomas's dissent: the express definition should control
Thomas would begin with the provision creating the cause of action and then apply the enacted definition. That definition deliberately included communications offering or confirming a sale, without limiting them to public offerings. Ordinary or technical meanings yield to an explicit statutory definition. On his reading, prospectus was one item in a broad list, not a restrictive concept silently controlling every other item. (Id. at 584-588.)

Thomas disputed both canons. A catchall can intentionally make a list redundant as a safety net, and noscitur a sociis should resolve genuine obscurity rather than create ambiguity in expressly broad language. Requiring one uniform meaning also ignored the instruction that context could require a different application. Section 10's context could justify a narrower formal-document meaning while section 12(2) retained the general definition. Confirmation slips illustrated the problem with assuming that every defined prospectus must satisfy section 10's information requirements. (Id. at 586-589.)

Thomas illustrated the difference with a hypothetical Act defining car to include motorcycles, trucks, and trailers. A seatbelt requirement could use car in its narrower ordinary sense, while a sales-misrepresentation remedy could retain the broad enacted definition. Narrowing the latter merely because the seatbelt provision was narrow would defeat the definition. This analogy appears in the assigned excerpt and is a dissenting argument, not the Court's holding. (Id. at 589; assigned PDF page 9.)

He also relied on section 12(2)'s absence of the issuer, public-offering, or transaction-exemption language used elsewhere. For him, congressional silence cut toward breadth, whereas for the majority silence favored the common meaning across provisions. He rejected reliance on the Act's dominant public-offering focus and on avoidance of remedial overlap, citing Naftalin and the possibility that different securities provisions reached the same conduct. Finally, he charged that concern about excessive liability had displaced the legislature's chosen definition. (Id. at 589-595.)

## Ginsburg's separate dissent: context, drafting, and established understanding
Ginsburg, joined by Breyer, endorsed Thomas's textual criticism and added that the uniform-meaning presumption was flexible. She pointed to the Act's context clause and to later legislation expressly differentiating a section 10 prospectus from a prospectus under the general definition. Those examples supported distinguishing a formal disclosure document from the broader communications subject to liability. (Id. at 596-599.)

She further emphasized that the American drafters borrowed parts of the British definition while omitting its public-offering limitation. The conference report described the liability broadly, and prominent early commentators, subsequent scholarship, and decisions applying section 12(2) to private transactions supported that understanding. The majority considered much of this evidence unreliable or insufficiently directed to the exact coverage issue. Ginsburg thought the longstanding understanding cautioned against judicial revision. She acknowledged the majority's practical concerns but would leave any narrowing to Congress. (Id. at 599-604.)

The complete dissent also invokes the Uniform Securities Act's analogous liability provision and its application to secondary and private transactions. In her final footnote, Ginsburg acknowledges both the absence of a loss-causation requirement under the broader reading and its potentially anomalous exposure of private sellers: the section 2(10)(a) exception for certain communications following delivery of a formal section 10 prospectus could protect a public seller where a private seller lacked that protection. She treats those policy concerns as a reason for Congress to consider amendment, not as a basis to disregard the enacted definition. (Id. at 602–604 & nn. 6–8.)

## Study significance and sources
The case does not divide simply into judges using text and judges ignoring it. Both sides rely on enacted words, statutory structure, canons, and the relationship between definitions and context. The disagreement concerns which provision supplies the starting point, what presumptions do when an express definition is broad, and how much weight practical consequences and drafting history receive. An accurate account must retain the assumed inaccuracies, the contractual adjustment already paid, the distinct rescission request, and the reversal and remand.
