Parties
Arthrex, a medical-device company and patent owner, challenged a Patent Trial and Appeal Board decision
invalidating claims of its patent. Smith & Nephew and ArthroCare, treated together as Smith & Nephew in the
Court’s account, had been accused of infringement and sought inter partes review of the patent. The United
States intervened in the Federal Circuit to defend the statutory appointment system. Each side later sought
Supreme Court review of a different aspect of the appellate judgment. Roberts opinion, Part I-B.
The relevant officials were administrative patent judges, or APJs, appointed by the Secretary of Commerce,
and the Director of the Patent and Trademark Office, appointed by the President with Senate confirmation. The
dispute concerned whether the APJs’ authority matched their appointment to inferior offices and what judicial
relief would cure any mismatch. It did not ask the Supreme Court to decide whether Smith & Nephew
infringed or whether Arthrex’s invention was actually patentable. Id., Parts I–II.
Material Facts
The PTO administers the federal patent system within the Department of Commerce. Congress vested the
Office’s powers and duties in its Director and gave the Director policy-direction and management-supervision
responsibilities. The Patent Trial and Appeal Board included the Director, other specified PTO officials, and
more than 200 APJs. At least three Board members ordinarily sat on a panel. The Secretary appointed the APJs
as inferior officers; the President did not nominate each APJ for Senate confirmation. Id., Part I-A.
Inter partes review permits an adversarial reconsideration of an issued patent’s compliance with novelty and
nonobviousness requirements. The Director controlled whether to institute review, subject to statutory
conditions including a reasonable likelihood that the challenger would prevail on at least one claim. The
Director had delegated that institution decision to the Board. After institution, a designated panel conducted the
proceeding and ordinarily had 12 to 18 months to issue its final written decision. Id.
The Director also controlled regulations, prospective policy guidance, panel assignments, pay, and designation
of precedential decisions. But 35 U.S.C. § 6(c) reserved rehearings to the Board. No superior executive officer
could individually review and reverse a panel’s final decision under that arrangement. A party could appeal to
the Federal Circuit, an Article III court. Once appellate proceedings ended, the Director had a statutory duty to
issue the certificate implementing the panel’s determination; that ministerial act did not supply discretion to
reject it. Id., Parts I-A, II-B.

United States v. Arthrex, Inc.
Arthrex obtained its patent in 2015 for a device that reattached soft tissue to bone without tying a knot. After an
infringement dispute with Smith & Nephew, three APJs conducted inter partes review and found the
challenged claims anticipated by a prior patent application. The constitutional objection arose from the legal
authority of the officials issuing that decision, rather than an asserted absence of an adversarial hearing or
technical expertise. Id., Part I-B.
Procedural History
Arthrex first raised its Appointments Clause objection on appeal to the Federal Circuit. The Government
intervened. The Federal Circuit held that APJs were principal officers because neither the Secretary nor the
Director could review their decisions or remove them at will. Their appointment by the Secretary therefore
failed the constitutionally required method for principal officers. Id.
The Federal Circuit attempted a prospective cure by making the APJs’ statutory removal protections
unenforceable. It reasoned that at-will removal would render them inferior officers, vacated the Board’s
decision, and ordered a new hearing before a different panel. The Government, Smith & Nephew, and Arthrex
each unsuccessfully sought rehearing en banc and then petitioned for certiorari. The Supreme Court accepted
the constitutional objection to unreviewable APJ authority but chose a different remedy. Id., Parts I-B, III.
Five Justices joined the constitutional analysis: Roberts, Alito, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, and Barrett. Roberts’s
remedial Part III had the votes of Alito, Kavanaugh, and Barrett; Breyer, Sotomayor, and Kagan separately
agreed with its remedial holding despite their contrary merits position. Gorsuch would have granted different
relief, and Thomas dissented. Describing the entire opinion as either a five-Justice or seven-Justice majority
would conceal those differences. Opinion headings; Breyer opinion, Part II.
Issue
Was it consistent with appointment to an inferior office for APJs to issue final inter partes review decisions
binding the Executive Branch when no presidentially nominated and Senate-confirmed officer could review
those decisions? If not, should the Court invalidate the review system, remove APJ tenure protections, or
instead permit the Director to review panel decisions? What relief did Arthrex itself receive? Roberts opinion,
Parts II–III.
Rule and Holding
The Appointments Clause permits Congress to vest appointment of inferior officers in specified actors,
including department heads, while principal officers require presidential nomination and Senate confirmation.
Applying Edmond’s direction-and-supervision principle, the Court held that the unreviewable authority
exercised by APJs in these proceedings was incompatible with their appointment by the Secretary to inferior
offices. Administrative supervision alone did not cure their power to make final decisions binding the
Executive Branch without review by a principal officer. Id., Parts II-A–II-C.
The remedial holding made § 6(c) unenforceable insofar as it prevented the Director from independently
reviewing final Board decisions. The Director could review and issue a decision on behalf of the Board; the
rest of § 6(c) remained operative for other Board members. The Constitution did not require the Director
actually to review every case. The availability of discretionary review supplied the missing supervisory
authority. The decision addressed inter partes review and expressly did not formulate an exclusive
principal-versus-inferior test for every office or resolve supervision outside adjudication. Id., Parts II-C, III;
Breyer opinion, Part II.

Judgment
The Supreme Court vacated the Federal Circuit’s judgment and remanded for proceedings consistent with its
opinion. Arthrex was entitled to an opportunity for the Acting Director to decide whether to rehear Smith &
Nephew’s petition. It was not entitled to a fresh hearing before different APJs: their appointment permitted
initial adjudication, and the constitutional defect concerned the restriction on principal-officer review. The
Court neither dismissed the entire patent-review proceeding nor decided the patent’s validity. Roberts opinion,
Part III.
Reasoning
The Court treated the Appointments Clause as a mechanism for assigning responsibility for exercises of
executive authority. The nomination and confirmation requirements connect principal officers to the President
and Senate, while the inferior-officer exception accommodates the administrative inconvenience of using that
process for every subordinate. The exception nevertheless depends on genuine subordination. All parties
accepted that APJs were officers exercising significant statutory authority, so the dispute concerned their rank
and supervision rather than the employee/officer threshold. Id., Parts II-A–II-B.
Edmond provided the operative comparison. The military appellate judges there were subject both to
administrative oversight and to review by another executive tribunal composed of presidentially nominated,
Senate-confirmed officers. The significance of that combination was that they could not finally bind the United
States without permission from superior executive officers. The PTO supplied administrative control but
withheld the corresponding decisional supervision over APJ patent determinations. Id., Part II-B.
The Director’s powers over institution, policy, and assignments did not permit correction of a final
determination already issued. The statute’s command to implement that determination further showed why the
Director’s role at the certificate stage was insufficient: the Director was carrying out the panel’s decision, not
evaluating whether to accept it. Thus an official whom the President could supervise lacked authority over the
particular exercise of governmental power affecting the parties. Id.
The Government proposed several indirect mechanisms, including selecting sympathetic APJs, altering panel
composition, convening a rehearing panel containing the Director and two other officials, or withdrawing
institution before an unwelcome final decision. The majority considered those mechanisms inadequate
substitutes for transparent responsibility. Influencing a panel through personnel choices while lacking authority
openly to take ownership of the result obscured who was accountable. The Court did not approve panel
manipulation as the constitutionally required solution. Id.
Reassigning a judge after a decision likewise could not change the decision itself. APJs’ removal from federal
service was also protected by the requirement of cause promoting service efficiency. The majority considered
the combination of final decisional power and insulation from effective principal-officer control material. It did
not hold that the Secretary’s ability to dismiss at will could never cure the problem; the remedial discussion
expressly left that alternative’s sufficiency unresolved. Id., Parts II-B, III.
Federal Circuit review did not supply the missing Article II relationship. Article III courts review executive
action in a different constitutional capacity. The majority treated these patent adjudications as exercises of
executive power notwithstanding their judicial form; review by a court therefore could not substitute for
direction by a principal executive officer. This reasoning operated within the Court’s existing treatment of inter
partes review as an adjudication of public rights. Id., Part II-B.

The majority also examined proposed counterexamples. Some modern officials were subject to superior
executive review even when their decisions were final within a particular department. Other structures
presented distinct questions or lacked a sufficiently established history to validate this one. The Court did not
declare every cited agency arrangement unconstitutional. It rejected their use as conclusive precedents for the
particular unreviewable authority at issue. Id., Part II-C.
Historical patent arrangements also differed. Earlier systems often placed ultimate authority in the
presidentially appointed Commissioner or an executive reviewing tribunal. The ad hoc arbitrators and
examiners invoked by the Government performed special, temporary assignments, which raised a different
officeholding question from permanent APJs. The majority acknowledged that the history was varied rather
than deriving an unqualified rule from an uninterrupted identical institutional design. Id.
On remedy, Roberts reasoned that the statute already established a Director responsible for the PTO and for
supervising APJs. Removing the restriction that prevented that official from reviewing their decisions therefore
fit the surrounding structure. It allowed the inferior officers to perform initial adjudication while reserving final
responsibility to a principal officer. The Board’s ordinary panel requirements continued to operate; they could
no longer prevent the Director himself from reviewing a final decision. Id., Part III.
That approach preserved the inter partes review program, contrary to Arthrex’s request to invalidate it entirely,
and directly addressed decisional supervision rather than removing employment protections as the Federal
Circuit had done. It also determined the individual relief. Because the APJs could validly conduct the initial
proceeding, the needed correction was an opportunity for Director review, not repetition before a newly
composed panel. The Director remained free to decline rehearing. Id.
Separate Opinions
Justice Gorsuch joined the constitutional merits and disagreed with the remedy. He would have set aside the
Board’s decision in this case after identifying the constitutional violation, leaving Congress to choose among
competing institutional solutions. In his account, permitting new Director review was a legislative choice rather
than a necessary act of disregarding an unconstitutional application. Congress could instead alter the
appointment method, the allocation of adjudication, or the entire review mechanism. Gorsuch opinion,
remedial discussion.
Gorsuch disputed the premise that the Court could confidently reconstruct Congress’s preferred alternative.
The statutory design had combined patent cancellation with decisional independence, and removing the
independence requirement could materially change the bargain. He also reiterated his disagreement with the
earlier decision permitting executive reconsideration of issued patents, emphasizing the protection of private
property and impartial adjudication. That position was his own; Arthrex did not overrule the Court’s
public-rights precedent or require Article III adjudication of all patent-validity disputes. Id., opening and
severability discussions.
Justice Breyer, joined by Sotomayor and Kagan, disagreed with the constitutional merits and joined Parts I and
II of Thomas’s dissent. He read the constitutional authorization for Congress to allocate inferior-officer
appointments as leaving substantial room to design offices and their duties. The Director and Secretary
exercised considerable control, and Edmond required direction and supervision at some level, not necessarily
individual review of every category of decision. Breyer opinion, Part I.
Breyer advocated examining statutory purposes and consequences. Congress could reasonably seek technical
expertise and decisional independence in reconsidering patent validity while preserving policy control in
superior officers. He regarded those features as compatible rather than mutually exclusive. Once a majority

Justice Thomas, joined by Breyer, Sotomayor, and Kagan in Parts I and II, would have upheld the
appointments. APJs were lower in rank than both the Director and Secretary and were subject to substantial
actual direction: superior officers controlled policy, institution, panel composition, and assignments. Thomas
read Edmond as requiring formal inferiority plus meaningful supervision, without making final-review
architecture a single decisive criterion. The Court had repeatedly declined to announce an exclusive test.
Thomas opinion, Parts I–II.
Thomas further argued that the Constitution distinguishes legislative, executive, and judicial power, rather than
separate categories of principal-officer and inferior-officer power. He considered the majority’s account
insufficiently clear about whether the appointment itself was invalid or whether validly appointed subordinates
had been given impermissible duties. His historical discussion also disputed the majority’s treatment of older
patent arrangements and the degree of superior review. Id., Part II.
Writing only for himself in Part III, Thomas criticized the remedy from a different direction. An invalid
appointment would ordinarily call for a hearing before properly appointed officials; a valid appointment
coupled only with an unlawful restriction on Director review would require attention to whether Arthrex had
actually requested and been denied that review. He argued that the majority’s relief did not follow cleanly from
either account. In Part IV he examined several possible original understandings of inferior officers and
concluded that each supported the APJs’ status, without selecting one as a comprehensive future test. Id., Parts
III–IV.
Notes
The case requires separating three questions: whether a person is an officer, whether the officer is inferior, and
what relief follows from a constitutional defect in the arrangement. Officer status was conceded; the contested
issue was the compatibility of final decisional authority with inferior-officer appointment. The remedy changed
supervisory authority instead of replacing the appointing official or invalidating all initial panel work. Roberts
opinion, Parts II–III.
Appointments and removal interact here without collapsing into one inquiry. The Federal Circuit used removal
as its cure; the Supreme Court chose review authority and did not decide whether at-will removal would also
suffice. Similarly, a statement that APJs lacked a constitutionally adequate superior does not mean the Director
lacked all practical influence. The majority found that influence inadequate for the final adjudications, while
the dissent considered the total supervisory relationship sufficient. Id.; Thomas opinion, Part II.
The judgment should be recorded as vacatur and remand with an opportunity for discretionary Director review.
It did not guarantee reversal of the patent ruling or even rehearing. This brief describes the arrangement and
judicial remedy in 2021; subsequent implementation, later appointments, and the current scope of
administrative review require separate sources. Roberts opinion, Part III.
