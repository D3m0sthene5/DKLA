# Wiener v. United States
357 U.S. 349 (1958) | Legislation and the Regulatory State

## Court, posture, and judgment

On June 30, 1958, the Supreme Court reversed the divided Court of Claims’ dismissal of a former War Claims Commissioner’s back-pay suit. Justice Frankfurter delivered the Court’s opinion; no separate opinion or dissent appears. President Eisenhower could not remove this member of an adjudicatory commission merely to replace him with someone of the President’s own choosing. Neither the Constitution nor the statute’s silence supplied that power. The actual judgment was reversal, without specifying a monetary award. 357 U.S. at 349–351, 356.

## Commission, appointment, and removal

The War Claims Act of 1948 established a three-member commission to receive and adjudicate claims arising from enemy-inflicted personal injury or property damage during World War II. Its statutory categories concerned internees, prisoners of war, and religious organizations. At least two commissioners had to be members of the bar. The President appointed members with Senate advice and consent. Their tenure was defined by the Commission’s limited life: it was to finish its work within three years after the claims-filing period expired. Successive legislation extended that filing deadline to March 31, 1952. Congress did not expressly provide for removal. Id. at 349–350.

President Truman nominated Wiener, the Senate confirmed him on June 2, 1950, and he took office on June 8. Wiener refused a request to resign. On December 10, 1953, Eisenhower removed him, explaining that completing administration of the Act with personnel of his own selection served the national interest. The President made recess appointments the next day, including an appointment to Wiener’s position, and submitted the nominees to the Senate on February 15, 1954. Those nominations remained unconfirmed when a reorganization plan abolished the Commission on July 1, 1954. Id. at 350.

Wiener then sought salary from his removal through June 30, 1954, the last day of the Commission’s existence. An earlier quo warranto proceeding had been dismissed, and its appeal was dismissed as moot by agreement. In the salary litigation, the Government asserted that the earlier judgment barred relitigation of certain issues. The Supreme Court did not decide that contention’s merits: in the particular circumstances, the Government should have presented it under the Court’s rules as a reason not to review the case. The Court expressly withheld any endorsement of the estoppel claim. Id. at 351 & n. *.

## The question raised by statutory silence

The decisive dispute concerned what to infer from Congress’s failure to write a removal provision. Wiener’s Commission had neither the FTC’s express for-cause language nor an indefinite existence. The Government’s asserted power nevertheless depended on commissioners remaining subject to presidential pleasure, because Eisenhower identified no misconduct or other cause beyond his preference for a different membership. Id. at 352–354, 356.

The Court explained the relationship between Myers and Humphrey’s Executor in the historical terms those opinions used. Myers concerned removal of an executive postmaster, but its discussion extended to officials performing quasi-judicial duties. Humphrey’s Executor later confined Myers to purely executive officers and expressly rejected its broader statements supporting removal of members of quasi-judicial bodies. That distinction rested on function: an official who must exercise independent judgment cannot reliably maintain that independence while serving only at another official’s pleasure. Wiener therefore treated the nature of the assigned duties as the most reliable indicator of congressional intent, rather than treating silence as an automatic authorization of removal. Id. at 351–353.

Congress was familiar with the removal controversy and Humphrey’s Executor when it created the Commission. Its silence had to be read within that setting and alongside its affirmative choices concerning the Commission’s work. The absence of express protection did not erase those choices. Id. at 352–353.

## Why adjudication implied independence

The legislative history strengthened the functional inference. The House initially placed a relatively narrow class of claims against Japan under the Federal Security Administrator, an executive official, while using a commission to investigate other claims. The Senate replaced that arrangement with a commission empowered to adjudicate three classes of claims according to law. The resulting statute made its determinations final and unavailable for review by another official or a court, and provided payment from a War Claims Fund held by the Treasury. With minor amendment, that Senate design became law. Id. at 354–355.

Congress could distribute funds directly, use the Executive, or establish adjudication. Direct appropriations were impractical given the number and diversity of the claims. Congress chose an adjudicatory body, although it could have assigned those disputes to district courts or the Court of Claims. Calling the body a commission did not change the judicial character of its task: it had to decide each claim on evidence and governing law, free from coercive influence by either the Executive or Congress. Id. at 355–356.

The Court reasoned from freedom in individual decisions to security against replacement for political preference. If the President could not influence the Commission’s adjudication of a particular claim, Congress could not plausibly have intended him to hold over its members a threat of removal simply because he wanted his own appointees. Eisenhower’s reason closely matched Roosevelt’s unsuccessful attempt to remove Humphrey from the FTC. The adjudicatory character and statutory design, rather than an express removal clause, controlled the inference. Id. at 354–356.

## Limits and assigned scope

The Court carefully identified what was absent: removal for cause concerning a member’s rectitude, or a temporary suspension while the Senate considered a replacement. It resolved the asserted power to remove solely for the President’s preferred personnel. The holding should not become an absolute immunity from every kind of removal, nor an assertion that every agency Congress calls independent has identical functions. Id. at 356.
