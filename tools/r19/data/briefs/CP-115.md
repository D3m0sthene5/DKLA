# Denman v. Spain

**Civil Procedure · CP-115**  
242 Miss. 431, 135 So. 2d 195 (1961) · Supreme Court of Mississippi · December 4, 1961

## Accident and verdict

Betty Denman, nearly seven years old, was riding with her grandmother Eva Denman in a Ford headed south on U.S. Highway 49E. On a rainy, foggy evening in March 1958, it collided with Joseph Ross's northbound Plymouth near Sumner. The grandmother, Ross, and a Plymouth passenger died. Betty and another passenger survived with injuries but no recollection of the collision itself. Betty, through her mother and next friend, sued the executrix of Ross's estate for negligent driving. 242 Miss. at 434–35.

The case went to a jury on Betty's evidence alone; the defense offered none. The jury awarded $5,000. The trial court then granted the defendant's motion for judgment notwithstanding the verdict. Betty appealed, asking that the jury's verdict and judgment be reinstated. A separate action against the grandmother's estate had ended in a directed defense verdict affirmed earlier; that other action was background, rather than the judgment under review here.

## Evidence and the parties' positions

Betty's father described the cars' positions after the crash, the Ford's displaced engine and transmission, and extensive damage. No evidence established skid marks or the actual point of impact. Numerous photographs documented the destruction but did not explain how the collision occurred. 242 Miss. at 435–36.

John Barnett testified that the Plymouth passed him about three-quarters of a mile before the collision at approximately seventy miles per hour. He observed it for only forty or fifty yards afterward, during which it remained in its lane. Hal Buckley described it passing him about two hundred yards before the crash at seventy-five to eighty miles per hour and continuing without slowing. He could see its taillights through the remaining distance, but could not identify the impact's location or how the two cars came together. He saw no departure from the Plymouth's proper lane and did not notice the oncoming Ford. Earlier testimony in which he professed no estimate of speed was used to impeach him. 242 Miss. at 436–38.

The court held Barnett's relatively remote observation inadmissible under the cited Mississippi decisions. Buckley's continuous observation over the shorter distance was competent; the inconsistency affected credibility rather than admissibility. Betty argued that the jury could infer excessive speed in bad conditions, inadequate control, and avoidability of the collision. She also suggested that physical evidence indicated the Ford had been angled across the highway at impact.

## Issue, rule, and reasoning

Was there a reasonable evidentiary basis for finding that Ross's negligence caused or contributed to the collision, allowing the jury verdict to stand? The court held that the proof did not supply that basis.

Betty bore the burden of establishing, by a preponderance, **both negligence and proximate contribution to the injury**. Buckley's testimony permitted a conclusion that Ross drove at an excessive and negligent speed. It did not establish the missing causal link. There was no evidence of the Ford's speed, lane position, or sudden movement; none located the contact point; the photographs did not reconstruct responsibility. Evidence that the Plymouth traveled too fast was therefore not evidence resolving how that speed caused this particular collision. 242 Miss. at 438–39.

The court distinguished reasonable inference from speculation among possible explanations. Excessive speed might have contributed under some hypothetical sequences of events, but the record supplied no sound basis for choosing one of those sequences as more probable than alternatives. It did not find that the grandmother actually caused the collision or that Ross drove carefully. Rather, the plaintiff had failed to prove a required element even though she had proof of a negligent act. The defense's choice to offer no evidence did not remove her burden.

## Exact judgment and significance

The Supreme Court **affirmed the judgment notwithstanding the verdict for the defendant**. Betty's $5,000 jury award was not reinstated, and the court did not order a new trial. Presiding Justice Lee wrote; Kyle, Arrington, Ethridge, and Gillespie concurred. No dissent, separate writing, or judicial footnotes are reported. 242 Miss. at 439.

The assigned procedural lesson is the limit on a jury's power to fill an evidentiary gap with conjecture. The disputed boundary is concrete: do the accident circumstances reasonably connect speed to impact, or merely permit imagining that connection? The court placed this record on the latter side. Its reasoning does not announce that circumstantial evidence is generally insufficient, nor that negligent speed can never support causal inference.
