# W.W.W. Associates, Inc. v. Giancontieri
77 N.Y.2d 157, 566 N.E.2d 639 (N.Y. 1990) | Contracts | Plain meaning, reciprocal cancellation, and extrinsic evidence

## A negotiated escape clause in a land contract

On October 16, 1986, the owners of a two-acre Suffolk County parcel agreed to sell it to W.W.W. Associates, a real-estate investor and developer, for $750,000. The buyer would pay $25,000 at execution, $225,000 cash at closing, and give a $500,000 purchase-money mortgage payable two years later. Closing was expected around December 1. The printed contract incorporated several negotiated additions, including a clause addressing pending litigation affecting the property. (77 N.Y.2d at 160.)

Paragraph 31 acknowledged that the sellers had been served in that litigation. If it delayed closing, closing would be adjourned until its conclusion; if it remained unresolved by June 1, 1987, either party could cancel, the down payment would be returned, and no further contractual rights would remain. Paragraph 19 merged prior understandings and agreements into the writing and disclaimed reliance on outside statements. The surrounding negotiated provisions expressly gave the purchaser alone other cancellation options: an unconditional ten-day option and an option if the sellers could not deliver building permits for fifty senior-citizen housing units. The contrast between those unilateral options and paragraph 31's reciprocal wording became central. (Id.)

Closing did not occur in December, and the litigation remained unresolved as June approached. On May 13 the buyer announced readiness to close on May 28 and also commenced this specific-performance action. On June 2 the sellers canceled and tendered return of the down payment, which the buyer refused. The sellers sought summary judgment based on their contractual cancellation right. (Id. at 160–161.)

## The buyer's account of the negotiations

The buyer's vice-president asserted that paragraph 31 had been added only to protect the purchaser. A notice of pendency raised concerns about obtaining construction financing and related title insurance for senior housing, despite the sellers' assurances that the underlying suit lacked merit. According to the affidavit, the feared financing obstacle never materialized. The buyer therefore argued that it could waive a condition inserted solely for its own benefit and insist on closing. (Id. at 161–162.)

The affidavit also alleged that a broker reported a seller saying the owners were doing nothing to defend the litigation, waiting until June 2 to cancel, and suggesting a resale at a higher price. The sellers did not respond factually to these assertions; their motion rested on the writing. Supreme Court granted them summary judgment and dismissed the complaint. The Appellate Division reversed, adopted the buyer's affidavit account, searched the record, and directed specific performance for the buyer. The Court of Appeals then reviewed whether extrinsic evidence could override the express reciprocal cancellation clause. The affidavit allegations were not findings made after a merits trial. (Id. at 161.)

## The whole contract supplied an unambiguous answer

Judge Kaye recognized the general principle allowing the sole beneficiary of a contractual condition to waive it. But its applicability depended first on determining what the contract actually said. A complete, clear writing ordinarily controls according to its terms. Outside evidence of an unstated or misstated intention cannot generally add to or vary it. Protecting against fraudulent claims, faulty memory, unavailable witnesses, and unreliable evaluation of alleged negotiations supports commercial stability, especially in real-property transactions. Whether a writing is ambiguous is a legal question for the court. (Id. at 162.)

Read as a whole, this contract unambiguously gave both parties cancellation rights if the litigation remained unresolved after the specified date. The merger clause further demonstrated an intention to embody the full agreement in the writing. There was also a rational reason for the sellers to reserve cancellation: they were financing two-thirds of the price through a purchase-money mortgage and might prefer an as-is cash sale if the property remained burdened by litigation. The court offered that as a logical explanation visible from the contract, not as a finding about the sellers' subjective negotiation motives. (Id. at 162–163.)

The separate clauses granting options to the buyer alone made the reciprocal formulation still clearer. Sophisticated parties who expressly used unilateral language in adjacent terms had chosen bilateral language here. Recasting paragraph 31 as solely the buyer's protection would rewrite that choice. Nor could the buyer acknowledge the sellers' absolute cancellation right while asserting an equally absolute unilateral power to extinguish it through waiver. A right genuinely conferred on both parties could not be converted to a purchaser-only condition by the proffered negotiations. (Id. at 163.)

The court rejected using extrinsic evidence to create ambiguity in an otherwise complete and unambiguous writing. The correct sequence under the rule it applied was to examine the agreement and reach outside evidence when an identified ambiguity required it. Beginning instead with a party's account of intended but unrecorded terms would undermine the completed contract. This did not amount to a holding that context and the contract's other provisions never matter: the court expressly read the clause in the context of the entire writing. (Id.)

## Undisputed allegations were not necessarily material or sufficient

The sellers' failure to contradict the negotiation account did not defeat summary judgment. If the account had been legally material, an undisputed assertion that the clause was for the buyer alone could have supported waiver. But because the writing clearly conferred reciprocal rights, those extrinsic allegations could not alter the bargain and were immaterial to the controlling interpretation. The court did not find the vice-president untruthful or conclude that the negotiations never occurred; it found the offered evidence unable to supply the proposed contractual effect. (Id. at 161–163.)

The full opinion separately addressed bad faith, a point omitted from the assigned judicial excerpt. The claim rested on the vice-president's report of what the broker said a seller had said. Once the sellers showed entitlement to judgment, the buyer needed admissible evidence raising a genuine issue or an acceptable excuse for not supplying it. Even with the more generous treatment accorded a summary-judgment opponent, this conclusory assertion did not raise a triable factual question. The ruling therefore did not adjudicate after trial that the sellers actively defended the underlying lawsuit; it determined that this record failed to establish a factual issue preventing judgment. (Id. at 164.)

## Judgment and source scope

The Court of Appeals reversed the Appellate Division with costs, granted the sellers' summary-judgment motion, and dismissed the complaint. It did not merely vacate the buyer's judgment and remand for factfinding. Kaye wrote for a unanimous seven-member court: Chief Judge Wachtler and Judges Simons, Alexander, Titone, Hancock, Jr., and Bellacosa concurred. The decision issued December 27, 1990. (Id. at 164.)
