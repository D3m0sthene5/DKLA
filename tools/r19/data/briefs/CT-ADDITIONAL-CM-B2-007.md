# Varney v. Ditmars

217 N.Y. 223, 111 N.E. 822 (1916) | Contracts | CT-ADDITIONAL-CM-B2-007

## Employment, the profit promise, and discharge

George Varney, an architect and draftsman, began working for architect Isaac Ditmars in October 1910 at $35 weekly, after asking for $40. When Varney reported another employment offer, Ditmars encouraged him to remain and help clear the office's accumulated work. On February 1, 1911, Ditmars offered Varney and another employee a $5 weekly increase and promised to close his books the following January and give them a fair share of profits if they continued their efforts and got stalled jobs moving. Varney took charge of drafting, helped reorganize personnel, and worked overtime, Sundays, and holidays. At least one longstanding job was completed, and he received $40 weekly. The alleged agreement depended on Varney's testimony; he was the only witness concerning it. 217 N.Y. at 224–227.

Before election day, November 7, Ditmars requested employees to work if possible. Varney said he wished to attend an election where he lived. He became ill that afternoon and remained unable to work until sometime after December 1. A November 11 letter discharged him, accusing him of disloyalty and insubordination and of instructing other employees to remain away. Those accusations were the employer's stated grounds, not an independent jury finding. After recovering, Varney offered to return on an unspecified date; Ditmars denied any agreement and refused. Varney earned about $50 for other work before January 1. Id. at 226–227, 232.

Varney demanded $1,680, comprising $40 weekly for November 7 through December 31 and a fair percentage of net profits from February 1 through year end. The trial court dismissed the complaint at the close of his case. The Appellate Division affirmed, and he appealed to the Court of Appeals. Id. at 224, 226–227.

## The majority's distinction between an ascertainable price and conjecture

Chase's majority opinion held the profit promise too indefinite. Nothing in the parties' words, documents, or another transaction identified a share or a method for determining it. The minds of the parties had not met on that element; it remained subject to the employer's will or further negotiation. Calling the share fair did not let a jury choose any amount it regarded as satisfactory. Id. at 227–228.

The court nevertheless rejected the contention that every agreement using fair or reasonable compensation, or omitting a fixed price, was unenforceable. The parties' intent and subject matter mattered. A fair market price for goods could be established through competent testimony, and contracts for goods or services often implied a reasonable price where nothing prevented that implication. The profit-sharing promise differed because its amount rested on conjecture rather than an identified objective standard. The court treated United Press as consistent with enforceable implied reasonable-price obligations, rather than a universal ban on open prices. Id. at 227–228.

After considering comparable indefinite-compensation decisions, the majority also preserved the distinction between enforcement of an executory bargain and restitution for completed performance. A person who performed under an indefinite arrangement could, in a proper quantum-meruit action, recover reasonable service value. Thus, if Varney's actual work was worth more than $40 weekly, a proper complaint could seek that value less compensation already received. This was a conditional explanation of a possible different claim, not an award or finding that his work exceeded his salary. Id. at 229–232.

## The salary claim and the dissent

The majority also rejected submission of the salary claim to a jury on this record. Varney performed no services during his illness, failed to establish when he offered to return, and earned $50 elsewhere afterward. Any remaining recovery was unquantified. The arrangement was conditional on continuing particular work and clearing specified problems; it did not unqualifiedly guarantee employment through January, and the record did not show satisfaction of the conditions. The court expressly left aside whether the illness itself would permit Ditmars to refuse renewed employment. Id. at 232–233.

Cardozo dissented, joined by Chief Judge Willard Bartlett and Hogan. He agreed that this plaintiff had not supplied the data needed to measure profit damages, even including the excluded evidence. But he rejected the broader premise that a fair share of profits was necessarily incapable of determination: custom might supply a percentage in another record, if contractual intent existed. He likewise read United Press as a case in which the parties reserved price for future agreement, rather than one denying that a fair price could ever be measured. Id. at 233–234.

The dissent would permit salary recovery. It understood the promise as employment through the year-end closing of the books, rather than hiring at will, and found evidence permitting a jury to conclude that discharge lacked cause. It proposed reversal and a new trial, with costs to abide the event. Agreement that profit damages were unproved therefore did not amount to agreement with the majority's complete disposition. Id. at 234–235.

## Exact disposition and reading scope

The court **affirmed the dismissal, with costs**, by four to three. Chase wrote, joined by Collin, Cuddeback, and Pound. Cardozo's separate dissent, joined by Bartlett and Hogan, was read in full. Id. at 233, 235.
