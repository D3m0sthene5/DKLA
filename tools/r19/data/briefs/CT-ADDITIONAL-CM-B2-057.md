# Holcomb v. Zinke
365 N.W.2d 507 (N.D. 1985) | Contracts

## Court, posture, and outcome

The North Dakota Supreme Court affirmed rescission of a home-purchase contract on March 26, 1985. Justice Levine wrote, with Chief Justice Erickstad and Justices Vande Walle, Gierke, and Meschke concurring. Unlike decisions allowing only a claim to proceed, Holcomb reviewed a completed bench trial and upheld the findings supporting constructive fraud, reasonable diligence in rescinding, and restoration of benefits. The sellers' direct nondisclosure liability made it unnecessary to decide vicarious liability for their agents or whether zoning representations independently constituted fraud. The court also affirmed the trial court's accounting without remanding for missing rental-value evidence. (507, 509–514 & nn. 7–8.)

## Sale and latent defects

Marvin and Dorene Zinke listed their rural Jamestown home with Hometown Realty in fall 1981. Agents Mark Johnson and Jolene Nitschke offered to help Jerry and Patsy Holcomb, who had moved from Idaho, find a residence. Nitschke showed the house in the sellers' absence. The buyers spent about an hour viewing it but did not inspect the appliances, sewage system, or water system. Nitschke and later Johnson described the home as almost new, in excellent shape, and with everything in good condition as far as they knew. The parties signed an $85,000 purchase agreement on October 13. (509.)

Before closing, Johnson disclosed that the purchase would violate a local zoning ordinance, but both agents assured the buyers that a variance would be granted. The Holcombs moved in October 31. They soon discovered foul-smelling tap water and defects in the dishwasher, disposal, antenna, water softener, and kitchen heating. Sewage first backed into the basement December 1 and did so repeatedly. They installed new sewer pipes and a drain field, repaired appliances, and replaced several items. Contrary to the assurances, a zoning variance was denied November 10. (509–510 & n. 1.)

The buyers served notice of rescission on February 19, 1982. The sellers refused. After a bench trial, the district court found that the sellers suppressed material facts and the agents affirmatively misrepresented the property's condition and zoning status. It found the buyers relied in good faith and were not at fault for failing to discover the defects. It granted rescission, offset the value of occupancy against repair and replacement expenditures, and ordered the agents' commission returned to the sellers. Only the sellers appealed; the agents and brokerage did not. (509–510.)

## Duty to disclose and constructive fraud

Rescission for fraud under the applicable statute required fraudulent procurement of consent, reasonable diligence in rescinding promptly after discovery, and restoration of the status quo. Timeliness depended on the circumstances and prejudice, not elapsed time alone. Equitable restoration included the reasonable value of use or any rents received; the offer could not be conditional on payment of damages. (510 & n. 2.)

The court understood the trial court's fraud ruling as constructive fraud under section 9-03-09(1), rather than requiring proof of actual fraudulent intent. This doctrine concerned breach of a duty that produced an advantage by misleading another to that person's prejudice. Whether a duty existed was a legal question; the existence of fraud ordinarily presented a factual question, regardless of the trial court's label of conclusions of law. No fiduciary or confidential relationship existed between these sellers and buyers, so any disclosure duty had to arise from the sale's other circumstances. (511 & nn. 3–4.)

The court recognized an exception to caveat emptor for passive concealment of defective real property. A seller had to disclose material facts known or that should be known to the seller and not discoverable by the buyer through ordinary care and diligence. It reasoned that a modern home's complex systems differed from the simple farm-home transactions associated with the old rule. The seller's superior knowledge made disclosure consistent with fair dealing without imposing undue hardship. Silence could mislead as effectively as an affirmative statement. This was a bounded duty involving material, undiscoverable defects, not an obligation to reveal every fact about real estate. (511–513 & n. 6.)

The sellers denied liability for defects they did not know about. But the record supported actual knowledge of the major problems. Mr. Zinke acknowledged that the drain field needed pumping about every two weeks and the septic tank occasionally; otherwise sewage would back into the basement. He also acknowledged occasional water odor, which the household simply tolerated. Litigation testing established contamination. Under Rule 52(a)'s clearly erroneous standard, the court upheld findings that the sellers knew the water and sewage systems were defective and that neither they nor their agents disclosed those conditions. (512.)

The sewage, water, and heating defects were material and latent. The buyers were not at fault for failing to discover them. The court found clear and convincing proof of breach of the disclosure duty and of the resulting advantage: the sellers received a higher price and achieved a sale that full disclosure would have prevented. Footnote 5 separately explained the buyer's right to rely on positive misrepresentations unless failure to investigate was wholly irrational, preposterous, or in bad faith. That explanation was not a holding that all nondisclosure cases dispense with ordinary buyer diligence. (512–513 & nn. 5–7.)

## Timeliness, restoration, and remaining objections

The sellers showed no prejudice from the period preceding the February notice. The court concluded that the buyers exercised reasonable diligence. It expressly acknowledged the trial court's failure to make a separate timeliness finding, calling it a serious lapse, but treated that finding as implicit in the finding that the buyers were not at fault for failing to discover defects. The Holcombs also offered to restore benefits, including the value of their occupancy. The court thus upheld all necessary conditions for rescission without deciding zoning-based fraud or the agents' vicarious liability. (513 & nn. 7–8.)

The court rejected several procedural objections. Admission of bills and checks in this bench trial was not shown to cause reversible prejudice. If the memorandum decision conflicted with the judgment, the judgment controlled. Rule 54(c) permitted relief justified by the case even though the amended complaint had not demanded rescission. Other attacks on factual findings lacked merit. These rulings addressed the sellers' challenges to the actual trial judgment, not a pleading-only standard. (513.)

The final issue concerned the setoff. Although no evidence established reasonable rental value, the trial court equated it to roughly $5,300 in repair and replacement expenses. The Supreme Court recognized a prior case that had remanded for valuation, but concluded that further evidence here would produce little change and that the small practical accounting issue did not justify a remand. Its affirmance rested on this case's circumstances and judicial economy; it did not eliminate the general duty to account for occupancy or announce that repair cost invariably measures rent. The judgment was affirmed without remand. (513–514.)
