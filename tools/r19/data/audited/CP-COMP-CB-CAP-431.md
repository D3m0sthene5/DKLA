# Neely v. Martin K. Eby Construction Co., Inc.

**386 U.S. 317 (1967) — Supreme Court of the United States, March 20, 1967.** Justice White wrote for the Court. The judgment was 6–3: Douglas and Fortas agreed with the construction of Rule 50 but would reverse on evidentiary sufficiency; Black dissented separately. Assigned source: *CivPro.pdf*, physical 566–567, printed 1115–1117, with relocated editorial footnote b at physical 599.

The Court affirmed an appellate judgment directing dismissal after overturning a plaintiff’s jury verdict for insufficient evidence. Neither the Seventh Amendment nor the applicable version of Rule 50 categorically barred that disposition. An appellate court must nevertheless consider proper grounds for a new trial, and must leave matters requiring the trial judge’s discretion to that judge when appropriate. The majority did not decide whether the underlying evidence actually was insufficient. 386 U.S. at 321 n.3, 322–330.

## Facts and course of the litigation

The plaintiff brought a diversity wrongful-death action in federal district court in Colorado after her father fell from a scaffold platform during construction of a missile silo near Elizabeth, Colorado. He was the Night Silo Captain for Sverdrup & Parcel, an engineering firm working on the missile-launcher system. She alleged that Eby’s negligent construction, maintenance, and supervision of the platform proximately caused the death. At the end of her evidence and again after all evidence, Eby moved for a directed verdict. The judge denied the motions, and a jury awarded her $25,000. Id. at 318–319.

Eby then timely sought judgment notwithstanding the verdict or, alternatively, a new trial under Rule 50(b). The district court denied both motions and entered judgment on the verdict. The Tenth Circuit held the evidence insufficient to establish negligence or proximate cause and reversed with instructions to dismiss. The plaintiff had defended her verdict on appeal without presenting alternative grounds for a new trial. She did not petition for rehearing, but sought Supreme Court review of the appellate court’s power to terminate the action. Id. at 319–321, 329.

The majority treated its review as limited to appellate authority and the procedure protecting a verdict winner. Its third footnote expressly declined to decide whether the Tenth Circuit’s sufficiency conclusion was correct. Black disagreed with that limitation and developed a substantial contrary account of the evidence; Douglas and Fortas likewise thought the case should have gone to the jury. The distinction matters: affirmance cannot be represented as a majority merits determination that the scaffold was safe or that its design did not cause the fall. Id. at 321 n.3, 330–335.

## Issue and the competing procedural positions

The plaintiff contended that a court of appeals could not direct dismissal after reversing denial of a Rule 50(b) motion, because doing so denied her an opportunity to ask the trial judge for a new trial. She relied on the jury right, the 1963 amendments to Rule 50, and decisions emphasizing the trial judge’s familiarity with the evidence. Eby sought to preserve the appellate dismissal. Id. at 320–321, 325–326.

The relevant rules had different starting points. Historical Rule 50(c) governed a trial court’s grant of judgment notwithstanding the verdict and its conditional treatment of an accompanying new-trial motion. Rule 50(c)(2) allowed the verdict winner ten days to seek a new trial after such a judgment. Rule 50(d) governed denial of judgment notwithstanding the verdict: it allowed the prevailing party, as appellee, to assert alternative grounds for a new trial if the appellate court rejected the verdict, and authorized appellate treatment or referral of that question. These are the provisions as reproduced and construed in 1967, rather than a statement of present filing deadlines. Id. at 319–320 & nn.1–2, 322–324.

## Holding and reasoning

The Court first rejected a constitutional distinction between trial and appellate entry of judgment notwithstanding the verdict. The appellate court did not restrict the jury’s province more than the trial court would have by granting the preserved motion. The earlier conditional-verdict analysis in *Baltimore & Carolina Line v. Redman* supported that conclusion. Congress’s broad appellate grant in 28 U.S.C. §2106 also authorized an appropriate final judgment or further proceedings. Id. at 321–322.

Rule 50(d), rather than Rule 50(c)(2), controlled because the trial court had denied the motion and jurisdiction then passed to the appellate court. The plaintiff’s opportunity to obtain a new trial depended on the appellate disposition. Rule 50(d)’s protection was real: an appellee could urge new-trial grounds, and the court could grant a new trial or direct the trial judge to consider it. But its permissive provisions did not prohibit judgment for the appellant in every case. Id. at 323–324.

The Court differentiated earlier decisions according to the motions actually made and the issues remaining. In *Cone* and *Globe Liquor*, the verdict loser had not properly sought judgment notwithstanding the verdict in the district court; *Johnson* involved a motion inadequate under Rule 50(b). Those procedural failures prevented appellate entry of that judgment. *Weade* involved a proper motion, but the record suggested an alternative liability theory that could justify another trial. These precedents protected verdict winners and preserved unresolved new-trial matters without imposing an absolute ban on appellate judgment. Id. at 324–326.

The correct approach depended on what the record and the appellee’s arguments required. Dispositive legal issues such as jurisdiction or absolute privilege could end the litigation. Insufficiency cases required more care: erroneous exclusion of evidence or an erroneously demanding burden of proof might explain why the plaintiff’s proof fell short. Appellate courts ordinarily decide such legal issues themselves. A voluntary nonsuit to remedy an evidentiary gap might instead require trial-level discretion; the appellate court could remand for that decision. Judgment should not be ordered where the appellee presented grounds more appropriately addressed by the trial judge. Id. at 325–328.

The appellee could present alternative grounds when the defendant first moved for judgment notwithstanding the verdict, in the appellate brief defending the verdict, or in an appropriate rehearing petition after reversal. Even without such a request, the appellate court could identify a reason for a new trial from its own review and grant one or refer the matter to the district court. Thus the opinion preserves discretionary protection rather than establishing automatic forfeiture in every silent-appellee case. Id. at 328–329.

Here the plaintiff proposed no new-trial ground on appeal and requested no rehearing. She had described the trial as fair and the jury as properly instructed, and had not suggested that the appellate record prevented presentation of an alternative claim. The majority would not presume that the Tenth Circuit ignored the new-trial question, although an express discussion would have been better. Only in the Supreme Court brief did she raise exclusion of witnesses’ opinions about the platform’s adequacy. That argument had not been presented below or included in her certiorari petition, so the Court declined to consider it under its ordinary review policy. Id. at 329–330.

## Separate positions

Douglas and Fortas agreed with the majority’s interpretation of Rule 50 but would reverse because the evidence of negligence and causation was sufficient for the jury. Their short statement does not adopt Black’s procedural argument. Id. at 330.

Black’s dissent advanced three objections. First, he believed the jury verdict was supported and that the Court should reach sufficiency. He described a platform 130 feet above the silo bottom, separated horizontally and vertically from concrete blocks, with a railing requiring a diagonal jump or climb. Witness testimony and photographs, together with possible kickboard and rough-board impediments, permitted negligence and causation inferences. Those details and that evaluative conclusion appear in his dissent, rather than a majority sufficiency holding. Id. at 330–335 & nn.1–2.

Second, Black understood prior decisions to preserve the trial judge’s initial discretion over a new trial after reversal. The judge’s firsthand exposure to the witnesses and case could not be replaced by an appellate transcript. Section 2106’s general authority did not resolve the particular Rule 50 restriction, and a rule saying an appellee “may” assert new-trial grounds did not mean she must do so or lose trial-level consideration. Id. at 335–342 & n.3.

Third, even accepting appellate power, Black thought its application unfair. The plaintiff had relied on the earlier cases, and the record itself showed uncalled witnesses, excluded opinion testimony, and excluded photographs that could warrant another trial. At minimum she should receive an opportunity to argue for that relief rather than lose her claim because of a newly required appellate presentation. Id. at 342–344.

## Disposition and assigned significance

The Court **affirmed the Tenth Circuit’s dismissal direction**. It did not affirm the plaintiff’s verdict or remand for another trial. The practical lesson is to preserve alternative new-trial grounds while defending a favorable verdict, without assuming that appellate reversal necessarily returns the issue to the trial judge. Id. at 330.
