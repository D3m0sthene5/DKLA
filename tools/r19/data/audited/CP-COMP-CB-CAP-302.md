# Golden Eagle Distributing Corp. v. Burroughs Corp. — sanctions appeal

**801 F.2d 1531 (9th Cir. 1986) · Ninth Circuit · October 9, 1986 · Civil Procedure**

**Assigned account:** CivPro.pdf, physical page 373, printed page 713, complete Note 1. The separately briefed district ruling is 103 F.R.D. 124 (1984); the 1987 denial of rehearing is linked history, not this panel opinion.

**Decision in one sentence.** The Ninth Circuit reversed a Rule 11 fee sanction because the district court accepted that the challenged motion had a supportable basis but imposed additional requirements to label an argument as law reform and to discuss adverse authority as an ethical obligation. Under the panel's interpretation of the 1983 rule, those requirements did not independently establish a Rule 11 violation. 801 F.2d at 1539–1542.

## Dispute, motion, and sanctions procedure

Golden Eagle sued Burroughs in Minnesota state court over an allegedly defective computer system, asserting fraud, negligence, and breach of contract. Burroughs removed on diversity grounds and obtained transfer to the Northern District of California, where the proof, computer, and almost all witnesses were located. Kirkland & Ellis represented **Burroughs, the defendant**. It moved for summary judgment, arguing California's limitations law applied and barred the claims, and that California law did not permit the economic-loss negligence recovery Golden Eagle sought. Golden Eagle opposed, and both sides submitted further briefing. Id. at 1533–1536; 103 F.R.D. at 125 (diversity basis of removal).

The district court denied the motion and, on its own initiative, required defense counsel to explain why Rule 11 sanctions should not follow. After briefing and argument, it imposed $3,155.50 in attorney's fees against Kirkland & Ellis. The appellant here was the sanctioned law firm; the appeal challenged the sanctions, not an ultimate judgment that Burroughs was liable for the computer dispute. Id. at 1534.

The district court considered the arguments supportable but objected to their presentation and omitted authority. In the limitations argument, counsel portrayed Van Dusen as supplying a forum-non-conveniens exception under which the transferee court could use California rather than Minnesota law. The Supreme Court had left the relevant question open. There was also uncertainty whether Minnesota would dismiss for forum non conveniens when limitations prevented pursuit elsewhere. The district judge thought counsel's later sanctions memorandum fairly identified these uncertainties, while the original motion presented the position as established law. Id. at 1534–1535.

For economic loss, counsel relied on Seely's restriction of negligence and strict-liability recovery. The district court identified J'Aire's later special-relationship approach and the intermediate cases Pisano and Huang discussing its effect. Counsel discussed J'Aire in reply after Golden Eagle raised it, but omitted the two intermediate cases. The district judge treated these omissions as breaches of candor under professional-conduct standards and, in turn, Rule 11. The panel **assumed** the omitted authorities were directly contrary to decide the larger Rule 11 question; it did not finally resolve whether each was actually indistinguishable. Id. at 1535–1536 & n.1.

## Governing historical rule and review standard

The 1983 rule certified reasonable inquiry into factual and legal support and the absence of an improper purpose. Its amendments made sanctions mandatory for a violation while using an objective reasonableness standard. They aimed to curb frivolous or abusive filings without chilling creative arguments or spawning excessive satellite sanctions litigation. The panel reviewed the amendment's history, earlier decisions, and commentary to situate the dispute within those purposes. Id. at 1533–1534, 1536–1538.

The panel applied the then-prevailing differentiated review standards: whether conduct violated Rule 11 was a legal issue reviewed **de novo**; disputed underlying facts were reviewed for clear error; the appropriateness of the selected sanction was reviewed for abuse of discretion. Id. at 1538. Those are this opinion's historical standards, not a certification of the present review rule.

## Why the additional requirements failed

**Argument identification was not a textual condition.** The rule allowed a filing supported either by existing law or by a good-faith argument to extend, modify, or reverse it. The district judge added a duty to say which category the lawyer was invoking. The panel found no such labeling requirement in the text or the materials explaining its adoption. Once the motion had a reasonably supportable legal and factual basis, an imperfect description of how established the law was did not establish the additional violation imposed here. Id. at 1539–1540.

The distinction can itself be the contested legal issue: an advocate may argue an existing precedent controls while the judge regards the proposed application as an extension. Mandatory sanctions based on that close characterization would put counsel's reputation and personal financial interest in conflict with forceful client representation. The district court made no finding of knowing misrepresentation; its interpretation swept in earnest advocates as well as deliberate deception. The panel feared this would invite hindsight and costly disputes about the degree of confidence in every brief. Id. at 1540–1541.

The panel also interpreted the 1983 rule as addressing the pleading, motion, or other paper itself. An unmeritorious supporting argument did not alone make an otherwise nonfrivolous filing sanctionable. Id. at 1540–1541. That proposition must be kept within the rule version and interpretation actually before the panel, rather than transferred uncritically to later certification language concerning particular claims and contentions.

**Ethical disclosure obligations were not automatically Rule 11 duties.** The panel agreed that lawyers should not misstate law, omit critical facts, or conceal directly adverse authority. It nevertheless distinguished those ethical concerns from the sanction mechanism invoked. Expanding Rule 11 into review of whether every possible adverse precedent had been adequately discussed would multiply research and briefing burdens, conflate advocacy with adjudication, and duplicate established professional-discipline procedures. Id. at 1539, 1541–1542 & nn.2–3.

This did not authorize making legally baseless arguments in ignorance of controlling authority. The panel expressly preserved sanctions where omitted authorities rendered the position unsupportable, and noted other judicial tools for dealing with misconduct. Its objection was to this use of Rule 11 to punish how a motion with a reasonable basis was advocated, rather than to the existence of professional candor requirements generally. Id. at 1542.

## Judgment, panel, and assignment distinctions

The court **reversed the district court's imposition of sanctions**. Judge Schroeder wrote for a panel including Judges Reinhardt and Beezer; the opinion reports no panel dissent, concurrence, or appendix. Id. at 1533, 1542. All four judicial notes were read, including the complete Model Rule 3.3 quotation and comment in note 2, the reservation about judicial ethical enforcement in note 3, and the commentary in note 4.

The assigned account describes the challenged advocate as **plaintiff's counsel**. Both judicial opinions identify Kirkland & Ellis as **defense counsel for Burroughs** (801 F.2d at 1533; the caption lists the firm only as appellant and Burroughs as defendant). The brief corrects that attribution. The assignment's quotation at 1539–1540 accurately captures the panel's rejection of mandatory argument labeling. Its question whether the 1993 amendment changes the result is an editorial question about later law, not a holding of the 1986 panel. The later [Rule 11 text and committee notes](https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/frcp/rule_11) provide separate context; this historical brief does not assert that every aspect of the panel's formulation controls current practice.
