# Anderson v. City of Bessemer City, North Carolina

**470 U.S. 564 (1985) — Supreme Court of the United States, March 19, 1985.** White wrote an eight-Justice Court opinion. Powell joined and also concurred separately; Blackmun concurred only in the judgment. All nine agreed to reverse. Assigned source: *CivPro.pdf*, physical 635, printed 1257–1258, with repeated treatments at physical 65, printed 72, and physical 583, printed 1150.

The Court held that the Fourth Circuit misapplied Rule 52(a) by replacing the district judge’s permissible factual assessment with its own assessment in a Title VII sex-discrimination action. Clear-error review applies to factual findings based on documents and inferences as well as testimony. Credibility choices receive particularly strong deference, but neither the standard nor the case makes factual findings immune from meaningful review. 470 U.S. at 573–580.

## Facts and trial findings

In early 1975 Bessemer City sought a Recreation Director to manage facilities and develop athletic and other recreational programs. A five-person selection committee consisted of four men and one woman, chairperson Auddie Boone. Eight people applied. Anderson, the only female applicant, was a 39-year-old schoolteacher with degrees in social studies and education. The committee selected Donald Kincaid, a 24-year-old recent graduate in physical education. All four men voted for him; Boone voted for Anderson. Id. at 567.

Anderson alleged that her sex explained the rejection. Five years after she filed an administrative charge, the EEOC found reasonable cause; conciliation failed and she received a right-to-sue letter. She then brought this Title VII case in the Western District of North Carolina. After a two-day trial featuring testimony from Anderson, Kincaid, and the five selection-committee members, the judge announced a discrimination finding in a memorandum and requested expanded proposed findings from Anderson. The city objected at length, Anderson replied, and the judge issued formal findings that differed considerably from her proposal. Id. at 567–569, 572–573.

The court found Anderson better qualified for the position’s full range of functions. She had teaching and recreational-supervision experience, earlier hospital recreation work, civic involvement, bookkeeping and public-speaking skills, and familiarity with sports, music, dance, and crafts. Kincaid’s strengths centered on athletics, youth basketball coaching, student teaching, and his physical-education degree. The court considered the broader responsibilities more favorable to Anderson. Id. at 568–569.

The judge also found bias and differential questioning. A male member said the job would be difficult for a woman and he would not want his wife doing it; another had recruited men without recruiting women. Anderson was seriously questioned about night work, travel, and her husband’s approval, while male candidates were not similarly questioned. Boone acknowledged making a comment about Kincaid’s new bride, but the judge found it facetious rather than a serious comparable inquiry and credited her account over competing accounts from male members. Id. at 569–570.

The court rejected the offered explanations for hiring Kincaid as pretextual. The committee earlier preferred Bert Broadway for experience despite his lack of a physical-education degree; it rejected him only because he would not relocate. Testimony also undermined the claimed superiority of Kincaid’s proposed programs. Because Anderson conceded that ordering her appointment would be inappropriate, the court awarded $30,397 backpay and $16,971.59 attorney’s fees. Id. at 570–571 & n.1.

## Appellate dispute and questions presented

The Fourth Circuit held clearly erroneous the findings concerning relative qualifications, differential questioning, and bias. It also suggested that the manner of preparing the findings justified close scrutiny. The Supreme Court therefore addressed both the proper treatment of party-proposed findings and the permissible scope of clear-error review. Id. at 571–573.

The city’s position could succeed only by establishing error in the district court’s factual conclusion, not merely by showing that another reading of the evidence was possible. Discriminatory intent under Title VII was a factual question governed by Rule 52(a), as *Pullman-Standard v. Swint* had held. The Supreme Court did not classify it as a legal conclusion subject to unrestricted fresh review. Id. at 566, 573.

## Rule: two permissible factual views do not establish clear error

The familiar test asks whether the reviewing court, on the whole evidence, has a definite and firm conviction that a mistake occurred. It does not authorize reversal simply because appellate judges would have reached a different conclusion. If the district court’s account is plausible on the entire record, choosing it over another permissible account is not clearly erroneous. Id. at 573–574.

That rule applies even when a finding rests on physical or documentary evidence, or inferences rather than demeanor. Rule 52(a) does not exempt categories of factual findings from clear-error review. The Court also emphasized reasons beyond the trial judge’s ability to observe witnesses: factfinding expertise, the costs of duplicating the trial, and the parties’ entitlement to treat the trial as the principal factfinding event. Id. at 574–575.

Credibility findings receive added deference because demeanor and tone matter. But calling a finding a credibility determination does not immunize it. Extrinsic documents, objective evidence, internal inconsistency, or facial implausibility can support clear error. By contrast, choosing between coherent, plausible, externally uncontradicted accounts ordinarily cannot be clearly erroneous merely because appellate judges prefer the other witness. Id. at 575–576.

## Party-proposed findings do not change the standard

The Court reiterated criticism of uncritical verbatim adoption, especially conclusory findings lacking record support, and recognized the risk of prevailing counsel exaggerating after learning the result. Nevertheless, adopted findings remain the court’s findings and are reviewed for clear error. Id. at 572.

This judge had supplied his own framework before asking for more detail, afforded the city an opportunity to respond, and issued findings differing in organization and substance from Anderson’s proposal. The Court saw no basis to doubt that they reflected his considered judgment or to impose a more stringent appellate standard. It did not endorse automatic approval of every lawyer-written draft. Id. at 572–573.

## Applying the standard to this record

On qualifications, the Fourth Circuit defined the position primarily through athletics; the trial judge treated it as a broader recreational-management job. The Supreme Court found both interpretations plausible. If either had been the trial court’s view, it would not readily qualify as clear error. But the actual trial finding favored Anderson, so the appellate court could not substitute its permissible alternative. Id. at 576–577 & n.2.

On spouse-related questioning, the appellate court interpreted Boone’s recollection of her comment as establishing that Kincaid was questioned comparably. The transcript was ambiguous. Boone denied questioning him in the same context, and tone could explain why the trial judge regarded her remark as facetious. The competing witnesses’ accounts were facially plausible and not contradicted by reliable external proof. The district judge’s choice was entitled to deference. Id. at 577–579 & n.3.

Those findings, plus a committee member’s reservations about women doing the job and male-only recruitment, supported the bias inference. The fourth footnote rejected the appellate suggestion that the members’ wives having worked necessarily dispelled bias. Such evidence was not declared categorically irrelevant; its weight belonged to the district judge. With the subsidiary findings intact, the discrimination finding also survived clear-error review. Id. at 579–580 & n.4.

The Supreme Court expressly disclaimed superior personal knowledge of events or the committee members’ minds. Its task was appellate review of the trial judge’s conclusions, not reconstruction of what happened without deference. Id. at 580–581.

## Separate concurrences and exact disposition

Powell warned that the opinion should not discourage comprehensive review of the record. The Fourth Circuit had carefully examined the evidence rather than acted arbitrarily, and one could reasonably think the trial judge mistaken. But because the factual issue fairly could be decided either way, clear error was absent. His starred note pointed out the five-year administrative delay and stale recollections. He joined the Court’s opinion as well as its judgment. Id. at 581.

Blackmun agreed with reversal and most of the reasoning but did not join the opinion. He regarded the wholly documentary-evidence proposition as broad dictum unnecessary in a case containing extensive oral testimony. Having previously accepted a different approach, he preferred to await a case requiring resolution with focused briefing and argument. His reservation did not create a dissent from the result. Id. at 581–582.

The Court **reversed the Fourth Circuit’s judgment**. The final mandate states reversal, rather than an independently specified damages award or a new-trial direction. Id. at 581.
