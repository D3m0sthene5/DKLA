# Eramo v. Rolling Stone LLC

**314 F.R.D. 205 (W.D. Va. 2016).** Chief United States District Judge Glen E. Conrad decided the nonparty discovery dispute on January 25, 2016. The assigned account appears in CivPro physical page 468, printed pages 910–911. Pinpoints below use the fourteen-page original memorandum opinion, identified as Doc. 34 in No. 3:15-MC-00011; the accompanying order is also reproduced in the complete reported decision.

## Facts and procedural setting

Nicole Eramo, an associate dean at the University of Virginia and head of its Sexual Misconduct Board, sued Rolling Stone LLC, reporter Sabrina Rubin Erdely, and Wenner Media LLC for defamation. Their November 19, 2014 article described the alleged gang rape of a student called “Jackie” and portrayed Eramo’s handling of the student’s report as unsympathetic. Among other things, it attributed to Eramo a comment about the university’s reputation as a “rape school.” Eramo denied making that statement or discouraging a police report. She alleged that she had encouraged reporting, offered assistance, and arranged a meeting with police. The memorandum recounts these competing allegations as background to discovery; it does not decide the defendants’ liability or adjudicate whether the reported assault happened. (Memorandum 1–4.)

The article attracted extensive attention. According to the complaint, it damaged Eramo’s reputation and prompted threats and abusive messages. Later investigations could not substantiate important aspects of the student’s account. Rolling Stone publicly acknowledged discrepancies on December 5, 2014; the following March, the local police said they lacked a substantive factual basis for the incident as described in the article. Eramo alleged that the publishers and reporter had ignored warning signs about their source. They denied negligence, actual malice, and prior awareness that Jackie was unreliable. Those positions made the communications available to the journalists and their efforts to corroborate them central to the defamation dispute. (Memorandum 2–4.)

Eramo filed the defamation action in state court on May 12, 2015, and the defendants removed it on May 29. She served a Rule 45 subpoena on Jackie on July 27. Jackie was a **nonparty**, not a defendant in the defamation action. When negotiations failed to produce documents, Eramo moved to compel in the Eastern District of Virginia. The motion was transferred to the Western District on December 9. Production from other sources then allowed Eramo to narrow the subpoena. The court heard the remaining dispute on January 12, 2016. Demand 17 was withdrawn at that hearing. (Memorandum 4–5 & n.1.)

The remaining demands sought communications with the reporter, the magazine, Eramo, and UVA; communications made under or referring to the pseudonym “Haven Monahan”; and communications about the article. Jackie objected under Evidence Rule 412, challenged relevance and burden, and claimed a patient-counselor privilege for communications with university personnel. A FERPA objection had already been rejected in a separate January 14 order in the underlying action; note 3 reports that earlier ruling rather than independently repeating its analysis. (Memorandum 4–6 & n.3.)

## Governing discovery rules and the parties’ positions

The question was which documents could be compelled from the nonparty under the then-recently amended Rule 26 and under Rule 45, with appropriate protection for intimate information. Rule 26(b)(1) allowed discovery of nonprivileged information relevant to a claim or defense and proportional to the case’s needs. Discoverability did not require admissibility at trial. The court applied broad discovery principles but emphasized that the December 1, 2015 amendment had restored proportionality to the scope provision. Earlier decisions remained useful because proportionality had already appeared elsewhere in Rule 26; the amendment nevertheless warranted greater emphasis on that consideration. (Memorandum 5–6 & n.2.)

The court described a prima facie showing of discoverability followed by the resisting person’s burden to demonstrate insufficient relevance or harm outweighing marginal relevance. It also quoted the Advisory Committee’s warning that the amendment did not require the requesting party to address every proportionality consideration. That framework did not make all requests automatically permissible. Rule 26 required limits on cumulative, duplicative, excessively burdensome, or otherwise out-of-scope discovery. Rule 45 required protection of a nonparty from significant compliance expense. The court considered those limits separately for the particular demands. (Memorandum 5–6, 8–9.)

## Rule 412 did not create a blanket discovery prohibition

Rule 412 generally restricts evidence offered to prove an alleged victim’s other sexual behavior or sexual predisposition. In a civil case, its admissibility exception requires probative value substantially outweighing harm to the victim and unfair prejudice to a party. Jackie treated that protection as a reason to deny discovery. Eramo responded that the documents sought concerned the alleged assault that was the focus of the article rather than proof of other sexual behavior or predisposition, and that admissibility was a different question from discoverability. (Memorandum 6–7.)

The court agreed that Rule 412 governed trial admissibility and did not replace Rule 26’s discovery inquiry. Its advisory notes expressly distinguished the admissibility procedures from civil discovery. But that distinction did not remove the student’s privacy interests from the analysis. The same notes called for protective orders against unwarranted inquiries and to preserve confidentiality. The court therefore examined relevance and proportionality while taking those interests into account. It did not declare every sexual detail discoverable, or decide in advance that every produced document would be admissible at trial. (Memorandum 7–8.)

## Application: communications closely tied to the reporting were discoverable

Demands 1, 2, 6, 12, and 18 sought communications between Jackie and the defendants or between Jackie and Eramo/UVA. These bore directly on what the defendants had been told, what their reporting could have verified, and whether the article accurately described the university official’s response. They supported both Eramo’s allegations that warning signs were ignored and the defendants’ anticipated diligence defense. Although the defendants had already produced their own communications with Jackie, the court allowed corroboration from the student’s copies. Similarly, Eramo could substantiate receipt of communications she herself possessed. The requests were relevant and proportionate, and Jackie had not sufficiently rebutted that showing. (Memorandum 9–10.)

Demand 15 required narrower treatment. Communications involving “Haven Monahan” and Ryan Duffin were discoverable, as were communications involving others whose names Jackie had supplied to the defendants before publication. The same limit applied to communications from Jackie referencing the pseudonym. These could show what the reporters would have learned had they contacted the disclosed people. But pseudonymous communications with undisclosed third parties would not help evaluate the defendants’ assessment of information available to them. The court limited production accordingly instead of authorizing an unrestricted search through all pseudonymous communications. (Memorandum 10–11.)

Demand 16, covering communications about the article, was also granted only in part. The permitted production covered communications initiated by Jackie about the article on or before December 5, 2014. The court understood that date to mark her last public comment standing by the article’s account. Later communications would not illuminate what she had told the defendants or what they should have done to corroborate her story. The opinions of recipients of her earlier communications likewise did not bear on the defendants’ assessment in the way her own statements did. (Memorandum 11–12.)

Crucially, the court excluded communications containing details of the alleged assault from this demand. The dispute concerned the defendants’ portrayal of Eramo and the student’s communications with university personnel; graphic particulars of the alleged attack were irrelevant and disproportionate to that inquiry. This limitation shows why rejecting a categorical Rule 412 objection did not produce unlimited discovery into the student’s sexual history or the alleged incident. (Memorandum 11–12; accompanying order paragraph 3.)

## Privilege, confidentiality, and exact disposition

Virginia Code § 63.2-104.1 required certain providers of sexual-assault services to preserve client confidentiality, but also permitted disclosure pursuant to a court mandate. The court found no authority establishing that this statute created the patient-counselor evidentiary privilege claimed here, and declined to recognize one. It added a conditional observation: even if such a privilege existed, voluntary disclosure of the communications’ contents to the defendants might have waived it. That was an alternative possibility, not an unequivocal independent finding that every conceivable privilege had been waived. The court concluded that Demands 6 and 12 did not implicate privileged material. (Memorandum 12–13.)

The court **granted the motion for Demands 1, 2, 6, 12, and 18; granted and denied it in part for Demands 15 and 16 under the limits above; and denied Demand 17 as moot**. All produced documents were to be marked confidential and handled under the protective order already governing the defamation action. The reported decision includes an accompanying order implementing those terms. There was one district judge’s ruling, with no separate opinion or numerical appellate vote. The decision resolved production, not the defamation merits or final admissibility. (Memorandum 13–14; accompanying order paragraphs 1–5.)
