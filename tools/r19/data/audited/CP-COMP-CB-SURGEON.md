# The Surgeon’s Case

**Y.B. Hil. 48 Edw. III, fol. 6, pl. 11 (King’s Bench); assigned as 1375, translated report dated 1374. Civil Procedure.** Assigned account: CivPro physical 305–306, printed 575. Primary report: the complete Law French page reproduced in *The Pharos*, Fall 1982, printed 21, with the Harvard Ames Foundation translation at VII–24 as a reading aid. The account ultimately failed because the writ omitted the place of the undertaking. Cavendish’s discussion of liability for negligent treatment was reasoning in that unsuccessful action, not a judgment awarding malpractice damages.

A patient alleged that T. B. had wounded the patient’s right hand and that a surgeon, called J. Mort in the reproduced report, undertook to cure it. According to the plaintiff, the surgeon conducted the treatment negligently and the hand was impaired to the point of maiming. These were pleading allegations, not findings after a trial. The report supplies no established fee, treatment method, prognosis, damages amount, or evidentiary verdict. Its opening uses a generic man, while a later passage refers to a woman; this brief preserves that variation rather than inventing a consistent personal identity. Modern historical scholarship identifies a related Stratton–Swanlond record, but the separately identified plea roll has not been independently collated here and does not supply additional established facts for this report. (Reproduced fol. 6, opening and lower dialogue; Harvard VII–24.)

The plaintiff brought trespass on the case. The distinction mattered because the alleged wrong arose from the surgeon’s undertaking and negligent treatment rather than from the surgeon having inflicted the original wound by force. The writ did not allege force and arms or a breach of the king’s peace. More practically, it failed to specify where the surgeon had undertaken the cure. The declaration supplied a location in London, Tower Street in the parish of B., but that location was missing from the initiating writ itself. Much of the argument concerned whether the available form of action and method of trial could accommodate those allegations. (Reproduced fol. 6, opening.)

The defendant initially denied undertaking the cure and offered wager of law. The plaintiff’s advocate argued that the claim was trespass involving a matter within the knowledge of the country, meaning a matter appropriate for a jury, and asked for judgment and damages because the defendant had not answered in the required way. Cavendish rejected the proposed exclusion of wager of law: the writ did not use the force-and-arms or breach-of-peace allegations that would have supported that objection. The report expressly presents that position as the court’s opinion. It then records further exchanges about the tender, in which the plaintiff’s side refused to accept the law. Because the defendant saw that the case would otherwise be adjourned to another term, he waived the tender of law and denied the undertaking in a manner that joined issue. This procedural sequence matters: the later demand for a properly localized jury issue followed the shift away from wager of law. (Reproduced fol. 6, upper and middle dialogue; Harvard VII–24.)

The defendant then challenged the omission of the place of undertaking from the writ. Without it, the court could not know from which neighborhood the jury should come. The plaintiff answered that the declaration had supplied the location, which should suffice to summon the jury from the place where the undertaking was alleged to have occurred. Cavendish held that the challenge was timely and that the writ needed the place for this mode of trial. He also explained the different consequence if the parties had remained with the original wager-of-law issue: specifying a place in the writ would then have been unnecessary. Thus the defect was connected to the procedural method actually chosen, rather than to an abstract requirement that every allegation must always identify a locality. (Reproduced fol. 6, middle dialogue; Harvard VII–24.)

In that discussion Cavendish also said that an action of covenant of this kind had to be maintained without a specialty because a person could not always obtain a clerk to prepare a formal instrument for every small transaction. A specialty here concerns the formal document supporting the undertaking, not a requirement for a modern written medical consent form. The comment explains why the absence of such an instrument should not necessarily leave a person without a remedy for an undertaking badly performed. It did not cure the distinct omission of the place from this writ. The textbook selects this comment to illustrate development beyond the older formal boundaries among actions. Reading it with the report’s ending prevents the mistaken inference that the court sustained this particular pleading and awarded damages. (Reproduced fol. 6, middle; Harvard VII–24; assigned printed 575.)

The remaining dialogue examines who caused the maiming and how the surgeon’s undertaking fits that injury. Cavendish distinguished the person who struck the patient from the surgeon sued over treatment. Recovering damages from the surgeon would not necessarily eliminate an appeal of maiming against the original attacker. In this medieval context, that appeal was a form of proceeding against the alleged wrongdoer, not review by a modern appellate court. The plaintiff’s advocate used an analogy to a horse: a farrier who injures a horse while shoeing it, or a horse healer whose negligent cure maims it, can face a corresponding action. Cavendish accepted the analogy while distinguishing the horse’s inability to bring an appeal of maiming from the patient’s ability to proceed against the original striker. These exchanges are developed reasoning in the report; they are not separate litigated judgments involving the hypothetical horse. (Reproduced fol. 6, lower dialogue.)

Cavendish then articulated the important distinction between negligent performance and an unsuccessful cure despite diligence. If a farrier undertakes a cure and the horse is impaired by the practitioner’s negligence or by failure to attend to the cure within a reasonable time, culpability is reasonable. If the practitioner does everything possible and applies diligence to the cure, culpability does not follow simply because the horse remains uncured. The discussion links liability to the manner of performing the undertaking, not to an absolute promise that treatment will always succeed. It does not state a complete modern medical-negligence test, a standard for expert testimony, or the measure of recoverable damages. Those would exceed the reasoning actually reported. (Reproduced fol. 6, final substantive paragraph.)

The disposition is explicit: because the writ omitted the place where the cure had been undertaken, the action abated and the plaintiff was amerced. The report does not record a damages recovery, a trial on negligence, or an appellate reversal and remand. It contains advocacy and Cavendish’s judicial responses, not a modern majority opinion accompanied by a numerical vote or a written dissent. There are no judicial footnotes in the reproduced primary report. (Reproduced fol. 6, final two lines.)

The assigned citation says 1375; the Harvard translation and Chapman’s source note identify Hilary 48 Edward III as 1374. The reproduction’s surrounding caption also uses 1375. Those conflicting printed labels are preserved without certifying an exact calendar date. The Harvard source warns that the reported advocates’ names may be wrong, so the account does not infer biographies or identify additional judges from them. The complete reproduced Law French report and the whole supplied textbook account were read; the shortened English translation contains omissions and was used alongside the reproduction, not as a substitute for its missing passages.
