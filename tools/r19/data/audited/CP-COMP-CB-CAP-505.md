# Will v. United States

**389 U.S. 90 (1967) · Supreme Court of the United States · November 13, 1967 · Civil Procedure**

The Court vacated an appellate writ of mandamus directed against a district judge's criminal-discovery order and remanded for further consideration. An asserted discovery error, even one the government might have no ordinary opportunity to appeal, did not by itself establish the exceptional circumstances required for mandamus. The decision left the underlying discovery question and the possibility of a properly supported writ open. The assigned account appears in CivPro, physical PDF page 624, printed page 1235, within Note 2 before Kerr.

## Facts and procedural course

Simmie Horwitz faced federal income-tax-evasion indictments in the Northern District of Illinois. He requested a bill of particulars containing thirty items. Negotiations resolved the other disputed requests, leaving request 25: identification of people who had heard oral statements by Horwitz that the government intended to rely on, the circumstances of those statements, whether the listeners were government agents, and any memoranda or transcripts. The district judge initially struck a request for the substance of the statements because of work-product concerns, then allowed that information for statements made to government agents, while excluding the corresponding demand concerning statements to private people. This was a narrowed order rather than an unqualified demand for every prosecution witness or every conversation. **389 U.S. at 91–93 & n.1.**

The government maintained that the order would disclose its witnesses and exceeded the judge's authority under Criminal Rule 7(f). It refused to comply. Judge Will indicated that he would dismiss the indictments unless the government complied, but had not entered a dismissal. The government instead obtained a stay and sought mandamus in the Seventh Circuit. The appellate court denied the petition on July 12, 1966, reconsidered that denial on August 16, and issued the writ on October 4. It supplied no opinion explaining the extraordinary circumstances supporting its decision and received no new briefs or oral argument before changing course. **Id. at 93–94 & nn.2–3.**

The government defended mandamus on two related grounds: the discovery order supposedly exceeded the district court's authority, and the judge allegedly followed a general practice of requiring disclosure contrary to established criminal-discovery rules. It invoked the supervisory reasoning of La Buy and also emphasized its restricted opportunities for appellate review in criminal proceedings. Judge Will disputed both the characterization of the particular order and the alleged general practice. **Id. at 97 n.5, 99–105.**

## Exceptional relief, not an interlocutory appeal

Chief Justice Warren explained that mandamus traditionally confines a lower court to its lawful authority or requires it to exercise authority that it has a duty to exercise. The writ is reserved for exceptional circumstances involving judicial usurpation, and the petitioner must establish a clear and indisputable entitlement. A disagreement about the correctness of an interlocutory ruling is insufficient. Otherwise, mandamus would displace the congressional policy against piecemeal appeals. **Id. at 95–96.**

The Court did not resolve every question about appellate jurisdiction under the All Writs Act. Note 4 expressly left open whether the writ here could operate in aid of an appellate jurisdiction the government might invoke; the Court assumed a sufficient jurisdictional basis and concluded that issuance on this record could not stand. Nor does describing an order as beyond the judge's “power” settle the issue. A court ordinarily has authority to decide a matter incorrectly, and calling the alleged error jurisdictional cannot manufacture the extraordinary conditions for a writ. **Id. at 95 n.4, 98 & n.6.**

Criminal proceedings make restraint particularly significant because interlocutory review can delay trial, and government appeals are constrained by considerations including double jeopardy. The absence of a later government appeal does not independently authorize appellate courts to supply review that Congress has withheld. For purposes of deciding the case, the Court assumed that the government lacked another avenue of review. It nevertheless required exceptional circumstances beyond the desire to obtain an appellate ruling. Note 5 also reserved whether a dismissal, if one were eventually entered, would be appealable. **Id. at 96–98 & n.5.**

These reservations matter: the Court neither decided the government's possible appeal rights after an actual dismissal nor categorically prohibited mandamus in criminal discovery. It rejected the inference that restricted ordinary appeals make every disputed discovery order suitable for extraordinary review.

## Why the particular record did not support the writ

Rule 7(f) gave the judge substantial discretion concerning bills of particulars. Such a bill may identify some potential witnesses when their identities are necessary to prepare the defense; that consequence does not automatically make the bill an impermissible witness-list order. The people who heard particular admissions were not necessarily coextensive with the witnesses the government intended to call. **Id. at 98–101.**

The government's broader allegation also failed to match the record. Judge Will had rejected another request, number 29, that would have demanded a general prosecution witness list. He had narrowed request 25 and indicated willingness to reconsider disclosures upon a concrete showing of danger to a witness or harm to the prosecution. The government had made no particularized showing of that kind. The judicial remarks reproduced in the opinion and its notes showed a liberal approach to discovery, but did not establish systematic defiance of governing rules. A general preference for fuller disclosure and an erroneous ruling, if there was one, were not equivalent to deliberate disregard of established authority. **Id. at 100–105, especially 101–103 & nn.11–13.**

The Court carefully limited its doctrinal comparisons. Rule 7(f) had been amended effective July 1, 1966, to remove the former requirement of cause for a bill of particulars. The challenged order predated promulgation of the amendment, and note 7 left the amendment's construction and effect for consideration on remand. Note 10 distinguished La Buy's civil setting without deciding in the abstract whether criminal mandamus necessarily requires a stricter standard. Note 14 explained why Schlagenhauf's exceptional questions about a novel examination order and the scope of Rule 35 did not turn mandamus into a general corrective for discovery errors. **Id. at 99 n.7, 100 n.10, 104–105 n.14.**

The lack of an appellate explanation was therefore consequential. A reasoned decision needed to identify what the district judge actually required, whether an unlawful general practice existed, and why the circumstances justified extraordinary intervention. Mandamus is corrective rather than a punishment for a judge's views, and its instructional purpose cannot be served adequately by an unexplained command. The Court could not supply the absent reasoning by treating the government's characterization as established fact. **Id. at 104–107.**

## Judgment and Black's separate concurrence

The precise disposition was to **vacate the writ and remand to the Seventh Circuit**, leaving that court free to consider whether a more complete record supported relief under the proper standard. The Court did not affirm the discovery order on its merits or order the government to make the disputed disclosures. **Id. at 107.**

Justice Black concurred in the judgment and agreed substantially with the Court's opinion. He emphasized that the absence of a final judgment does not itself bar an extraordinary writ: exceptional circumstances and irreparable harm can justify intervention before final disposition. In his view, neither court had adequately considered the circumstances that might make this a proper exceptional case. Remand permitted that examination rather than imposing an absolute finality rule. **Id. at 107–108.** Justice Marshall did not participate. The judgment was unanimous among the eight participating Justices, with Black's separately expressed position preserved rather than converted into an unqualified joinder in every part of Warren's reasoning. **Id. at 107–108.**

## Assigned-source distinction

The textbook describes the result as a reversal and states that the Court did not discuss the government's lack of later review, illustrated by its inability to appeal an acquittal. The actual opinion expressly addresses limited government appeals and assumes for present purposes that there is no other avenue of review. Its answer is that this limitation does not eliminate mandamus's exceptional-circumstances requirement. The formal mandate is vacatur and remand, leaving further consideration possible. **Assigned physical 624, printed 1235; actual 389 U.S. at 96–98 & n.5, 107.**
