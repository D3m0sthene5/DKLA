# King v. Whitmer

**556 F. Supp. 3d 680 (E.D. Mich. 2021) · United States District Court, Eastern District of Michigan · August 25, 2021 · Civil Procedure**

**Assigned account:** CivPro.pdf, physical page 374, printed page 715, complete Note 6. This brief distinguishes the August sanctions determination from the December 2, 2021 fee-amount order, 2021 WL 5711102, which the account also identifies. Linda V. Parker issued both decisions. Pinpoints to the first decision use its original 110-page ECF No. 172 pagination; December pinpoints use the Supreme Court-hosted reproduction at appendix pages 139a–163a.

**Decision in one sentence.** Attorneys who pursued Michigan election litigation without a reasonable inquiry into its legal and factual premises, advanced it for an improper purpose, and prolonged proceedings after their own asserted deadline for effective relief could be sanctioned under Rule 11, § 1927, and inherent authority; neither political importance nor asserted personal belief displaced those distinct obligations. August opinion at 47–103.

## Facts and procedural path

Registered Michigan voters and Republican presidential-elector nominees sued Governor Gretchen Whitmer, Secretary of State Jocelyn Benson, and the Board of State Canvassers late on November 25, 2020. They alleged constitutional and Michigan Election Code violations in the presidential election. An amended complaint followed on November 29. Their requested relief included decertifying Michigan's results, certifying a result favoring Donald Trump, impounding voting machines, and obtaining a recount. The allegations rested on hundreds of pages of exhibits, including affidavits recycled from earlier election challenges and purported expert analyses. August opinion at 4–8, 54.

The City of Detroit, the Democratic National Committee and Michigan Democratic Party, and voter Robert Davis intervened. On December 7 the court denied emergency injunctive relief, identifying sovereign immunity, laches, standing, mootness, and abstention obstacles, as well as the deficient evidentiary foundation. Counsel appealed and petitioned the Supreme Court. In seeking extraordinary relief they themselves said effective relief would become unavailable after December 14, when presidential electors voted. Nevertheless, they continued the district-court proceedings afterward. They voluntarily dismissed in January; the sanctions proceedings remained. Id. at 8–18, 47–52.

The City served its Rule 11 motion on December 15 and filed it January 5, after the safe-harbor interval. It also sought § 1927 sanctions through its dismissal motion. The State Defendants and Davis pursued sanctions as well. A July 12, 2021 hearing lasted almost six hours. The lawyers could obtain their own counsel, respond to the court's questions, submit further briefs, and supply affidavits. No attorney filed the offered post-hearing affidavit. The court rejected the premise that another trial testing each election affiant's truthfulness was essential to evaluating counsel's reasonable inquiry. Id. at 10–19, 32–34, 65–68.

The August order reached nine lawyers: Sidney Powell, L. Lin Wood, Emily Newman, Julia Z. Haller, Brandon Johnson, Scott Hagerstrom, Howard Kleinhendler, Gregory Rohl, and Stefanie Lynn Junttila. It did not impose sanctions on the plaintiffs themselves; note 9 explains that the record did not show that they caused the violations. Id. at 19 n.9, 109–110. This distinction prevents treating attorney responsibility as an automatic punishment of every unsuccessful litigant.

## Sources of authority and the attorneys' defenses

**Rule 11 required an objective inquiry.** Presenting or later advocating a pleading certified that it had a proper purpose, legally warranted contentions or a nonfrivolous basis for legal change, and supported factual contentions or appropriately identified ones likely to receive support. The relevant question was what a reasonable attorney should have investigated before presenting the assertions. Honest belief did not convert speculation into evidentiary support. The court separately assessed Rule 11(b)(1), (2), and (3), rather than treating all three as a subjective bad-faith test. Id. at 21–25, 53–101, 65–66 nn.45–47.

**Section 1927 addressed unreasonable multiplication.** The court used the Sixth Circuit's objective standard requiring more than inadvertence or negligence but less than subjective bad faith. Its application focused on failing to dismiss after December 14, thereby forcing additional dismissal motions and litigation work, rather than treating the initial complaint alone as multiplication. An interlocutory injunction appeal had not divested the district court of authority over the entire action; no stay had been obtained. Counsel's reliance on continued appellate activity did not excuse unnecessarily prolonging the district-court matter. Id. at 20–21, 47–52, 50–51 n.27.

**Inherent authority required its own findings and limits.** For inherent-authority fees, the court required meritless claims, counsel's actual or constructive knowledge of that deficiency, and an improper purpose. It also recognized that compensatory fees must be caused by the misconduct under Goodyear, rather than serving as an unrestricted punitive award. The court found the required bad faith from the combined litigation conduct and applied that authority independently of Rule 11. Id. at 42–47, 101–102.

Several lawyers tried to distance themselves from the submissions. Wood denied authorizing his appearance or participating meaningfully. The court assessed his consent to assist, publicly acknowledged representation, knowledge of the sanctions effort, failure to object to being named, and inconsistent explanations. Newman cited her limited time and lack of drafting; Rohl characterized himself as local counsel who received an enormous complaint shortly before filing. The court rejected categorical exemptions for named consenting cocounsel, limited hours, or local-counsel status. It also explained that the post-1993 rule could reach responsible counsel who later advocated papers, not merely the individual who originally signed. These were record-specific conclusions about these attorneys, not a holding that every person mentioned in any filing is automatically sanctionable. Id. at 25–37, including nn.13–22.

Counsel challenged service, the specificity of the City's motion, and the absence of an accompanying supporting brief during the safe harbor. The court found that the motion identified the challenged legal, factual, and improper-purpose grounds and incorporated the City's earlier detailed opposition. The local requirement that a filed motion have a brief did not require serving that brief with the safe-harbor motion. The addresses were those counsel had supplied. Independent inherent-authority grounds also remained available, with adequate notice and opportunity to respond. Importantly, the court did **not** claim it had issued the Rule 11 show-cause order needed for court-initiated sanctions: note 10 says such sanctions require a show-cause order, that none was issued, and that the court need not rely on that own-initiative authority; the Rule 11 sanctions rest on the City's motion. Id. at 21 n.10, 37–47, including nn.23–26.

## Why the asserted facts and law failed reasonable inquiry

The court's legal analysis was not simply that plaintiffs lost. It identified threshold barriers and the absence of a nonfrivolous path to the requested relief. It also tested counsel's claims that particular election practices violated Michigan law. An observer's account of someone delivering multiple ballots did not establish a violation where state law allowed delivery for family or household members. The alleged absence of postmarks was not proof of illegality when lawful ballots could be delivered by hand or drop box. Ballots going through a machine more than once did not necessarily mean repeated counting; routine jams and problem ballots could require rescanning with the earlier count canceled. Counsel nevertheless treated suspicions as legal violations without investigating the governing rules. Id. at 53–64, including nn.35–44.

The factual analysis proceeded independently. Jessy Jacob's affidavit said people voted in person after applying for absentee ballots; it did not say they had also voted absentee. The asserted double voting required a missing factual step. A dog-walker's observation of bags with unseen contents being transferred at a post office supplied no connection to ballots, the counting center, or a missed deadline. Melissa Carone's observations of vans and a later news announcement did not establish that vans carried ballots, that the ballots were fraudulent, or that they favored Biden. Counsel had not asked the questions needed to bridge those gaps. Id. at 68–76, including nn.48–60.

Articia Bomer expressed a belief that workers were changing votes; counsel labeled it eyewitness testimony without asking whether she actually saw such changes. Copying affidavits from other cases did not transfer away their own reasonable-inquiry duty. Nor could expert reliance on an affidavit cure its lack of underlying support. The court further examined Russell Ramsland's extraordinary turnout figures, readily available contrary official data, earlier confusion between Minnesota and Michigan localities, and subsequent numerical revisions that did not excuse the deficient original inquiry. Id. at 75, 77–86, including nn.61–72.

These findings did not depend on proving every affiant a liar. The defect was frequently the gap between what an affidavit actually said and the much stronger proposition the lawyers claimed it established. The court therefore declined to convert the sanctions inquiry into another election-fraud evidentiary trial. Note 1 expressly reserves whether fraud actually occurred as outside the issue then before it. Id. at 2–3 n.1, 65–68.

For improper purpose and inherent bad faith, the court additionally considered efforts to use litigation to create a public political narrative, positions taken in the related Dominion defamation action, counsel's confirmation bias, the timing of their challenge, and misleading claims about supposed expert Joshua Merritt's military-intelligence credentials. Its First Amendment response distinguished public political speech from the professional obligations attached to court submissions. Those are the judge's findings in this sanctions record, not an independent political or election-merits finding made by this brief. Id. at 87–103, including nn.73–82.

## Exact orders and the linked fee determination

The August court granted the State Defendants' and City's sanctions motions. It granted Davis's motion insofar as sanctions were warranted but refused his own fees and costs because his intervention contributed little and consumed court resources. The nine lawyers were ordered jointly and severally to pay the State Defendants' and City's reasonable defense fees and costs, with documentation and objections to determine amounts. Each also had to complete at least twelve hours of nonpartisan continuing legal education within six months—at least six on pleading standards and six on election law—at personal expense. The clerk was to refer them to Michigan and their respective licensing jurisdictions for investigation and **possible** suspension or disbarment. The court did not itself enter nine disbarment judgments. Id. at 103–110, including nn.83–85. There was one district judge's opinion, with no separate judicial writing or appellate vote tally.

The December 2 order then awarded **$21,964.75 to Whitmer and Benson and $153,285.62 to Detroit**, jointly and severally against the nine lawyers. It rejected most objections to detailed billing, necessary multiple-attorney work, litigation-related media research, and the time spent developing and defending the sanctions motions. It accepted particular reductions, including **$26,077 for appellate work**, public-relations time, an inappropriate post-filing review entry, and limited quarter-hour billing. Thus the textbook's amount exceeding $175,000 corresponds to the two awards together; the August opinion itself had not yet fixed that total. December order at 139a–162a, including all six notes.

The December court distinguished deterrence under Rule 11 and § 1927 from compensatory inherent-authority fees, applied lodestar reasonableness, and made specific billing determinations rather than simply equating requested costs with the award. It ordered payment within thirty days but **stayed that obligation if either sanctions decision was appealed, pending resolution of all appeals**. Id. at 141a–163a. This is a historical account of those two 2021 orders. The assignment's statement that an appeal was pending is its then-current procedural description, not a claim about present appellate status.
