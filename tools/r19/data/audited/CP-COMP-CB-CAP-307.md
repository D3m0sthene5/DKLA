# Sprint Communications Co., L. P. v. APCC Services, Inc.

**554 U.S. 269 (2008) · Supreme Court of the United States · June 23, 2008 · Civil Procedure**

**Assigned account:** CivPro.pdf, physical page 377, printed page 720: complete Note 4 before the joinder heading. The preceding discussion distinguishes real party in interest, capacity, and standing; those concepts are related but not interchangeable.

**Decision in one sentence.** An assignee holding legal title to a claim for money could pursue it in federal court even though it had promised to remit all recovered proceeds to the assignor; the Court treated the transferred claim and the established tradition of collection assignments as sufficient for Article III standing. 554 U.S. at 271, 285–292.

## Facts and procedural path

A customer making a long-distance call from a payphone using an access code or 1–800 number paid the long-distance carrier, although the payphone operator initially connected the call. Federal law required the carrier to compensate the operator for these dial-around calls. The operators could sue for unpaid compensation. Individual suits were expensive, factually demanding, and often worth little, so operators assigned their claims to billing and collection firms called aggregators. Id. at 271–272.

The respondent aggregators collected assignments from approximately 1,400 operators. Each Assignment and Power of Attorney Agreement transferred all rights, title, and interest in the operator's dial-around compensation claims for periods since October 1, 1997. It appointed the aggregator attorney-in-fact, required litigation in the operator's interest, and prohibited revocation without the aggregator's written consent. A separate agreement required the aggregator to remit all proceeds to the operator, which would pay for the service, typically through a quarterly charge. Id. at 272. Thus legal title to the claim was transferred, but the aggregator retained no share of the substantive recovery.

The aggregators sued Sprint, AT&T, and other carriers. The district court first accepted AT&T's standing objection but reconsidered in light of the longstanding treatment of assignees for collection. A divided D.C. Circuit panel initially accepted standing but rejected a statutory private right of action. The Supreme Court vacated that judgment for reconsideration after Global Crossing recognized the relevant cause of action. On remand, the D.C. Circuit affirmed the district court orders permitting the litigation to proceed. The carriers then obtained review of the standing question. Id. at 272–273. The 2008 decision did not newly determine whether federal telecommunications law created the cause of action or whether particular compensation was actually unpaid.

## Majority: a transferred claim can supply the relevant injury

Justice Breyer began with injury in fact, causation, and redressability. The unpaid compensation was a concrete injury caused by the carriers, and a recovery would remedy it. The contested question was whether the aggregators could invoke that injury when the operators originally suffered it and would ultimately receive the recovery. Id. at 273–275.

**History explained why legal title mattered.** The majority acknowledged that early English common law broadly restricted assignments to avoid maintenance and multiplying litigation. As commercial needs grew, equity recognized beneficial assignments, while courts of law used powers of attorney and actions in the assignor's name. Legal title could support suit even after its holder had transferred the beneficial interest. American practice similarly developed protections for assignees and eventually permitted transfers of legal title and suits in the assignee's own name. Id. at 275–279.

The majority then addressed collection-only assignments specifically. Numerous nineteenth-century state decisions accepted suits where the assignee held legal title but had promised to account to the assignor for the proceeds. It acknowledged contrary or inconsistent state decisions, rather than claiming universal agreement. Its appendix supplied twenty-eight developed case examples. Federal practice and the Supreme Court's Waite, Spiller, and Titus decisions supplied additional support. Breyer expressly said those three precedents did **not conclusively resolve** the present Article III issue; their significance was the recognized amenability of collection-assignee litigation to judicial resolution. Id. at 279–285, 292–298.

**Modern standing doctrine also supported the assignment.** The operators transferred their claims completely. Vermont Agency had recognized that assignment could permit a plaintiff to invoke an injury originally suffered by the assignor. Breyer rejected the claim that an operator's injury was constitutionally incapable of traveling with its assigned legal claim. Id. at 285–286.

For redressability, the relevant question was whether a favorable judgment remedied the injury asserted, not how the plaintiff would subsequently use the recovered funds. Payment of the overdue compensation would cure the injury underlying the transferred claim, although contractual obligations then required remittance. The Court compared familiar suits by trustees, guardians, receivers, and executors benefiting others. The aggregators also had contractual duties to litigate in the operators' interests. Id. at 286–288.

The majority did not equate assignment with simply hiring counsel. An actual claim assignment conveyed a property right that creditors might attach; an ordinary attorney engagement did not. It also treated the carriers' proposed line as impractical: a small retained interest or a trust arrangement could readily change the result without changing the underlying dispute. That practical observation supplemented the historical and doctrinal analysis; it was not an instruction to disregard Article III whenever compliance was inconvenient. Id. at 288–289.

## Prudential objections and limits

The carriers argued that the aggregators were asserting third parties' rights. The Court answered that the complete assignments made the claims the aggregators' own legal rights, although the injuries initially belonged to the operators. This differed from an unassigned attempt to vindicate another person's claim. Id. at 289–290.

The carriers also characterized the arrangement as circumventing Rule 23. The Court observed that class actions were permissive and that the federal system recognized several aggregation methods, including joinder, consolidation, and transfer. Choosing a lawful alternative did not itself eliminate standing. Id. at 290–291.

Potential discovery, preclusion, and counterclaim difficulties likewise did not justify a categorical bar. The Court identified possible discovery orders, required-party joinder, third-party practice, and administrative solutions. It expressly declined to hold that any particular device was appropriate in this litigation; these examples showed that courts were not helpless. Finally, the assignments were made for ordinary business purposes and no bad-faith assignment was alleged. The Court reserved additional prudential questions that a different record might present. Id. at 291–292.

## Roberts's dissent

Chief Justice Roberts, joined by Scalia, Thomas, and Alito, would have vacated the D.C. Circuit judgment and remanded. His disagreement concerned Article III, so he expressly declined to reach prudential limitations. Id. at 298 n.1, 315.

He understood standing to require a personal, tangible stake in the recovery. These aggregators received service fees based on payphones and lines, not a percentage or amount dependent on the litigation's success. Business goodwill from pursuing clients' claims was a byproduct of litigation, insufficient by itself. Vermont Agency differed because the qui tam relator had a partial assignment including a share of the recovery. Id. at 298–303.

Roberts also rejected the analogy to an injured plaintiff who independently promised to donate a recovery. That plaintiff already held a substantive claim and independently chose its disposition; the aggregators acquired their rights only on the condition that every recovered dollar be remitted. His second note distinguished guardians, court-appointed receivers, and trustees with independent fiduciary obligations, and regarded the majority's contractual-duty response as circular. Id. at 303–305 & n.2.

The historical disagreement was substantial. Roberts argued that the general recognition of assignments did not establish the specific right to litigate with no beneficial interest. Maintenance restrictions, divergent state approaches, reversals of state doctrine, and nineteenth-century innovations made the tradition equivocal and too late to establish original Article III meaning. His third through fifth notes explain maintenance, the limited relevance of retained legal title and debt discharge, and exceptions that did not establish collection-only standing. He also read Waite as a statutory collusion decision, Spiller as enforcement of an agency award, and Titus as state assignment law and full faith and credit—not precedents deciding the present constitutional issue. Id. at 305–314, including nn.3–5.

## Judgment and source distinctions

The Court **affirmed** the D.C. Circuit judgment, five to four. Breyer wrote for Stevens, Kennedy, Souter, and Ginsburg; Roberts's four-Justice dissent was the sole separate writing. Id. at 270, 292, 298. The result permitted the litigation to continue; it did not award the underlying compensation.

The textbook compresses the arrangement into remitting recovery less a flat fee. The actual opinion describes remittance of **all proceeds**, with service payment under a separate arrangement, and the dissent emphasizes that compensation was not tied to recovery. Id. at 272, 300–301. Keeping that distinction explains why the dispute was not simply whether an assignee retaining some substantive recovery had standing.
