# Mohawk Industries, Inc. v. Carpenter

**558 U.S. 100 (2009) — Supreme Court of the United States, December 8, 2009. Civil Procedure.** Justice Sotomayor wrote for eight Justices; Justice Thomas joined Part II–C and concurred in the judgment, writing separately. All nine agreed to affirm. Disclosure orders adverse to the attorney-client privilege are not immediately appealable as collateral orders under § 1291.

## Facts and procedural history

Norman Carpenter, a former shift supervisor at Mohawk, sued the company in federal court in Georgia in 2007, alleging unlawful termination under 42 U.S.C. § 1985(2) and state law. According to his complaint, he told human resources that Mohawk employed undocumented immigrants. He did not then know that another pending case, Williams, alleged a conspiracy to depress legal employees’ wages through such hiring. Mohawk directed Carpenter to meet the lawyer it had retained in Williams. Carpenter alleged that the lawyer pressured him to recant and that the company fired him under false pretenses when he refused. Those were Carpenter’s allegations, rather than Supreme Court findings establishing wrongful termination. 558 U.S. at 103–104.

The Williams plaintiffs sought a hearing to investigate Carpenter’s allegations. Mohawk responded by denying them and presenting its competing account: Carpenter had tried to get the company to hire an undocumented worker, the company investigated, retained counsel interviewed him, and it fired him for violating company policy. These disclosures in the other litigation became central to the privilege dispute in Carpenter’s case. Id. at 104.

Carpenter sought discovery about the attorney meeting and termination decision. The district court agreed that the information was privileged but found implicit waiver through Mohawk’s representations in Williams and ordered disclosure. It declined certification under § 1292(b), but stayed the order to permit exploration of other review avenues. Mohawk filed both an appeal and a mandamus petition. The Eleventh Circuit dismissed the appeal because the order was not an appealable collateral order, and denied mandamus because it found no clear usurpation of power or abuse of discretion. The Supreme Court granted review of the collateral-order question. Mohawk did not ask it to review the mandamus denial. Id. at 104–105, 111 n. 3.

## Issue, holding, and reserved matters

Does a disclosure order rejecting an attorney-client privilege claim qualify for immediate collateral-order appeal? No. Postjudgment review and other established mechanisms sufficiently protect the privilege without authorizing an appeal as of right for the entire category of adverse privilege rulings. Id. at 108–114.

The circuit had found the order conclusive and sufficiently separate from the merits, but not effectively unreviewable later. The Supreme Court resolved the last condition and expressly declined to decide whether the other conditions were satisfied. It also did not determine whether Mohawk had actually waived the privilege or whether its employment actions were unlawful. Id. at 108.

## Framework and categorical reasoning

Section 1291 ordinarily postpones appeal until a final decision. Cohen permits a small class of orders that conclusively decide important questions separate from the merits and are effectively unreviewable after final judgment. The exception must not swallow the rule: piecemeal appeals delay litigation, burden appellate courts, and interfere with trial judges’ management responsibilities. Id. at 106–107.

The importance of an interest in the abstract is insufficient. The inquiry asks whether waiting so imperils that interest that an immediate appeal should be available for the category as a whole. Individual hardship, imperfect repair, or potential savings in one case do not by themselves establish jurisdiction. The Court assessed the entire class of adverse attorney-client privilege disclosure orders rather than making appealability turn on the particular consequences of Mohawk’s order. Id. at 107–109.

Mohawk argued that confidentiality promotes candid consultation and that disclosure irreversibly destroys it. The Court acknowledged the privilege’s institutional importance and the intrusion caused by disclosure. It nevertheless concluded that postjudgment remedies generally sufficed: an appellate court could vacate an adverse judgment and require a new trial excluding improperly disclosed material and its fruits. Id. at 108–109.

The Court also doubted that postponing review meaningfully reduced clients’ willingness to speak candidly. Clients ordinarily weigh the privilege’s scope and exceptions rather than the remote possibility of an erroneous order and its appeal timetable. Routine privilege rulings usually apply settled law and often receive factual deference. Footnote 2 reserved the possibility that systematic underenforcement might present a different situation, but the Court had no indication of such a problem. Id. at 109–110 & n. 2.

## Alternative safeguards and their qualifications

Parties could seek § 1292(b) certification and discretionary appellate acceptance when a privilege issue presented an appropriate controlling legal question. Exceptional judicial usurpation, clear abuse, or manifest injustice could support mandamus. Neither avenue guaranteed relief in every case; the Court treated them as safeguards for serious errors. Id. at 110–111.

A party could also refuse disclosure and incur sanctions, preserving the confidential information while obtaining later review of the resulting judgment. In appropriate circumstances contempt offered another review route, at least where the citation amounted to criminal punishment. The opinion did not say that every civil contempt order against a party was immediately appealable. Protective orders could limit spillover from sensitive disclosures. These mechanisms, together with final-judgment review, supported the categorical conclusion despite imperfect relief in some cases. Id. at 111–112.

The Court considered the costs of allowing successive appeals of adverse privilege rulings greater than the limited benefits. Mohawk pointed to few appeals in circuits permitting them, but the Court cautioned that wider recognition might increase use and generate efforts to extend the doctrine to other sensitive information. Footnote 4 expressly declined to decide the government’s proposal concerning certain governmental privileges rooted in separation of powers. The holding should not be expanded into an adjudication of that reserved category. Id. at 112–113 & n. 4.

## Rulemaking, Thomas’s separate opinion, and judgment

Part II–C emphasized Congress’s authorization of rulemaking to define appellate finality and provide additional interlocutory appeals. The process draws on bench and bar experience and permits broader consideration than adjudication of an individual dispute. Any further appeal avenue for attorney-client disclosure orders should come through that process. Thomas joined this part. Id. at 113–114.

Thomas agreed with the outcome but declined the remaining Cohen analysis. He considered Congress’s selection of rulemaking sufficient reason not to add this category of appeals. He criticized judicial expansion and later narrowing of Cohen as policy judgments better made through the authorized process. His starred note specifically questioned the majority’s confidence in later remedies where disclosure affected the separate Williams litigation and its speculative prediction of appellate burdens. He nevertheless preferred leaving those competing costs and benefits to rulemaking rather than deciding them through categorical assumptions based on one case. Id. at 114–119 & n. * at 118.

The Supreme Court **affirmed the Eleventh Circuit’s judgment**. That resolved the attempted collateral-order appeal, rather than approving the district court’s waiver determination on its merits. Id. at 114.
