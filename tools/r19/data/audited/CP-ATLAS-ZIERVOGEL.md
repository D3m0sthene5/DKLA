# Ziervogel v. Royal Packing Co.

**225 S.W.2d 798 (St. Louis Court of Appeals, Missouri, December 20, 1949); motion for rehearing or transfer denied January 20, 1950. Civil Procedure.** Assigned passage: CivPro physical 346–348, printed 659–661, with the complete five attached editorial notes through printed 662. Judge McCullen wrote the original appellate opinion; Presiding Judge Anderson and Judge Hughes concurred. This is a source-limited brief: the original appellate opinion’s complete reproduced text was read, but the later rehearing opinion is available here only in the assigned edited excerpt. Neither the original reporter facsimile nor the complete rehearing writing has been recovered.

The case distinguishes actual advance knowledge of an injury from legally sufficient pleading of special damages. The defendant knew before trial that the plaintiff attributed elevated blood pressure to the accident. Nevertheless, under the Missouri provision requiring items of special damage to be specifically stated, that knowledge did not authorize recovery for an unpleaded condition that was not shown to be a necessary or inevitable result of the injuries actually alleged. The appellate court reversed the plaintiff’s $2,000 judgment and remanded. It did not decide that the defendant was free of negligence or that the plaintiff had no recoverable injuries. (Reproduced original opinion ¶¶6, 8–10, 19–20; reporter markers 801–804.)

The plaintiff alleged an April 20, 1948 collision at Vandeventer Avenue and North Market Street in St. Louis. She drove a Studebaker north on Vandeventer; the defendant’s employee drove a tractor-trailer west on North Market. Her petition alleged failures of lookout, warning, speed control, stopping, swerving, and right of way. It described injuries to her neck, back, spine, and nervous system, other injuries, and permanently impaired earning capacity. The defendant admitted the vehicles’ directions and the collision but denied the other allegations. It asserted contributory negligence, including failure to observe traffic, excessive speed, inadequate control, and failure to obey a school stop sign. These were the competing pleadings, not appellate findings that either driver acted in every way alleged. (Reproduced ¶¶1–3.)

A jury returned a $2,000 verdict for the plaintiff. After an unsuccessful motion for a new trial, the defendant appealed. The appeal did not challenge whether the plaintiff had enough evidence to submit her claim to the jury. The court therefore declined to set out the entire collision evidence. Both sides had presented witnesses and medical testimony; the appellate dispute addressed the admission of particular injuries and aspects of the instructions. That limited appellate record prevents turning the decision into a detailed factual determination of fault at the intersection. (Reproduced ¶¶1, 4.)

The defendant objected when plaintiff’s counsel told the jury in opening that the accident had increased her blood pressure, asked that the jury be discharged, and objected again to evidence concerning that condition. It also challenged evidence of a shoulder injury. The petition did not specifically claim elevated blood pressure or aggravation of an existing blood-pressure condition. Nor did it allege an injury from which increased blood pressure could reasonably be regarded as inevitable; the evidence did not establish such inevitability either. Under the court’s stated Missouri doctrine, an injury neither pleaded nor necessarily resulting from a pleaded injury was an item of special damage requiring its own specific statement. The same problem applied to the unpleaded shoulder injury. (Reproduced ¶¶5, 9–10, 13, 16.)

The plaintiff had a substantial notice argument. Before trial she had told the insurer’s claim agent about the claimed blood-pressure consequence. The defendant’s physician, Dr. Leo A. Will, had examined her and reported to that agent. The defendant had also taken her deposition. The court expressly acknowledged that the defendant could not fairly claim surprise about the condition at trial. But it distinguished absence of surprise from satisfaction of the statutory pleading requirement. The defendant was entitled to insist that special damages appear in the petition. The plaintiff had neither amended nor requested leave to amend to include them, although the court noted that amendment could have been sought on terms appropriate at that stage. Thus the opinion does not say amendment was legally impossible; it says actual notice did not supply the missing amendment. (Reproduced ¶6, reporter marker 801.)

The plaintiff also invoked Missouri’s newer, liberalized Civil Code. Section 35 called for concise, direct allegations without technical forms; section 36 required a short and plain statement and demand for relief; section 57 directed construction to achieve substantial justice. The court accepted the code’s general purpose but read those provisions together with section 52, Mo. R.S.A. §847.52, which expressly required specific statement of special damages. General procedural liberalization could not nullify that specific retained requirement. Earlier Missouri decisions remained relevant because the new code itself preserved the principle they applied. The holding rested on this historical Missouri provision. The textbook substitutes a reference to Missouri Rule 55.19 in brackets; that editorial modernization should not be mistaken for the provision identified in the 1949 opinion. (Reproduced ¶¶7–10; assigned printed 660.)

Two related federal analogies also failed to change the result. The plaintiff invoked federal illustrative Form 10 to defend the petition’s general reference to other injuries. She argued that Missouri’s code drew on the Federal Rules, under which discovery could supply information not fully set out in a complaint. The court treated both arguments as subordinate to the explicit state special-damages provision. It did not hold that federal and state procedure were identical in every respect, or supply a complete modern interpretation of Rules 8 and 9(g). Its conclusion was that those analogies did not permit this state court to ignore section 52. (Reproduced ¶¶11–12, reporter marker 802.)

The plaintiff argued that the defendant waived its objection by offering its own blood-pressure evidence. The court rejected that inference because the defendant had objected consistently and introduced responsive proof only after the trial court overruled its objections. Meeting an issue forced on a party by an adverse ruling was different from voluntarily inviting the error or relinquishing the objection. The opinion accordingly preserved the defendant’s appellate challenge. It also addressed the code’s prohibition on reversal for an error that did not materially affect the merits. The court held the erroneous admission prejudicial on this record; it did not announce that any defect in pleading necessarily requires reversal regardless of prejudice. (Reproduced ¶¶13–15, 19.)

The court did not reach a separate challenge to instruction 8 concerning the blood-pressure and shoulder injuries, because its evidentiary holding already resolved that problem. It did reject the contention that instructions 1 and 2 assumed disputed facts about which vehicle entered the intersection first. Those instructions conditioned their factual propositions on what the jury found and believed. Instruction 9 also expressly left factual determination to the jury. Repeating additional qualifications after every proposition would add clutter without improving clarity. Thus the opinion’s reversal coexisted with rejection of this distinct instructional challenge. (Reproduced ¶¶17–18.)

The original opinion concludes that the trial court’s admission of the unpleaded special-damage evidence over repeated objections constituted prejudicial error. It reverses the judgment and remands the cause. Anderson and Hughes concur without a separate reproduced writing. The recovered original opinion contains no judicial footnotes. Paragraph references in this brief identify the numbered reproduction, not official reporter paragraph numbers; its visible reporter markers independently locate the later discussion at 801–804. A complete reporter facsimile has not been read. (Reproduced ¶¶19–21, marker 804.)

The textbook then supplies edited material from a later motion for rehearing or transfer. In that excerpt the plaintiff argues that the Missouri special-damages language was copied from Federal Rule 9(g) and therefore should receive the federal construction. McCullen responds that neither the plaintiff nor the court has located an in-point decision on these facts and that the plain language does not permit pleading only general damages and recovering special damages. The textbook brackets the outcome as motion denied; the reproduced reporter header independently gives January 20, 1950 for denial of rehearing or transfer. Because the complete later writing is unrecovered, this brief does not certify that those selected passages exhaust its arguments, qualifications, authorities, or any notes. (Assigned physical 347–348, printed 661.)

The five attached textbook notes broaden the teaching discussion. They distinguish specific statement of damages from particularity in pleading fraud, give examples of general and additional consequential losses, pose damages-classification hypotheticals, and explain that special damage can sometimes be an element of the substantive claim rather than merely a limitation on proof. They also discuss the relationship between attorney-fee requests and Rule 54 motions. Those are editorial teaching materials, not additional holdings of Ziervogel. Their cited decisions are not certified as fully read by this brief. The entire supplied principal passage and all five notes were read in native text and original scans.
