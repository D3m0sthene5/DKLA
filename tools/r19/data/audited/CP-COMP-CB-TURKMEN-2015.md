# Turkmen v. Hasty

**789 F.3d 218 (2d Cir. 2015); United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit; June 17, 2015.** Assigned: CivPro PDF 338, printed 641–642, Note 4. This is the Second Circuit merits decision, distinct from the later rehearing proceeding and *Ziglar v. Abbasi* in the Supreme Court. Pinpoints identify pages of the preserved, separately paginated filed majority and Raggi opinions; they are not invented Federal Reporter page conversions.

## Facts and the path to this appeal

The eight named plaintiffs were men arrested on immigration charges during the investigation following September 11, 2001. They came from the Middle East, North Africa, or South Asia; six were Muslim, one Hindu, and one Buddhist. Six were held at Brooklyn's Metropolitan Detention Center (MDC); Ibrahim Turkmen and Akhil Sachdeva were held at the Passaic County Jail. Their individual detentions generally lasted approximately three to eight months. The appeal concerned alleged unconstitutional confinement conditions and discriminatory treatment, rather than an assertion that no immigration detention could lawfully occur. The court considered a complaint on dismissal motions: the allegations it accepted were not findings made after trial. (Majority 4–7, 9–25.)

The government initially adopted a policy of holding people arrested in the investigation until the FBI cleared them of terrorism connections. The New York FBI maintained its own detainee list, including people encountered through leads that generated no individualized suspicion of terrorism. The fourth amended complaint alleged that officials nevertheless subjected detainees to severe restrictions based on actual or perceived race, religion, and national origin. At the MDC, the Administrative Maximum Special Housing Unit imposed confinement for twenty-three hours daily, constant illumination, shackling and extensive escorts outside cells, severely restricted communication, and repeated strip searches. Separate allegations described physical attacks, religious insults, threats, and obstruction of complaints by guards. The distinction between official conditions and unofficial abuse was central to the decision. (Majority 9–16, 21–25.)

Individual allegations supplied concrete illustrations. The complaint alleged that Benamar Benatta received clearance on November 14, 2001, yet remained in the restrictive unit until April 30, 2002, with that clearance information available to the MDC. The court treated this as support for the claim that officials continued severe conditions after the premise for them had disappeared. It did not adjudicate the allegation's truth. (Majority 66–67.)

The defendants included former Attorney General John Ashcroft, FBI Director Robert Mueller, and INS Commissioner James Ziglar, collectively the DOJ defendants; and wardens Dennis Hasty and Michael Zenk and Associate Warden James Sherman, collectively the MDC defendants. Captain Salvatore Lopresti and Lieutenant Joseph Cuciti also appeared in the complaint. Cuciti did not appeal; Lopresti's appeal was dismissed after failures to pay a fee and file a brief. (Majority 1–4 & 4–5 n.2.)

Litigation began in 2002. Some earlier plaintiffs settled or withdrew, leaving Turkmen and Sachdeva; six MDC detainees subsequently intervened. After the Supreme Court's *Iqbal* decision, the case returned to the district court and the plaintiffs filed a fourth amended complaint. Two Justice Department Inspector General reports supplied information unavailable when the original complaint was written. The district court dismissed all claims against the DOJ defendants but allowed several against the MDC defendants. The MDC defendants appealed, and plaintiffs cross-appealed the DOJ dismissals following a Rule 54(b) judgment. The plaintiffs did not appeal dismissal of their interference-with-counsel claims, numbered 4 and 5. (Majority 5–9, 25–26 & n.13.)

## The questions and the parties' competing positions

The appeal required the court to distinguish three inquiries: whether a judicially implied constitutional damages remedy under *Bivens* existed, whether the complaint plausibly alleged each defendant's own constitutional misconduct, and whether qualified immunity barred suit despite the allegations. The MDC defendants disputed extending *Bivens* to a response involving national security and immigration. The officials also maintained that the pleadings insufficiently attributed misconduct to them and that legitimate security concerns explained restrictive confinement. Plaintiffs relied on detailed allegations about discriminatory selection, officials' knowledge, continued restrictions without a supporting connection to terrorism, and deliberate indifference to abuse. Their separate statutory claim alleged a discriminatory conspiracy under 42 U.S.C. § 1985(3). (Majority 26–38, 38–105.)

Under *Iqbal*, factual content must permit a reasonable inference of liability, not merely recite an element or describe conduct compatible with liability. A court assumes well-pleaded facts true and evaluates plausibility in context. Each official must violate the underlying right through his own conduct; supervisory rank alone is insufficient. But identifying conduct as deliberate indifference or supervisory involvement does not automatically make it forbidden vicarious liability. The question is whether that conduct satisfies the elements of the particular constitutional wrong. (Majority 26–28, 68–71.)

The Inspector General reports presented a related evidentiary problem. Plaintiffs incorporated them except where their allegations contradicted the reports. The court could consider their words and context, but it could not resolve disputed facts or competing explanations as though deciding after an evidentiary hearing. The reports helped support factual inferences; they did not transform every accusation into proven misconduct. (Majority 8–9 & n.6, 47–54.)

## Bivens: the majority's remedy analysis

Judges Pooler and Wesley treated the Fifth Amendment punitive-confinement and discrimination claims and the Fourth Amendment strip-search claim as applications of previously recognized remedies. Their analysis asked whether both the asserted right and the mechanism of injury had appeared in prior *Bivens* litigation. Although the emergency was extraordinary, they declined to let the defendants' asserted motivation redefine otherwise familiar conditions-of-confinement claims into a new context. Because they found an existing context, they expressly did not undertake the alternative-remedy and special-factors inquiries. (Majority 28–35, 36–38 & n.17.)

The First Amendment free-exercise claim received a different answer. The Supreme Court had not recognized that constitutional damages remedy, and the majority would not create the new remedy without its guidance. The free-exercise claim was therefore dismissed against all defendants, even though other claims concerning treatment in detention could proceed. This decision did not authorize damages actions under every constitutional provision. (Majority 35–36, 38, 108.)

## Punitive conditions: why some claims were plausible

Under *Bell v. Wolfish*, restrictions on a pretrial detainee cannot serve punishment. Where no express punitive intent is established, a restriction's lack of reasonable relationship to a legitimate objective can support an inference of punitive purpose. The majority did not impose an across-the-board requirement of individualized terrorism suspicion for every lawful detention restriction. It distinguished ordinary restrictions inherent in detention from the allegedly extreme conditions imposed without a supporting security connection. The parties used the pretrial-detainee standard; the court reserved whether civil immigration detainees deserved a more protective standard. (Majority 38–40 & 39 n.19, 54–62.)

For the DOJ defendants, the decisive allegations concerned officials' knowledge and the merger of the New York list into the national INS list. An initially lawful detention policy did not necessarily support an inference of unconstitutional intent, and high-level officials could initially expect lawful implementation. The majority nonetheless found a plausible inference that officials endorsed continued extreme treatment after learning that detainees lacked suspected terrorism connections. Its analysis focused on the November 2001 merger and supporting allegations about information moving among officials. It acknowledged that the particular merger theory was the court's reading of the complaint, rather than a theory plaintiffs had advanced in their briefs. (Majority 40–54 & 41 n.21.)

The majority considered the competing national-security explanation but concluded it did not require dismissal of these allegations. The inference depended on severe conditions, known lack of a terrorism nexus, and defendants' own alleged participation, rather than knowledge of misconduct alone. Discovery might later show that the high-level officials were not personally responsible. At this stage, the complaint plausibly connected them to punitive conditions after the merger. The majority also held the relevant prohibition sufficiently established to deny qualified immunity under the circuit's prior *Iqbal v. Hasty* reasoning. (Majority 54–62, 107.)

Hasty allegedly ordered subordinates to design the MDC conditions, which he and Sherman allegedly approved and implemented. Regular updates allegedly disclosed no terrorism evidence, required individualized assessments were omitted, and the officials approved a document falsely representing that individualized high-security assessments had occurred. Continued restrictions after clearance reinforced the inference. Those concrete allegations went beyond their titles and supported personal responsibility for the official conditions. General allegations against Zenk, who became warden on April 22, 2002, when only two plaintiffs remained in the unit, were insufficient. (Majority 62–68.)

The unofficial-abuse claim required deliberate indifference to a serious risk. Hasty allegedly obstructed grievances, avoided rounds to avoid seeing abuse, received notice through complaints, hunger strikes, and suicide attempts, and permitted abuse to continue. Those allegations supported his own deliberate indifference. By contrast, allegations of Sherman's awareness were too vague to connect him adequately to unofficial abuse. Zenk was not alleged to be responsible for that abuse. Thus Hasty faced both official-conditions and unofficial-abuse claims; Sherman faced only the former. The majority denied qualified immunity for the surviving claims. (Majority 62 n.32, 68–75, including 68–69 n.34.)

## Discrimination, searches, and conspiracy

Equal protection required purposeful discrimination, not merely awareness of consequences. The majority found the detailed allegations sufficient to connect the DOJ defendants' merger decision and Hasty and Sherman's treatment to allegedly discriminatory designations. It distinguished the amended complaint from the more conclusory allegations rejected in *Iqbal*. The fact that many members of the same ethnic or religious groups went to Passaic rather than the MDC did not compel dismissal: the officials allegedly deferred to others' discriminatory designations and placement decisions. Later evidence might change that conclusion. Zenk's allegations remained insufficient, and the Passaic plaintiffs did not adequately plead the challenged discriminatory confinement. The surviving equal-protection claims were not barred by qualified immunity. (Majority 75–95, particularly 82–89 & n.39.)

The strip-search claim challenged repeated searches even when detainees allegedly had no opportunity to obtain contraband. Hasty and Sherman allegedly created and implemented the restrictions; search logs supplied additional context rather than the sole basis for responsibility. Taking those allegations as true, the majority found a lack of rational relationship to a legitimate prison purpose under the standard the parties used and denied qualified immunity. It reserved whether civil detainees were entitled to a more protective standard. Zenk's later arrival and deficient allegations again required dismissal. (Majority 95–100 & nn.42–43.)

The § 1985(3) claim required an agreement to deprive equal protection, an act in furtherance, resulting injury or deprivation, and discriminatory animus. The alleged merger, continued treatment, and falsely represented assessments permitted an inference of coordinated conduct among the DOJ defendants, Hasty, and Sherman. The majority assumed defendants might ultimately invoke the intracorporate-conspiracy doctrine, which can treat a single entity's agents as incapable of conspiring. But the officials' varied responsibilities and separate policy creation prevented a determination on the pleadings that they constituted one policymaking entity. Acknowledging DOJ supervision over the prison system did not settle that factual and legal question. The majority also rejected qualified immunity under its earlier circuit precedent. Zenk and the Passaic plaintiffs' claims failed. (Majority 100–106, especially 103–105 & n.46.)

## Raggi's concurrence in part and dissent in part

Judge Raggi distinguished unofficial abuse from attacks on executive policy. Her disagreement did not concern the non-policy unofficial-abuse claims; she would dismiss the surviving policy challenges. First, she viewed the relevant *Bivens* context as the entire legal and factual setting, including immigration, national security, senior officials, and an unprecedented emergency. Combining a previously recognized right with a broadly described injury mechanism did not reproduce the settings of *Bivens*, *Davis*, or *Carlson*. Separation of powers, national-security responsibility, immigration authority, congressional legislation and inaction, and possible prospective relief counseled against a judicially created damages action. A non-damages alternative could matter without providing equivalent compensation. (Raggi 1–37, particularly 6–17, 21–37; 73 n.41.)

Second, she considered both plausibility and qualified immunity defective. The Inspector General report attributed the merger to Stuart Levey, not the named DOJ defendants; the majority's inference of consultation with Ashcroft was, in her view, speculation. Merging detention lists also did not itself establish knowledge or intent concerning the specific MDC restrictions. She regarded national security as the obvious, more likely explanation and argued that precedent had not put beyond debate a requirement of individualized terrorism suspicion before restrictive confinement of these lawfully arrested detainees. An abstract prohibition on punishment did not clearly resolve the particular emergency policy. (Raggi 38–69, particularly 43–58, 60–68.)

She similarly found discrimination implausibly inferred despite *Iqbal* and the less restrictive treatment of many Arab or Muslim detainees at Passaic. On searches, she disputed both alleged personal responsibility and the conclusion that repetitive searches necessarily lacked a rational security relationship. She specifically cautioned that a 2004 juvenile-search decision could not establish what officials knew in 2001. Because she would dismiss the equal-protection claim, she would dismiss the accompanying conspiracy claim too. Her proposed dismissal rested on remedy limits and, independently, qualified immunity; it was not a finding that the officials committed the alleged acts lawfully after trial. (Raggi 69–91, especially 85 n.46, 89 n.47, 90–91.)

## Judgment, later history, and the assignment

The court **affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded**. For the MDC plaintiffs, it reinstated the DOJ punitive-confinement, equal-protection, and conspiracy claims; allowed Hasty's official-conditions, unofficial-abuse, discrimination, search, and conspiracy claims; and allowed the corresponding Sherman claims except unofficial abuse. It dismissed free exercise against all defendants, dismissed all claims against Zenk, and affirmed dismissal of the Passaic plaintiffs' claims. Pooler and Wesley supplied the majority; Raggi concurred in part in the judgment and dissented as described above. There was no final damages award or merits verdict. (Majority 108–109; Raggi 90–91.)

The later Supreme Court judgment, independently read for the separate *Abbasi* brief, reversed the appellate judgment except for Hasty's prisoner-abuse claim, vacated that portion, and remanded. It rejected the Second Circuit's broad remedy analysis; it did not resolve the case merely by declaring all allegations implausible. Thus this brief explains the 2015 historical holding rather than presenting it as controlling current authorization of these damages claims. (*Abbasi*, Kennedy slip 14–26, 33.)
