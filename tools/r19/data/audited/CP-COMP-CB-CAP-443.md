# Ginsberg v. Williams

**270 Minn. 474, 135 N.W.2d 213 (1965).** Minnesota Supreme Court, March 26, 1965; additional per curiam opinion denying reargument May 21, 1965, at 270 Minn. 486–487. Civil Procedure. Assigned account: *CivPro.pdf*, physical pages 583–584, printed pages 1151–1152, together with both attached notes through printed page 1153. Pinpoints refer to the complete Minnesota Reports reproduction, including the later opinion.

The trial judge could grant a new trial on the broad grounds authorized by Minnesota's procedural rules, but could not create a free-standing ground called the interests of justice. The court declared the particular order void and prohibited its enforcement. A later explanatory opinion clarified the consequence: the plaintiff's underlying motion remained pending for proper disposition. The case therefore distinguishes invalidating an unexplained order from deciding that the plaintiff could never obtain a new trial. **483–487.**

## Facts and the route to review

Louis Ginsberg sued Carle DeWayne Williams on February 12, 1962, for damages from an October 30, 1959 collision in which Williams drove into the rear of Ginsberg's automobile. The issues at trial were sharply disputed. On June 10, 1963, the jury returned a defendant's verdict. Ginsberg moved the next day for judgment notwithstanding the verdict or a new trial. His stated new-trial grounds included insufficient evidence, a verdict contrary to the weight of the evidence, and an allegedly excessive emphasis on contributory negligence in the charge. **475 & n.1.**

Williams waived costs on June 17, and judgment was entered. After hearing the motion on June 19, the trial court entered its July 5 order denying judgment notwithstanding the verdict but granting a new trial in the interests of justice. The order did not identify one of the grounds in the motion or attach an explanatory memorandum. Although it did not expressly vacate the judgment, an order granting a new trial had that legal effect. Williams attempted an appeal. Ginsberg moved to dismiss it as nonappealable, and Williams also obtained an order requiring the trial court to show cause why prohibition should not prevent enforcement. **475, 477–480.**

This distinction between remedies was central. Williams conceded at oral argument that the new-trial order was not appealable under the recently amended civil appeal code. The court agreed. The statutory right to appeal did not extend to this order merely because its effect was to vacate an existing judgment. **475–479.**

## Why an ordinary appeal was unavailable

Before the 1963 revision, a statutory provision allowed appeals from orders involving the merits of an action. The courts had used that provision to permit review of orders vacating judgments, including new-trial orders entered after judgment. The separate restriction limiting appeals from new-trial orders to orders based exclusively on errors of law did not eliminate that alternative route. **476–478.**

The 1963 legislation retained the restriction concerning errors of law but omitted the provision that supplied the vacated-judgment route. It also declined proposals that would have restored an unrestricted appeal from a new-trial order or allowed discretionary appeals from other interlocutory orders. Whatever the reason for those legislative decisions, the court would not reconstruct an omitted statutory right. The revision took effect May 22, 1963, before this July order; a cited earlier case had involved an appeal taken before the revision and therefore did not establish a contrary result. **478–479; 478 n.7.**

The court distinguished statutory appeal rights from its constitutionally derived appellate jurisdiction. Its decision about the unavailable ordinary appeal did not mean it was powerless to examine whether the trial judge had exceeded lawful authority through an appropriate extraordinary remedy. **476 n.3, 479–480.**

## The limited function of prohibition

Prohibition was available to restrain a court from exceeding its legitimate powers even where that court had jurisdiction over the parties and subject matter, provided no speedy and adequate remedy existed. Here an appeal after another trial would not supply such a remedy. Williams challenged the judge's power to grant a new trial on the stated ground, rather than asking the Supreme Court to reweigh the evidence through prohibition. The court confined its review accordingly. **479–480 & n.10.**

Rule 59.01 supplied eight broad categories of new-trial grounds, including irregularities depriving a party of a fair trial, misconduct, unavoidable accident or surprise, newly discovered evidence, unavailable transcripts, improper damages, legal errors, and evidentiary insufficiency or a decision contrary to law. Other rules addressed inconsistent interrogatory answers and disability of the presiding judge. Their breadth did not create a separate permission to order a new trial whenever a judge was personally dissatisfied with the outcome. **480–481 & n.11.**

The court carefully examined older cases without treating their general statements as decisive holdings on the exact issue. Some decisions suggested exclusivity of statutory grounds; others concerned the permissible use of extraordinary remedies or a judge's initiative in ordering a new trial on an otherwise authorized ground. An earlier inherent-power decision was distinguishable because the judge had specified evidentiary insufficiency, and the later rule expressly governed new trials initiated by the court. **481–483.**

The court ultimately treated the enumerated grounds as limits on the trial court's power. Broad provisions already allowed correction of unfairness and unsupported verdicts. Adding an undefined interests-of-justice ground would invite subjective and potentially arbitrary action, made particularly difficult to control when immediate appellate review was unavailable. Saying substantial justice had not been done **because the evidence did not justify the verdict** identified an authorized ground; invoking justice without such a ground did not. The formal process for procedural rulemaking, including advisory consideration and opportunities for bench and bar input, also counseled against creating another ground by construction in this case. **483–485; 484 n.19.**

## Why the judge's later explanation did not cure the order

In responding to the show-cause order, the trial judge explained that the verdict was not justified by the evidence and that the interests-of-justice language expressed that conclusion. That explanation described a permissible ground under Rule 59.01(8). The Supreme Court nevertheless held that the return was not an amendment to the order or a memorandum incorporated into it. Treating a collateral response as curing the invalid order would distort the limited prohibition proceeding into a method for indirectly rewriting the challenged order. **485–486.**

The court noted that rules governing clerical mistakes and oversight provided possible routes to an actual correction, including correction during an appeal with the Supreme Court's permission. No such amendment had occurred. It therefore declared the order void and prohibited its enforcement. Murphy specially concurred in the result in a one-sentence writing; the report supplies no numerical vote to infer. **486; 486 nn.20–21.**

Importantly, note 21 acknowledged that the record did not show evidence manifestly and palpably favoring the verdict. A properly specified new trial for insufficient evidence would not have been an abuse of discretion. The decision did not endorse the merits of the defendant's verdict as the only permissible outcome. **486 n.21.**

## The separate May 21 disposition

On reargument, Ginsberg explained that counsel had orally requested a remand to permit amendment during the earlier proceedings. The request had not been acted upon or pursued. The per curiam opinion said that because the order was a nullity, the underlying motion had not been decided and remained pending before the trial court for whatever proper disposition that court deemed appropriate. It denied reargument. That clarification preserves an unresolved motion, rather than itself granting a new trial or permanently denying one. **486–487.**

## Assigned context and limits

The assignment quotes the central reasoning about the breadth of the authorized grounds and the danger of an undefined justice standard. Its first attached note contrasts *Coppo*'s Washington account of inherent new-trial authority, including its quotation about the trial judge's superior ability to assess witnesses. That is an assigned comparative account, not the Minnesota holding. The second asks whether several individually insufficient errors could together deprive a party of a fair trial. It is an editorial question; *Ginsberg*'s recognition of the broad fair-trial ground supplies context without deciding that hypothetical.
