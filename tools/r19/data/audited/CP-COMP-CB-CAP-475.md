# Winter v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.

**555 U.S. 7 (2008) · Supreme Court of the United States · November 12, 2008 · Civil Procedure**

The Court reversed the Ninth Circuit and vacated the challenged portions of a preliminary injunction restricting Navy sonar training. A preliminary-injunction applicant must show likely irreparable harm, not merely a possibility, and must satisfy the requirements concerning likely merits success, the balance of equities, and the public interest. In this case the balance and public interest independently required removal of the two challenged restrictions, even assuming the plaintiffs had shown irreparable injury and could prevail on the underlying environmental claim. ## Concrete conflict: Navy training and marine mammals

The Navy conducts integrated exercises in which ships, submarines, and aircraft operate together as a strike group. Detecting quiet diesel-electric submarines is a critical task. Active sonar emits sound and analyzes returning echoes, while passive sonar listens without introducing sound. Because quiet submarines can evade passive detection, the Navy regarded training with mid-frequency active sonar as essential to certifying strike groups for deployment. Operators needed experience with changing ocean conditions, coordination, interference, and tactical demands. **555 U.S. at 12–14 & n.1.**

The waters off Southern California were particularly useful for these exercises but also contained at least thirty-seven species of marine mammals. Environmental organizations and individuals maintained that sonar could cause serious injuries and behavioral disruptions, citing scientific material and strandings elsewhere. The Navy disputed the asserted harm and emphasized its forty years of regional training without a documented sonar-related mammal injury. These were competing positions; the Court did not adjudicate a universal scientific conclusion that sonar is harmless. **Id. at 14–15.**

A 2007 national-defense exemption from the Marine Mammal Protection Act required mitigation measures, including lookouts, graduated power reductions as mammals approached, and complete sonar shutdown within 200 yards. The Navy separately prepared an environmental assessment for fourteen exercises scheduled through January 2009 and found no significant environmental impact, rather than completing an environmental impact statement before the exercises. Its modeling and classification of potential harms became disputed features of the record. **Id. at 15–16.**

## The injunction and executive actions

The plaintiffs sued under NEPA and other environmental laws. The district court initially prohibited active-sonar use. The Ninth Circuit found that remedy overbroad and directed a narrower injunction permitting training with mitigation. On remand, the district judge imposed six additional restrictions. The Navy's Supreme Court challenge concerned two: shutdown when a mammal was detected within 2,200 yards, and a six-decibel power reduction during significant surface ducting, an ocean condition affecting sound propagation. Four other mitigation restrictions were not challenged in the review the majority decided. **Id. at 17–19 & n.2.**

The Navy also sought executive relief. The President granted a statutory exemption from Coastal Zone Management Act requirements. The Council on Environmental Quality approved alternative NEPA arrangements on a finding of emergency circumstances, allowing training under the Navy's existing mitigation measures with added notice, research, and reporting obligations. The district court refused to dissolve the challenged restrictions, and the Ninth Circuit affirmed. It questioned the validity of the emergency arrangements, found likely success on the NEPA claim, and treated a possibility of irreparable injury as sufficient where merits prospects were strong. **Id. at 18–20 & nn.3–4.**

The Navy argued both that the environmental merits favored it and that the restrictions threatened realistic training and readiness. Plaintiffs maintained that the record met even a stricter harm standard. **Id. at 20–22.**

## Four requirements, and why harm alone was insufficient

Chief Justice Roberts stated four requirements: likely success on the merits, likely irreparable harm without preliminary relief, a favorable balance of equities, and consistency with the public interest. A preliminary injunction is extraordinary relief rather than an entitlement that follows automatically from a plausible legal claim. Mere possibility of harm is too permissive. **Id. at 20–22.**

The Court did not simply find that the plaintiffs had proved no environmental danger. It acknowledged uncertainty about whether the Ninth Circuit's incorrect verbal standard affected its analysis: that court had also affirmed the district court's finding of near-certain harm. Moreover, the lower court had not reassessed the residual harm after taking account of the four unchallenged mitigation restrictions. The majority held that even a showing of irreparable environmental injury would not overcome the balance and public interest on this record. It therefore did not resolve likely merits success. **Id. at 22–24.**

NEPA's procedural character was also relevant. It requires informed environmental consideration rather than a particular substantive outcome. The majority noted the existing detailed assessment and history of training, while leaving the disputed obligation to prepare an EIS and the executive arrangements' validity undecided. It did not declare that an assessment always substitutes for a required EIS. **Id. at 23–24, 31 & n.5.**

## Why the two restrictions upset the equitable balance

The majority credited detailed declarations from senior Navy officers concerning the effects on realistic training. Courts must consider military judgments with appropriate deference, especially their predictive assessments of readiness, but military interests do not invariably prevail. Roberts expressly rejected that categorical proposition. The plaintiffs' scientific, recreational, and ecological interests were serious; the Navy's demonstrated need to train crews effectively nevertheless outweighed the asserted harms in this case. **Id. at 24–27.**

The 2,200-yard shutdown radius increased the covered surface area more than one hundredfold compared with the existing 200-yard shutdown requirement. A single interruption could waste a training scenario that had taken days to develop. Prior voluntary shutdowns at longer distances did not establish that compulsory shutdown during a critical tactical phase would be manageable; the record supported the Navy's explanation that those earlier shutdowns occurred at tactically insignificant times. The majority also distinguished the different operational characteristics of low-frequency sonar rather than treating its larger shutdown zone as controlling. **Id. at 27–29.**

Surface ducting made realistic training especially important because submarines could exploit the way sound traveled. Rarity did not make a restriction harmless: it made opportunities to train under the condition harder to replace. A six-decibel reduction represented a seventy-five-percent power reduction and changed the interference conditions operators needed to experience. The Navy's willingness to accept other mitigation measures did not demonstrate that these additional restraints were equally tolerable. Nor did it have to wait for actual training failure before seeking relief from a predictably harmful order. **Id. at 29–31.**

The same balance would preclude a permanent injunction imposing these restrictions on the record described. Even success on the environmental merits would not require that remedy automatically. A court could consider declaratory relief or relief tailored to preparation of an EIS rather than restrictions credibly threatening readiness. The Court did not determine the underlying NEPA violation. **Id. at 31–33 & n.5.**

## Separate opinions and exact disposition

Roberts's Court opinion was joined by Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, and Alito. Justice Breyer, joined by Stevens in Part I of his opinion only, also concluded that the challenged injunction lacked adequate support: uncertainty about residual environmental harm and insufficient responses to the Navy's detailed evidence undermined the lower courts' balance. His analysis nevertheless stressed the injury to NEPA's purposes when action precedes informed review. **Id. at 34–41.**

Breyer's Part II, not joined by Stevens, would retain the modified mitigation conditions imposed in the Ninth Circuit's temporary stay until an acceptable EIS was complete. He regarded them as the best workable interim balance given the short time before the exercises ended. The majority rejected that solution because the stay ceased to govern once the underlying injunction appeal was resolved; unexamined stay conditions could not become a new preliminary injunction. **Id. at 42–43; majority at 31–32 n.5.** Thus agreement on vacating the district court's two restrictions must be distinguished from agreement with the majority's complete remedial approach.

Justice Ginsburg, joined by Souter, would affirm. She emphasized NEPA's requirement of environmental review before action and denied that CEQ could erase the statutory obligation. Her account defended the district court's revised balancing and stressed the predicted injury, difficulty detecting harm to beaked whales, and proposed increase in training intensity. She also defended equitable flexibility and a sliding-scale approach, while agreeing that remote hypothetical injury alone does not warrant relief. **Id. at 43–54 & nn.1–4.** These were dissenting explanations, not merits holdings adopted by the Court.

The exact mandate **reversed the Ninth Circuit and vacated the preliminary injunction only to the extent challenged by the Navy**. It did not eliminate every mitigation condition or resolve the legality of the executive alternative arrangements. **Id. at 33.**

## Assigned-source qualification

The textbook accurately states the four factors and the rejection of a mere possibility of irreparable injury, but its short explanation should not be read as a final finding that plaintiffs lacked likely injury or that the Navy prevailed on NEPA compliance. The actual decision rests independently on equities and public interest and expressly reserves the environmental merits. The assignment's extraordinary-remedy citation transposes the reporter numbers; the United States Reports passage is **555 U.S. at 22**, not page 376. **Assigned physical 602, printed 1190.** Its discussion of a later appellate sliding-scale dispute is editorial context, not a later-law holding of Winter.
