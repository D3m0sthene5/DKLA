# Stadin v. Union Electric Co.

**309 F.2d 912 (8th Cir. 1962) — United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit, November 15, 1962.** Judge Blackmun wrote for a panel comprising Judges Sanborn, Blackmun, and District Judge Register. No separate opinion is reported. Civil Procedure: intervention of right, permissive intervention, and a minority shareholder's proposed derivative claims. Assigned account: CivPro, physical PDF 412, printed 792. The case applies the historical versions of Rules 24 and 23, not their current formulations.

## Facts and proposed intervention

Union Electric generated and supplied electricity. Following criminal antitrust judgments entered in Philadelphia in February 1961, Union sued electrical-equipment manufacturers for treble damages under the Clayton Act. Its December 1961 action against Westinghouse and General Electric concerned turbine-generator overcharges. A January 1962 action, joined by two other utilities, challenged meter overcharges against those companies and Sangamo. The suppliers had entered guilty or no-contest pleas in the criminal proceedings. The civil complaints sought recovery for alleged overcharges; this intervention decision did not determine the amounts owed. **914–915.**

Harry Stadin held forty-three Union common shares out of more than eleven million outstanding. Before Union filed the civil suits, he proposed a shareholder resolution directing pursuit of the company's rights against convicted suppliers. Management omitted it from proxy materials on the stated ground that it was not a proper subject of shareholder action under Missouri law. Stadin presented it at the annual meeting, where it lost overwhelmingly. Four days later he threatened a derivative suit unless Union pursued its remedies. Union eventually sued concerning eight equipment categories, while purchases in eleven other categories remained under investigation. **915–916 & n.4.**

Stadin sought to intervene in the two pending cases but not to add damages concerning the turbine-generators or meters already at issue. His proposed complaints incorporated Union's allegations and demanded recovery for the additional equipment categories. He argued that management's earlier resistance showed bad faith and condonation of price-fixing; that Union's general counsel, also a director, was not friendly with him and had other disputed dealings; and that litigation organized by defendants rather than equipment categories would be more efficient. **915–918 & n.5.**

Union supplied affidavits describing ongoing investigation of thousands of items and purchases from multiple corporations. Its counsel disputed the asserted personal hostility and explained that the SEC had not objected to omission of the proposed resolution from proxy materials. Stadin offered his own affidavits and testified that his allegations were true. The district court denied intervention. Its final order consolidated the cases for appeal and made a Rule 54(b) determination; an earlier request for discretionary §1292(b) review had been denied without prejudice to other appellate rights. **914 n.1; 916–917.**

## The pleading inquiry

For intervention of right, well-pleaded allegations ordinarily received an assumption of truth absent sham or frivolity. But the court distinguished concrete allegations from conclusory descriptions such as bad faith. It could also consider the opponents' uncontroverted, material facts. Accepting pleaded facts did not require adopting every legal inference Stadin drew from them. The appellate court therefore examined what his proposed claims actually added and whether their asserted connection to the existing cases satisfied the rule. **917.**

## Intervention of right: two independent deficiencies

Historical Rule 24(a)(2) required both potentially inadequate representation and that the applicant was or might be bound by a judgment in the action. Stadin could not show the necessary binding effect. The existing turbine-generator and meter claims involved different equipment and factual circumstances from his proposed additional claims. His central concern was that adverse decisions would make separate litigation harder through precedent. The court recognized that the rule had been interpreted realistically rather than as a rigid res judicata formula, but relied on *Sam Fox* to explain that practical difficulty or comity was not equivalent to the requisite legal binding effect. **914 n.2; 917–918.**

Nor did the record show possible inadequate representation. Inadequacy could arise through collusion, an adverse interest, or failure to perform the representative's duty. Stadin alleged no collusion with suppliers. Management's earlier vote did not establish a refusal to bring the very cases now pending, whose complaints counsel had signed. Counsel's alleged lack of personal friendliness, the separate stock-rights dispute, and disagreement over how to structure lawsuits did not establish an interest of Union adverse to Stadin's shareholder interest. It was unrealistic to demand that every corporate attorney be on affirmatively friendly terms with every shareholder, and if a mere difference of opinion among attorneys were inadequate representation, intervention of right would become almost automatic. **919.**

The opinion's qualification is important: it did not say conflicting interests or attorney hostility can never matter. It distinguished *Lane Lifeboat*, where a potential indemnitor's exposure to an existing suit's judgment supplied the central intervention basis. That case did not make personal unfriendliness alone dispositive. Here Union was pursuing the existing claims and investigating others. It needed a reasonable opportunity to act before its failure of duty could be inferred. **919–920.**

## Permissive intervention and the derivative theory

Common questions did not compel permissive intervention under historical Rule 24(b)(2). The court had to consider delay and prejudice. Adding numerous equipment categories would introduce additional witnesses, discovery, pretrial work, longer trial, and confusion into two already complicated suits. The appellate court found no abuse of discretion in refusing that expansion. **915 n.3; 920–921.**

Stadin also characterized his applications as derivative proceedings under then-Rule 23(b). The court assumed without deciding that a derivative suit could be instituted through intervention, but found its requirements unmet. A demand to take appropriate steps could include investigation, negotiation, settlement, or a justified decision not to sue, rather than immediately filing every conceivable claim. Subsequent suits in eight categories and continued investigation of the rest did not show an unwarranted refusal or breach of trust. Corporate litigation decisions remained within management's discretion absent the necessary misconduct. **921–922.**

The *Rogers* precedent did not change that conclusion. It involved alleged conspiratorial control of a captive corporation by directors and its supplier. Stadin alleged no comparable conspiracy. Management's concern that widespread suits could weaken suppliers and industry research did not itself establish a scheme to protect price-fixers at Union's expense. The court found fair assurance, on this record and at this time, that Union's interests were being protected. It did not permanently foreclose a shareholder remedy upon a different showing. **922.**

## Exact judgment and significance

The court **affirmed the order insofar as it denied intervention of right and dismissed the appeal insofar as it concerned permissive intervention**. Its discussion found no abuse of discretion, but the reported mandate uses dismissal for that part; those formulations should not be collapsed. A later rehearing-denial notation is reported without a separate developed opinion. **922; caption 912.**

The assigned parenthetical accurately isolates the adequacy point: disagreement over conducting litigation, without a showing of failure of duty to shareholders, did not create a right to intervene. The full opinion adds the separate binding-effect requirement, the proposed expansion to different equipment claims, the permissive-intervention analysis, and the derivative-action deficiencies. Its legal-binding discussion belongs to the 1962 rule and should not be substituted uncritically for a modern Rule 24 test.
