# Fisher v. Petr Konchalovsky Foundation
2016 WL 1047394 (S.D.N.Y. Mar. 10, 2016) | Civil Procedure

## Decision and assigned account

District Judge Alison J. Nathan granted permission to serve the Moscow-based Foundation by email and denied the earlier request for international-mail service. This was a procedural order in 15-cv-9831 (AJN), rather than an adjudication of a painting's authenticity or the Foundation's liability. The assigned account appears in Civil Procedure PDF page 158, printed page 267, Note 4. The complete actual order has five pages and one judicial footnote; there is no separate writing. Pinpoints below refer to the original numbered order pages, rather than assuming a mapping to Westlaw star pages. [Complete judicial order](https://lettersblogatory.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/file0.856524058888805.pdf).

## The painting dispute and unsuccessful postal proposal

Richard Y. Fisher owned a painting he claimed was Petr Petrovich Konchalovsky's 1920 oil painting, *Still Life with Grinder*. Fisher alleged that the Foundation had wrongfully declared it inauthentic. His December 17, 2015 action sought relief concerning that alleged declaration. The service order recites these allegations without deciding whether the painting was authentic, whether the Foundation's opinion was wrongful, or whether Fisher was entitled to damages. Page 1.

Fisher initially sought permission on January 14, 2016 to serve the Foundation through United States Post Office International Mail. An attorney experienced in international process submitted an affidavit identifying the Foundation as a Russian corporation based in Moscow and explaining that Russia no longer effected service through its Hague Convention Central Authority. The opinion also cited Russia's suspension of civil and commercial judicial cooperation with the United States in 2003. Page 2.

That practical obstacle did not persuade the court to disregard Russia's objection to Article 10 postal-channel service. The court had denied the postal request and required Fisher either to explain why it should depart from the district's contrary authority or to propose another method. The underlying distinction was important: a country's failure to execute Convention requests did not itself establish that the country consented to service by mail. Fisher therefore proposed email on January 28. Page 2.

## Evidence that the proposed email address actually functioned

Fisher's attorney, David Steiner, enlisted Paris art dealer Valère Lamblot to contact the Foundation. Lamblot emailed an authentication inquiry about a purported Konchalovsky painting. Alexander Konov, whom Fisher's affidavit identified as the Foundation's director, replied the same day from konov@pkonchalovsky.com. Konov recommended art experts in Geneva or Moscow and provided their institutes' websites. Steiner later emailed Konov himself but received no response. Pages 2–3.

The order therefore did not rest on a finding that Konov had answered counsel, acknowledged the lawsuit, or agreed to accept process. It rested on evidence of a recent response to the dealer's substantive authentication inquiry. The court considered both who used the address and how its use related to the Foundation's work. Pages 3–4.

## Two requirements for court-directed alternative service

Rule 4(f)(3) permitted a court to order another method of foreign service if international agreement did not prohibit it. Footnote 1 explains why that rule applied to this corporate defendant: Rule 4(h)(2) incorporated the Rule 4(f) methods for corporations abroad, with its exception for personal delivery under Rule 4(f)(2)(C)(i). Page 1 & n.1.

The court applied two requirements: compatibility with international agreements and constitutional due process. It followed decisions interpreting an objection to Article 10's enumerated methods as not expressly rejecting email. It acknowledged *Agha v. Jacobs*, which treated Article 10's postal-channel language as potentially encompassing email, but adopted the contrary interpretation. Russia had not explicitly objected to electronic service, and the court was unaware of another international agreement or Russian law prohibiting it. This describes the court's 2016 interpretation and record, rather than a verification of present treaty positions. Page 3.

Due process required a method reasonably calculated, under the circumstances, to inform the defendant of the action and permit objections. Identifying an email address was not enough by itself. The movant had to supply facts indicating that process sent there would likely reach the defendant. Page 4.

## Why this evidence satisfied due process

Konov's prompt reply within the preceding two months established recent use of the address by the person identified as the Foundation's director. The subject of the exchange supplied an additional connection: authentication of a Konchalovsky painting was the very type of activity implicated by Fisher's suit. Those circumstances supported using this address to notify the Foundation of litigation about its authentication work. The court distinguished business-related communication channels from informal addresses used only to accept requests for information. Page 4.

The opinion contrasted records containing obvious evidentiary gaps. In *Fortunato*, a Facebook profile's listed address lacked facts showing it was operational and accessed. In the SEC's *China Intelligent Lighting* service application, the record neither adequately linked the proposed addresses to the individuals nor established their continued use. Here, the director's recent, substantive response supplied the missing connection. The court's reasoning did not depend on certainty that the Foundation would respond to process; it depended on a supported expectation that the proposed channel would reach it. Pages 4–5.

## Exact disposition and significance

The court granted Fisher's request to serve the Foundation by email, denied international-mail service, and resolved Docket Nos. 13 and 16. It did not enter default, award damages, decide the painting's authenticity, or establish liability. Page 5.

The assigned parenthetical accurately reports that no international agreement barred email service, that Russia had not objected to it, and that Fisher supplied sufficient evidence that email to the designated address was likely to reach the defendant. The full order adds the failed postal proposal, the recent use of the address, director identification, and the authentication-related substance of the exchange. Together, those details show why this was a fact-supported alternative-service authorization rather than a general rule that foreign process may always be emailed. Assigned printed 267; order pages 2–5.
