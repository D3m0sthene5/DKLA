# Deyo v. Internal Revenue Service

**2002 WL 1482517, at *1 (D. Conn. 2002). Civil Procedure.** Assigned account: CivPro physical 340, printed 646, Note 1 under Form of Pleadings. This is a limited assigned-account brief. The complete 2002 judicial opinion and original order have not been recovered; the available evidence consists of the whole textbook account and a later filed court order said to describe Deyo’s Rule 8 disposition; the saved copy of that order is a failed download containing none of its text, so what it says could not be independently verified. No complete-primary-source accuracy certification is made.

The textbook describes a 43-page complaint that included reproductions of dialogue from telephone conversations. It says the district court conditionally dismissed the complaint because its presentation was neither short nor plain. That supplies a concrete problem of pleading form, rather than an adjudicated account of the underlying dispute with the Internal Revenue Service. The assignment does not state the tax transaction, substantive causes of action, requested amount, named judge, exact amendment deadline, or ultimate outcome after the conditional dismissal. Those details cannot be supplied from the short account without the actual opinion. (Assigned printed 646.)

The point of the assignment is that a pleading can fail to communicate a claim clearly even when it supplies abundant information. The surrounding note contrasts Deyo with Hearns and Swanson, where including substantial or extraneous material did not itself justify the challenged result. It then refers to the difficulty of selecting relevant allegations from excessive verbiage. That is the editors’ comparison; it does not establish that Deyo announced an automatic maximum number of pages or held every lengthy complaint defective. The assignment also asks about mechanisms for striking redundant allegations. It does not reproduce a Deyo ruling under Rule 12(f). (Assigned printed 646.)

The statements in this paragraph could not be independently verified: the saved file for the later order records a failed download and contains none of the order’s text. A later primary judicial source is reported to supply a narrow procedural cross-check. In the Initial Review Order in Alston v. Lindsey, the District of Connecticut cites Deyo at *1, dates it April 25, 2002, and characterizes it as dismissing a pro se complaint without prejudice for failure to comply with Rule 8(a)(2). The same cited passage explains that liberal construction of a pro se litigant’s pleading does not dispense with compliance with court rules. That is an actual later court’s description of the earlier decision, not recovery of Deyo’s full reasoning. The later order’s own allegations, constitutional-claim analysis, amendment timetable, and disposition belong to Alston and are not attributed to Deyo. ([Alston filed order, judicial page 7](https://docs.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/connecticut/ctdce/3%3A2015cv01673/110247/6).)

The supported result is therefore a conditional pleading disposition (described as without prejudice, and as involving a pro se complaint, only in the unverified later order), not a final rejection on the merits of the plaintiff’s underlying claim. All supplied textbook material was read in native text and the original scan. Full facts, complete procedure, all judicial authorities and footnotes, exact amendment conditions, and any separate writing remain unknown until the actual 2002 order is recovered.
