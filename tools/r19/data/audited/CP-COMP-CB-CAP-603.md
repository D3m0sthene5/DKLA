# Delta Air Lines, Inc. v. August

**450 U.S. 346 (1981) — Supreme Court of the United States, March 9, 1981.** Justice Stevens wrote for five Justices, joined by Brennan, White, Marshall, and Blackmun. Justice Powell concurred in the result on a different ground. Justice Rehnquist dissented, joined by Chief Justice Burger and Justice Stewart. The judgment was 6–3; the Court’s construction of Rule 68 had five votes. Civil Procedure: offers of judgment, prevailing defendants, and the relationship between Rules 68 and 54(d). Assigned source: *CivPro.pdf*, physical page 708, printed 1407–1408.

## Claim, settlement offer, and procedural history

Rosemary August alleged that Delta discharged her from her flight-attendant position solely because of her race, in violation of Title VII. She requested reinstatement, approximately $20,000 in backpay, attorney’s fees, and costs. A few months after the complaint, Delta formally offered to allow judgment for $450, including attorney’s fees, together with accrued costs. The offer disclaimed an admission of liability or damage. August rejected it, the case proceeded to trial, and Delta won. (348–349, nn.1–2.)

The district court directed each party to bear its own costs. Delta moved to modify the judgment, asserting that Rule 68 required August to pay Delta’s costs incurred after the rejected offer. The court denied that motion because it considered the $450 proposal neither a good-faith settlement attempt nor at least arguably reasonable. Its ruling did not declare August’s claim wholly specious merely because she lost. The Seventh Circuit affirmed, requiring an offer sufficient to justify serious consideration in the circumstances. (349–350, nn.3–4.)

The Supreme Court affirmed the outcome but used a threshold ground the lower courts had not addressed: Rule 68 did not apply when the defendant, rather than the plaintiff-offeree, obtained the judgment. The Court did not revisit the racial-discrimination merits. Nor did it decide whether the district court abused its separate discretion under Rule 54(d), because Delta had not raised that issue in the court of appeals. (350; 362.)

## Issue and holding

Under the then-existing Rule 68, a defending party could make a formal offer of judgment. If the offer was rejected and the judgment finally obtained by the offeree was not more favorable, the offeree had to pay postoffer costs. The question was whether that formulation encompassed a judgment entirely against the plaintiff-offeree. Stevens held that it did not. A defense judgment remains subject to Rule 54(d)’s usual presumption and discretion concerning prevailing-party costs rather than Rule 68’s mandatory mechanism. (348, n.1; 350–352.)

The opinion’s plaintiff/defendant terminology was shorthand for this single-claim case. Note 5 recognizes that a party defending a counterclaim or crossclaim can also be a defending party for purposes of the rule. Nothing in this case determined the amount or particular items of Delta’s cost request; note 6 expressly identifies that limitation. (350, nn.5–6.)

## Textual reasoning

Stevens distinguished three possible judgments: one for the defendant, one for the plaintiff less favorable than the offer, and one for the plaintiff more favorable than the offer. The last plainly falls outside Rule 68. The second plainly involves a judgment obtained by the offeree. The first is a judgment for the other party, which the majority considered outside the ordinary meaning of that phrase. (351–352.)

Other language supported the distinction. An accepted offer produces a judgment taken against the offering defendant; a judgment obtained by the plaintiff was correspondingly understood as one favorable to the plaintiff. Rule 68 therefore imposed consequences on a plaintiff’s insufficient recovery, not on the complete absence of a favorable judgment. The Court affirmed on that construction without adopting the lower courts’ reasonable-offer prerequisite. (350–352; 355.)

## Purpose and Rule 54(d)

Both sides already face incentives to settle because defeat ordinarily carries adverse consequences, including costs. Rule 68 adds an incentive where the plaintiff probably will recover but the recovery’s amount remains uncertain. A plaintiff who rejects a sufficiently favorable offer can lose benefits normally associated with victory. Applying the same mechanism to a losing plaintiff would ordinarily add little because costs normally follow defeat anyway. (352, nn.8–9.)

Delta identified an additional advantage: a rejected offer could eliminate the district court’s ability under Rule 54(d) to deny costs to a prevailing defendant. The majority objected that Delta’s construction would let a nominal offer accomplish that change regardless of whether it seriously promoted settlement. Delta even argued that a one-penny offer could trigger the mechanism if it won. Stevens considered it implausible that the rules granted judges discretion and simultaneously let defendants alone extinguish it through a token act. (353–355, nn.10–14.)

The majority acknowledged the apparent asymmetry that a defendant winning outright might fare worse concerning costs than one losing for a small amount. Note 12 explains why that concern arises only within a limited set of cases, involving discretionary denial of costs and the relationship between the offer, judgment, and recoverable costs. It did not regard that possibility as a sufficient reason to disregard the rule’s text. Note 16 also observes that a reasonable offer could influence a judge’s discretionary cost determination even where Rule 68 did not compel an award. (353–356, nn.12–16.)

On the majority’s interpretation, sham offers gain nothing. The rule encourages meaningful offers in cases presenting genuine recovery risk, and judicial discretion survives at both extremes: a defense judgment and a plaintiff’s judgment exceeding the offer. The Court therefore found no need to insert a judicial reasonableness requirement into Rule 68’s operative language. (354–356.)

## Historical support

The Court examined the state statutes identified by the original Advisory Committee, the related state cost provisions, decisions applying those procedures, commentary, and committee explanations. Minnesota, Montana, and New York already required ordinary costs for prevailing defendants. Their offer provisions served a separate function concerning plaintiffs who recovered less than the rejected offer. The majority treated that structure as support for limiting the federal rule to plaintiffs obtaining a favorable judgment. (356–358, nn.17–23.)

Committee discussions and later amendment notes described stopping the running of the defendant’s exposure to a recovering plaintiff’s costs. The limited historical use of nominal federal offers by defendants who later prevailed also supported the majority’s reading. The extensive authorities in notes 23–27 are historical support, not additional judgments being adjudicated in August’s case. (359–362, nn.24–27.)

## Powell’s different reason for affirmance

Powell rejected the Court’s exclusion of defense judgments and agreed with much of Rehnquist’s dissent. He nevertheless found Delta’s particular offer defective. In his view, Title VII’s attorney’s-fee provision formed part of the accrued costs that a proper offer had to preserve. The offer should specify substantive relief while permitting the plaintiff, upon acceptance, to obtain a reasonable accrued fee determined by the court or agreed afterward. Delta instead capped substantive relief and attorney’s fees together at $450. (362–366, concurrence nn.1–4.)

Powell distinguished the interpretation of costs in *Roadway Express* because that case concerned statutory sanctions against opposing counsel rather than the settlement mechanism here. He also preserved the different standards for prevailing plaintiffs and defendants under Title VII. His note 5 rejects the implication that his interpretation automatically gives a prevailing defendant attorney’s fees; a defendant’s entitlement still depended on the governing standard concerning frivolous or unfounded litigation. These fee conclusions are Powell’s separate reasoning, not the five-Justice Court’s basis for decision. (364, n.2; 366, n.5.)

## Rehnquist’s dissent

Rehnquist agreed that the lower courts’ good-faith or reasonableness requirements were absent from Rule 68 but rejected the majority’s substitute rationale. Rule 54(a) defined judgment broadly enough to include an appealable take-nothing judgment. The original rule referred to failure to obtain a more favorable judgment, and he found no relevant narrowing purpose in the amendment. He also disputed the majority’s inference from the state statutes and its reading of commentary. (366–375, dissent nn.1–3.)

For the dissent, Rule 54(d) expressly allowed other rules to displace its discretion, and Rule 68’s mandatory language did exactly that. Making a plaintiff who recovered nothing better situated than one who recovered too little was the principal practical objection. Rehnquist also rejected Powell’s fee rationale: he construed Rule 68 costs by their traditional meaning, considered fees subject to compromise, and feared that an open-ended fee obligation would discourage offers. His final note develops that settlement-certainty concern. He would not sustain the denial of Delta’s asserted Rule 68 entitlement. (374–380, dissent nn.4–5.)

## Judgment and assigned comparison

The Court **affirmed the Seventh Circuit’s judgment**, leaving the district court’s each-side-pays-own-costs disposition intact. Its ruling rested on the absence of a judgment obtained by the plaintiff, not a finding that every $450 offer is invalid, a special Title VII exemption, or approval of the lower courts’ reasonableness test. The unpreserved Rule 54(d) abuse-of-discretion claim remained outside the decision. (350–352; 362.)
