# Wallace B. Roderick Revocable Living Trust v. XTO Energy, Inc.

**725 F.3d 1213 (10th Cir. 2013) · Tenth Circuit · July 9, 2013 · No. 12–3176**

**Civil Procedure — commonality, predominance, and contract differences in royalty class actions.** Assigned: CivPro.pdf, physical page 426, printed page 823, the royalty-owner account in Note 2 before Note 3. Pinpoints below refer to the numbered pages of the preserved court-issued opinion; no unverified reporter-page conversions are supplied.

## Concrete dispute and proceedings

XTO produced natural gas and constituent products from wells in Kansas. Thousands of royalty owners claimed that it underpaid them by deducting expenses that Kansas law required the producer to bear. Their representative, the Wallace B. Roderick Revocable Living Trust, held royalty interests in eight wells under five leases. The proposed class covered roughly 650 leases and more than 300 wells across ten fields, with Kansas production from January 1, 1999 onward. Initially proposed Colorado claims had been dropped, and Oklahoma claims had been severed and transferred to separate litigation. The Kansas action alleged breach of contract, unjust enrichment, and sought an accounting. (2–3 and n.1.)

The legal theory depended on Kansas’s implied duty of marketability. Unless the contract provides otherwise, a gas lessee bears the costs of gathering, compression, dehydration, treatment, and processing necessary to put gas into marketable condition. The Trust maintained that gas from every class well required one or more of those services. XTO disputed that proposition and said some gas was sold directly into interstate pipelines without processing. Neither XTO nor an affiliate owned or operated a gathering system or processing plant pertaining to these class wells; marketing arrangements with third parties supplied the relevant services and payment terms. (3–4 and n.2.)

XTO calculated royalties through its Avatar accounting system. The system did not adjust a royalty owner’s payment according to that owner’s lease language. Instead, payment depended on XTO’s marketing contract with the gas purchaser. The Trust argued that this uniform calculation improperly passed marketability expenses to the owners. XTO responded that leases differed and that the point at which gas became marketable could differ from well to well. Its expert grouped a sample of one-fifth of the leases into twenty categories of royalty provisions, some allegedly eliminating the implied duty altogether or authorizing particular deductions. These were contested positions, not appellate findings that every lease negated the duty or that all deductions were lawful. (4–5 and n.3.)

The district court certified under Rule 23(b)(3), relying on one asserted common issue: whether XTO’s uniform payment methodology breached the implied duty of marketability. It did not address the Trust’s other proposed common claims. The Tenth Circuit permitted a Rule 23(f) appeal. XTO challenged commonality, typicality, adequacy, and predominance. (5–7.)

## Holding and disposition

**A uniform payment system did not, on this record, establish that its legality could be decided for the entire class in one stroke.** The plaintiff had to prove shared contractual rights and address potentially different points of gas marketability. The district court’s relaxed commonality standard and possible shift of the burden to XTO were inconsistent with rigorous certification analysis. Its brief predominance analysis likewise failed to address the relevant differences adequately.

The court **vacated the certification order and remanded for further proceedings**. It did not decide the royalty claims on their merits or hold that certification could never be justified. It expressly allowed the district court to examine lease categories and potentially conclude that no category displaced the implied duty. (8–11, 16.)

## Why the common methodology was insufficient

Judge Kelly explained that Rule 23 requires proof of actual compliance, not merely allegations or a permissive presumption favoring certification. A common question must be capable of producing an answer central to each claim. The inquiry here could not stop at asking whether every owner’s royalty payment had been processed through the same software. The challenged deduction was wrongful only if the owner had the asserted contractual right and the gas had not already reached marketable condition when the relevant cost was incurred. (7–9.)

The district court assumed a marketability duty in every lease because XTO had not identified a provision that unambiguously negated it. That approached the burden backward. Given known variations, the Trust had to establish that the necessary duty existed throughout the class. The record described roughly 430 leases not yet examined by the Trust or the court. On remand, a chart grouping lease terms could help establish the answer. The appellate court did not prejudge what that examination would show. (9–10.)

The district court’s reliance on a Kansas decision did not remove the federal inquiry. Even if individual testimony about contracting intent was unnecessary, the written language within each lease might still require examination. The earlier Kansas case did not apply Federal Rule 23 and had to be evaluated against Wal-Mart and Comcast; notably, it had upheld certification despite finding that some leases expressly abrogated the covenant. (10 n.4.)

Marketability supplied a second potential difference. Under Kansas law, marketable condition is not identical to the existence of an available market at a particular location. Gas might already be marketable at one well but require further services at another. A deduction could therefore be permissible for one owner and breach the implied duty for another, despite a uniform calculation system. The district court needed to investigate whether those differences affected commonality. The court was also “not so sure” that XTO had conceded that it uniformly charged owners for making gas marketable, as the district court may have concluded: the company had not challenged the existence of a uniform payment policy but had consistently denied the claimed classwide improper deductions. That distinction cast doubt on treating a contested liability proposition as admitted. (10–11 and n.5.)

Because commonality, typicality, and adequacy overlap, the appellate court directed reconsideration of whether the identified differences also affected the representative’s typicality or adequacy. It did not independently find those requirements impossible to satisfy. (11–12.)

## Predominance, damages, and possible tailoring

Rule 23(b)(3) calls for analysis more demanding than Rule 23(a)’s commonality inquiry. Focusing only on the defendant’s common conduct was insufficient where owners might possess different legal rights or encounter different defenses. The district court needed to examine how the litigation would actually proceed, including the same lease and marketability questions discussed under Rule 23(a). Merits overlap was not a reason to avoid that inquiry when those questions bore on certification. (12–13.)

The court also required attention to damages differences. Individual damages calculations do not, standing alone, defeat certification. But they remain relevant if their complexity would overwhelm common issues. The opinion recognized ways to preserve class treatment, including subclasses or liability-only proceedings followed by individual damages calculations. It left the practical assessment and suitable tailoring to the district court. Thus the holding neither announced a categorical prohibition on royalty classes nor treated individual damages as an automatic bar. (13–14.)

## Why an earlier XTO settlement did not bind this litigation

The Trust argued that XTO should be collaterally or judicially estopped from contesting certification because it had previously settled the Fankhouser royalty class action. The court declined. The Trust had not shown identical issues or clearly inconsistent positions. Fankhouser involved a gathering system owned or operated by XTO and an asserted improper payment based on sales between affiliated companies, differences material to the earlier certification rationale. The Trust also had not addressed the settlement’s prohibition on using it in later litigation against XTO. (14–16 and n.6.)

Finally, Fankhouser’s class had been certified in March 2009, before Wal-Mart and Comcast, although settlement approval came in 2012. The district court deserved an opportunity to apply those intervening decisions to this record. The court declined to exercise its equitable power to foreclose XTO’s objection. (16.)

## Panel and assignment comparison

Judge Kelly wrote for Judges Kelly, McKay, and Matheson. No separate opinion, appendix, or division among the panel members is reported. All six judicial footnotes and their continuations were read.

The assigned paragraph says that a common calculation methodology did not establish commonality and asks what more Wal-Mart requires. The full opinion answers with a factual and legal connection: the methodology must generate a shared answer about rights actually held by the owners, after accounting for lease terms and gas condition. Certification was vacated for inadequate proof and analysis, with a genuine opportunity to develop those issues on remand. This brief describes the historical decision, not a current-law or citator determination.
