# Costello v. United States

**365 U.S. 265 (1961) · Supreme Court of the United States · February 20, 1961**

Justice Brennan wrote the Court’s opinion. Justice Douglas dissented, joined by Justice Black; Justice Harlan did not participate. The judgment was six to two to affirm revocation of Costello’s citizenship. The assigned account is in Note 3 on CivPro PDF pages 648–649, printed 1284–1285, and emphasizes the earlier dismissal’s lack of claim-preclusive effect.

## Facts and the two denaturalization proceedings

Frank Costello became a naturalized citizen on September 10, 1925. In his preliminary form, petition, and appearance before the naturalization examiner, he identified his occupation as real estate. Decades later, the government sought to revoke his citizenship on the ground that this representation concealed his actual occupation, large-scale bootlegging. The applicable statute authorized revocation for concealment of a material fact or willful misrepresentation. The district court revoked citizenship on March 9, 1959, and the Second Circuit affirmed. 365 U.S. at 266–267.

An earlier complaint, filed in 1952, had followed a different path. The district court dismissed it because wiretapping might have infected the government’s evidence and affidavit of good cause. The Second Circuit reversed so the government could demonstrate an independent or otherwise admissible basis for its evidence. The Supreme Court then reversed on a ground not considered below: the required good-cause affidavit had not been filed with the complaint. On remand, the district court declined to label its dismissal “without prejudice” and entered an order silent on that point. The government did not appeal. Instead, on May 1, 1958, it filed a new complaint accompanied by the required affidavit. The present decision reviews the resulting second proceeding; it is distinct from the earlier Supreme Court decision, 356 U.S. 256, concerning the first complaint. Id. at 267–268.

Costello challenged the new judgment on four grounds: insufficient proof of a willfully false occupation statement, wiretap-tainted admissions, the long delay before suit, and the supposedly preclusive effect of the earlier dismissal under Rule 41(b). The Court rejected all four. It did not need to review the district court’s separate finding that he had sworn false allegiance, because the occupation ground was sufficient. Id. at 268–269 n.2.

## Proof of misrepresentation and materiality

Denaturalization required clear, unequivocal, and convincing evidence. Because citizenship is precious and its loss can produce severe consequences, concurrent lower-court findings did not foreclose the Supreme Court’s examination of the record. The Court also required a materially significant falsehood: truthful disclosure could justify denial or lead to facts warranting denial. Historical naturalization decisions indicated that known bootlegging during the relevant period would probably have prevented admission under the then-applicable good-moral-character and constitutional-attachment standards. Id. at 269–272.

The evidence went beyond suspicion. Costello had admitted involvement in the illicit liquor business to federal and state investigators beginning in 1938, including admissions concerning his Prohibition-era business and earnings. Associates described substantial hauling, storage, sales, financing, and dealings conducted through his Lexington Avenue office. The Court compared those admissions and testimony with the government’s evidence of his real-estate investments. Id. at 272–276.

Koslo Realty Corporation genuinely existed and engaged in property transactions, but the Court concluded that Costello invested illicit earnings in real estate rather than pursued it as his occupation. It found neither principal income from that business, appreciable time conducting its affairs, nor central business commitment. Witnesses describing the office recounted bootlegging rather than real-estate activity. The Court considered the suggestion that “occupation” meant only a legal occupation unreasonable on these facts and found deliberate falsity, not merely an inaccurate answer. It did not need to decide whether the corporation was originally organized as a front. Id. at 276–277.

The conclusion did not depend on drawing an adverse inference from Costello’s failure to testify in the denaturalization case. The Court expressly left unresolved whether such an inference would be permissible. Its third note also explained that the 1952 statute clarified the availability of revocation for false swearing within naturalization proceedings without indicating a relaxed proof standard. Id. at 271–272 n.3, 277–278.

## Wiretapping and the delay defense

Costello argued that his 1943 admissions were induced by questioning based on tapped telephone conversations. The Court found an independent basis for the relevant information: earlier admissions, public criminal proceedings, official files, newspapers, and court records. District Attorney Hogan denied deriving his questions about bootlegging from the 1943 wiretaps, which concerned a different investigation. Although those taps prompted Costello’s appearance, the connection to the admissions about earlier bootlegging was too attenuated to require exclusion. The fourth note separately addressed alleged 1920s taps and the district court’s credited testimony that the government’s knowledge had instead come from individuals familiar with his activities. Id. at 278–280 & n.4.

The Court did not conclusively decide whether laches can ever apply in denaturalization. It assumed applicability for the sake of analysis and found the necessary prejudice absent. Laches ordinarily requires both lack of diligence and prejudice; without the latter, the Court did not need to resolve the government’s diligence. Real-estate records remained available. Deceased examiners and the admitting judge had learned his occupation from him rather than independent business knowledge, and Costello did not identify material help the missing witnesses could have supplied. Memory loss more obviously burdened the government’s proof. Id. at 281–283.

The Court recognized his residence since age four and potential reliance on citizenship, but distinguished those harsh consequences from an inability to present a defense. Congress had enacted no limitations period for fraudulently procured citizenship, and the delay did not deny due process on this record. The holding is therefore not simply that every delay defense against the government necessarily fails. Id. at 283–284.

## Assigned procedural holding: the first dismissal did not bar the new action

The Court permitted Costello to add the Rule 41(b) question to his certiorari petition and then resolved it. Under the version of the rule quoted in the opinion, covered dismissals ordinarily operated as adjudications on the merits unless the order specified otherwise, with exceptions including lack of jurisdiction and improper venue. Costello argued that the absent affidavit was not a fundamental jurisdictional defect, so silence in the first dismissal made it a merits adjudication. Id. at 284–285.

The Court interpreted “lack of jurisdiction” more broadly for this rule. It included failure to satisfy a precondition required before the court could proceed to the substantive merits. That usage need not coincide with the narrower category of defects rendering an existing judgment void on collateral attack. The affidavit requirement was such a precondition. Therefore, the first dismissal fell within the rule’s exception even without an express “without prejudice” label. Id. at 285–288.

Common-law practice reinforced that reading: dismissals not reaching a substantive right ordinarily did not bar a new action. The rule’s preclusive defaults largely addressed situations in which the defendant had to prepare to meet the merits, such as failure to prosecute or comply with an order once no initial obstacle prevented adjudication. A missing prerequisite affidavit instead required dismissal before that stage. The fifth note discussed the analogous issue of indispensable parties; it did not enact the proposed rule amendment it described. Id. at 285–288 & n.5.

## Dissent, judgment, and assigned citation

Douglas, joined by Black, disputed the merits inference. The form asked for an occupation, not every activity or source of income. Because Costello’s real-estate corporation existed, conducted transactions, and had him as president, its secondary role did not necessarily make his answer false. Douglas would resolve that ambiguity against the government under the demanding denaturalization proof standard. His starred note reproduced the form’s actual prompt. The dissent also questioned the historical treatment of bootlegging in a society that widely demanded its products. Id. at 288–289.

The Court affirmed the Second Circuit, sustaining revocation in the second proceeding. Its Rule 41(b) ruling allowed that proceeding but did not alone establish the substantive ground for revocation; the separate evidentiary analysis did so. Id. at 288.
