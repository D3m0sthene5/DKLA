# Slocum v. New York Life Insurance Co.

**228 U.S. 364 (1913) · Supreme Court of the United States · April 21, 1913**  
**Course:** Civil Procedure  
**Assigned account:** CivPro, physical PDF page 572, printed page 1127, Note 1 following *Redman*.

Justice Van Devanter delivered the Court’s opinion. The Court agreed that the insurance company should have received a directed verdict, but held that the appellate court could not replace the plaintiff’s unconditional jury verdict with judgment for the company on the evidence. It instead required a new trial. Justice Hughes, joined by Justices Holmes, Lurton, and Pitney, dissented from that remedial conclusion. The judgment was therefore 5–4 on the disputed constitutional question; the dissent agreed that reversal of the plaintiff’s judgment was proper. (375, 399–401, 428.)

## Facts and chronology

Alexander W. Slocum held a $20,000 life insurance policy issued in 1899. It required an annual premium of $579.60, due November 27, and permitted a month’s grace. The policy restricted authority to modify its terms or extend payment time to specified company officers. By 1907, Slocum had borrowed the policy’s entire $2,360 reserve. Consequently, the reserve could not fund continued or paid-up insurance after a premium default. That eliminated one possible route to recovery independently of the dispute about the company’s agent. (366–374.)

The company also used a “blue-note” arrangement under which an insured could pay part of the amount in cash and sign a prescribed note for the remainder. The signed note supplied the terms of a temporary extension, including what happened if the note remained unpaid. Slocum and his wife knew this practice because they had used it three or four times before. The agent could make an adjustment under that arrangement, but could not dispense with the note and extend coverage on different terms. (371–375.)

Before the 1907 premium fell due, Slocum’s wife asked the Pittsburgh agent about an easier payment arrangement because her husband lacked ready money. One proposed method called for $264.20 cash and a $434 blue note payable in six months. On December 27, the last day of grace, she delivered the cash amount by check. The agent gave her the note to obtain her husband’s signature, told her to return it, and agreed that she could mail it as soon as she could because he was ill. She took it home, but Slocum was too ill to attend to it. He died four days later without signing. No receipt for the partial payment was given or requested. (371–373.)

The St. Louis agency subsequently acknowledged the remittance without knowing of Slocum’s death. Its letter said that the money was held subject to his order pending return of the properly signed note. Although the check was deposited to the company’s credit, its amount was carried in a suspense account. Afterward, the executrix tendered the amount the note would have covered, and the company tendered return of the check amount. Neither tender was accepted. (373–374.)

## Procedure and competing positions

The executrix sued on the policy in the federal Circuit Court for the Western District of Pennsylvania. After all evidence had been received, the company requested a directed verdict. The trial court denied that request, and the jury returned a general verdict for the plaintiff for $18,224.02, reflecting deductions from the policy amount and interest. The trial court also declined to enter judgment for the company notwithstanding the verdict. The Third Circuit reversed and directed entry of judgment for the company under Pennsylvania practice. Supreme Court review concerned both the conclusion that the evidence was insufficient and the authority to end the case without another trial. (366–369, 375–376.)

The plaintiff claimed that the agent’s receipt of the partial payment extended coverage, or that the company adopted and confirmed his acts. She also had relied on reserve-based continuation of insurance. The company maintained that the loan exhausted the reserve and that an unsigned blue note could not produce the authorized adjustment. On procedure, the Pennsylvania statute allowed a party whose request for binding instructions had been reserved or refused to seek judgment on the whole evidence. The issue was whether federal courts could use that state procedure consistently with the Seventh Amendment. (370–377.)

## Insurance holding

The Court approved the appellate court’s conclusion that the policy was not in force when Slocum died. The policy did not authorize premiums to be paid in scattered installments. A partial payment therefore mattered only if a valid adjustment or waiver accompanied it. A person who knows an agent’s authority is limited cannot bind the principal through an act exceeding that authority. Here, the company’s practice allowed the agent to adjust the payment only through the prescribed blue note. Its terms were substantive, and the insured and his wife knew that the signed note was required. (374–375.)

The company’s handling of the check did not support ratification. Its correspondence and suspense-account treatment instead showed that it awaited the required note and held the funds subject to the insured’s direction. The evidence thus could not sustain a finding that coverage continued. The trial court should have directed a company verdict, and reversing the plaintiff’s judgment was correct. (373–375.)

## Why insufficiency nevertheless required a new trial

The majority treated the Seventh Amendment’s Reexamination Clause as an independent limit on the remedy for that trial error. State practice could not override the federal constitutional protection. It looked to the historical English common law, rather than the practice of an individual State, to determine how facts once tried by a jury could be reexamined. The ordinary routes were a new trial granted by the trial court or one ordered by an appellate court for legal error. (376–380.)

Once the appellate court set aside this general verdict, the factual issues raised by the pleadings were again unresolved. In the majority’s view, judgment on those issues still required the constitutionally constituted tribunal of judge and jury. The sufficiency of the evidence controlled whether the judge should direct the verdict, but did not eliminate the need for a verdict or authorize the appellate court to substitute its own final disposition of the factual issues. The Court distinguished the right to a jury trial, determined from the material factual issues in the pleadings, from the legal standard for directing a particular verdict on the evidence. (380–388, 397–399.)

Historical judgment notwithstanding the verdict and arrest of judgment did not support the Pennsylvania result. Those procedures addressed defects apparent in the pleadings: the verdict’s findings could be immaterial because the pleaded claim or defense was legally inadequate. They did not authorize judgment against a verdict merely because the trial evidence was insufficient. Likewise, a properly framed demurrer to evidence proceeded on facts admitted and ascertained through that procedure. A voluntary nonsuit left the plaintiff free to bring another action. Neither furnished the majority with authority to set aside an unconditional general verdict and enter a binding judgment on the evidence in this case. (381–382, 388–395.)

The Court considered contrary nonsuit precedents but distinguished their nonpreclusive effect. It also viewed the new trial as a substantive opportunity: the plaintiff might supply omitted evidence or expose errors in the defendant’s proof. That opportunity was not lost because the evidence at the first trial was inadequate. (395–400.)

## Hughes’s dissent

Hughes argued that the majority constitutionalized a procedural form rather than protecting the jury’s substantive role. Everyone agreed that there was no evidentiary basis for a plaintiff’s verdict. Whether such a basis existed was a question of law; correcting its erroneous resolution did not reweigh a factual dispute committed to the jury. If the trial judge had granted the requested instruction, final judgment for the company could have followed. Hughes saw no constitutional reason why the same legal conclusion required another trial merely because the judge initially made a mistake. (400–402, 427–428.)

He explained that Pennsylvania’s procedure preserved the distinction between law and fact: judgment against the verdict remained impermissible when conflicting evidence on a material fact prevented a binding instruction. The statute allowed deliberate reconsideration of a legal question after trial and avoided unnecessary retrials. Hughes also read the common-law demurrer to evidence as supporting final judgment where all favorable facts and inferences were admitted and their legal insufficiency was decisive. He regarded the modern procedure as a simpler way of performing that function. (402–423.)

Finally, he distinguished the majority’s precedents as involving factual issues, incomplete records, or courts actually replacing a jury’s factual determination. A general possibility of improving the plaintiff’s evidence did not, for him, create a constitutional entitlement to another trial. The case did not involve an application based on newly discovered evidence. (423–428.)

## Judgment and assigned significance

The Court **modified the Third Circuit’s judgment**, eliminating its direction to enter judgment for the defendant and substituting a direction for a new trial. It did not restore the plaintiff’s verdict or hold that the policy covered the death. (400.)

The assigned note accurately emphasizes that the directed-verdict motion had been denied and that the verdict was unconditional. Its ensuing distinction of *Redman* and discussion of later Rule 50 procedure are textbook commentary, not holdings of this 1913 opinion. This brief preserves *Slocum*’s historical constitutional reasoning without presenting its remedial restriction as a statement of current Rule 50 law.
