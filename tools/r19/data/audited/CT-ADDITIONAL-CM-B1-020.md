# Pine River State Bank v. Mettille
333 N.W.2d 622 (Minn. 1983) | Contracts

## Court and posture
Justice Simonett wrote the Minnesota Supreme Court's en banc April 29, 1983 opinion. Justice Coyne did not participate; the complete reproduction includes no separate opinion or numerical voting tally. The court affirmed denial of the bank's post-trial motions and sustained the judgment for its former employee on a contract counterclaim. Definite disciplinary procedures in a handbook distributed after hiring could become binding through an at-will employee's continued service. The actual holding concerned failure to afford those procedures, rather than a general tort prohibition on bad-faith discharge or a guarantee of lifetime employment. (624, 630–632.)

## Hiring, handbook, and dismissal
Richard Mettille, then unemployed and nearly forty-eight, accepted an oral offer from Pine River State Bank in early 1978 at $1,000 monthly. He moved from St. Paul and began work on April 10. Neither permanence nor a fixed term was discussed, and he understood that he remained free to leave for another job. After a six-month probation he held the loan-officer title, with responsibilities including loans, collateral insurance, financing statements, and student-loan reports. Late that year the bank distributed a printed handbook. President E. A. Griffith drafted it using a model from an employee-relations seminar. He testified that he intended information and internal guidance, rather than a contractual commitment. (624.)

The handbook covered many employment matters. Its job-security section praised the stability of banking employment and the bank's avoidance of seasonal layoffs. Its disciplinary section was more specific: an initial oral reprimand with written notice to the executive vice president, a written reprimand for the second offense, a further written reprimand and meeting with possible five-day unpaid suspension, and discharge if conduct did not improve after the previous action. Every discharge required an executive officer's review of the facts. These four listed steps appear in the full third note; the court's main discussion groups the sequence into broader stages. (625–626 & nn.1–3.)

Mettille received a seven-percent raise after his April 1979 review. In September, state examiners identified exceptions in loan files. The bank reviewed eighty-five files and characterized fifty-eight as containing serious exceptions, assigning responsibility for fifty-seven to Mettille. Problems included missing vehicle titles, missing or inadequate collateral insurance, and financing-statement deficiencies. Officers said loans exceeding $600,000 were involved. Those characterizations were contested: the bank disputed some examiners' financing-statement findings, and Mettille disputed that his errors were serious or uncorrectable. (624–625.)

When Mettille returned from illness on September 28, Griffith fired him without reviewing the exception list with him or following the handbook's disciplinary steps. He received two months' severance. The bank relied on loan errors, with sick leave and a reduction in force also identified as factors; Mettille asserted a personality conflict. Evidence showed no prior reprimands, errors that were easily correctable, and less harsh treatment of other officers' errors; Mettille also argued that the errors were in fact corrected within a month after dismissal. The court did not itself find every error trivial; it recognized a factual conflict for the jury. (625, 631.)

## Litigation and findings
The bank sued in November 1980 on two defaulted notes. Mettille counterclaimed for discharge without cause and denial of required disciplinary procedures. At the January 1982 trial, the jury found a contract restricting discharge without good cause, discharge without good cause, and $27,675 damages. After deducting debt owed to the bank, the trial court entered $24,141.07 for Mettille. Both sides unsuccessfully sought a new trial, but only the bank appealed. This counterclaim posture is more precise than describing the litigation simply as an employee's action against the bank. (625.)

## Contract formation through continued employment
An employment promise of indefinite duration can create a unilateral contract if sufficiently definite as an offer, communicated, and accepted through performance. Objective manifestations govern whether it is an offer; an employer's undisclosed subjective intention does not. General assurances of a career or stability are insufficient. Distribution can communicate definite terms, and an employee who stays with knowledge of them, although free to leave, can both accept and supply consideration. A later handbook can therefore modify an earlier employment arrangement. Using a common handbook also reduces the cost of individual contracting. Employers can reserve discretion or amendment rights in its language; the opinion does not make every handbook statement binding. (626–627 & n.4.)

The bank advanced three objections to job-security restrictions: at-will status should prevail, consideration beyond ordinary services was required, and the employee's freedom to leave defeated mutuality. The court rejected each as a categorical bar. At-will employment is a presumption for interpreting an indefinite hiring, not a substantive prohibition on agreed restrictions. Additional consideration can help show that parties intended genuine permanent employment, but is not the exclusive means of supporting a clear termination provision. One performance can support multiple promises. Once consideration is present, a separate mutuality requirement cannot invalidate the employer's undertaking merely because the employee retains freedom to resign. (627–630.)

The fifth note distinguishes post-hiring noncompete promises demanded from employees. Those restrictions implicated the law's disfavor of restraints on trade and were treated under a different public-policy rule requiring independent consideration in the cited cases. Pine River's continued-service analysis therefore should not be transplanted indiscriminately to those employee restraints. (630 n.5.)

## Breach and preserved questions
The bank's general “Job Security” statements were policy, but the definite “Disciplinary Policy” supplied an offer of termination procedures. Mettille's continued work accepted and supported it. The jury had been told to find that the bank intended to be bound, an incorrectly subjective formation test. The bank nevertheless chose to retain that instruction when withdrawal was offered, making it the law of the case; it could not obtain reversal on that basis. (630 & n.6.)

The handbook did not actually use “good cause” or “just cause.” The trial court's construction permitted summary dismissal for good cause, but neither the propriety of its good-cause definition nor whether the contract allowed that exception was presented for decision. The jury's finding that good cause was absent also made those questions unnecessary. The operative holding was that applicable, enforceable procedures were not followed. The bank's only excuse was its asserted at-will freedom, which no longer answered the contractual procedural promise. Mettille might have corrected his deficiencies if given warning and opportunity. (630–631 & n.7.)

## Evidence, damages, and exact disposition
The court found no reversible evidentiary error. Cross-examination exposed the model handbook's warning that a handbook constituted a contract. Admission would better have been avoided, but was not sufficiently prejudicial in the circumstances. The bank also had an adequate opportunity to explain other officers' errors before additional testimony was excluded as repetitive and irrelevant. (631–632.)

Lost wages through trial were supportable. The bank might eventually have lawfully terminated Mettille after complying with the procedures, but the record did not establish that this would have occurred before trial. The protections had some substantive value; otherwise warnings and an opportunity to improve would be meaningless. Mettille's request for a new trial on mental-anguish damages was not before the court because he filed no notice of review, rather than being rejected through a merits ruling on availability. The disposition was affirmance. (632.)
