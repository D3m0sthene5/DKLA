# Industrial Union Department, AFL–CIO v. American Petroleum Institute

**448 U.S. 607 (1980), decided July 2, 1980. Supreme Court of the United States.** Nos. 78–911 and 78–1036 concern the same consolidated decision, commonly called the Benzene Case. Assigned material: November continuation casebook, PDF 39–42, printed 983–986; the specific case account begins at the bottom of 983 and ends near the top of 985, followed by scientific-risk commentary. Pinpoints below refer to original U.S. Reports pages.

**Result and voting.** By 5–4, the Court affirmed the Fifth Circuit judgment remanding the benzene standard to the Secretary of Labor for further proceedings. Justice Stevens announced the judgment in an opinion joined throughout by Chief Justice Burger and Justice Stewart. Justice Powell joined Parts I, II, III–A, III–B, III–C, and III–E, but not III–D or IV, and wrote separately. Justice Rehnquist supplied the fifth vote for the judgment on a constitutional nondelegation ground. Burger also concurred separately. Justice Marshall dissented with Justices Brennan, White, and Blackmun. Consequently, Stevens's opinion is a plurality opinion, and its narrower three-Justice discussions must not be presented as a majority opinion. The Court did not hold that benzene at ten parts per million was safe, decide the general validity of cost-benefit analysis under the Act, or invalidate the statute as unconstitutional. (611, 614–615, 639–640, 658–662.)

## Facts and regulatory chronology

Congress enacted the Occupational Safety and Health Act in 1970. Section 3(8) defined an occupational safety and health standard by reference to conditions reasonably necessary or appropriate for safe or healthful employment. Section 6(b)(5), specifically addressing toxic materials and harmful physical agents, directed the Secretary to adopt a standard that most adequately assured, to the extent feasible and on the best available evidence, that employees would not suffer material impairment during a working lifetime. The dispute concerned how those instructions interacted when a substance was a recognized carcinogen but its risk at low exposure levels could not be measured confidently. (612–615, 639–652.)

Benzene was used in several industries, and high exposure could cause leukemia and other serious blood disorders. The previous standard, adopted from a consensus standard in 1971, generally permitted a ten-parts-per-million average over an eight-hour day, with higher short-term ceilings and peaks. In 1974, the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health had not recommended lowering the ten-ppm limit. In 1976, it recognized benzene as a human carcinogen and recommended one ppm, a concentration selected in light of monitoring detection limits and background exposure rather than a scientifically established safe threshold or an evaluation of economic and technological feasibility. An emergency standard in 1977 encountered judicial obstacles. The Secretary ultimately issued a permanent standard in February 1978 and amended it that June. (615–626 & nn.6–22.)

The new rule imposed a one-ppm eight-hour average, a five-ppm fifteen-minute ceiling, and provisions governing contact with benzene-containing liquids. A half-ppm action level triggered surveillance requirements. Depending on measured exposure, the rule required monitoring, medical examinations, engineering controls, and supplemental respiratory protection. The contact prohibition was amended to permit progressively smaller concentrations in liquids; fuel uses after bulk distribution were excluded. These details matter because the Secretary did not uniformly insist on eliminating every theoretically avoidable molecule of benzene. Technical monitoring limitations, background levels, and the scope of the regulated workforce influenced the practical standard. (623–629 & nn.14, 21–29.)

The Secretary's cancer policy presumed no safe threshold unless industry could establish one. Once benzene was shown to cause cancer at high exposures, that policy supported reducing exposure to the lowest level that the agency regarded as technologically and economically feasible. The Secretary did not estimate the number of additional leukemia cases occurring at ten ppm or the number prevented by lowering it to one ppm. The evidence involved small populations, uncertain historical exposure estimates, latency, and potential confounding factors. The plurality's extended review of this record questioned whether the agency had made the necessary finding; it did not establish its own medical conclusion about the safety of ten ppm. (630–638, 658–659; latency at 700 (Marshall, J., dissenting).)

## Procedure and competing arguments

Industry challengers sought judicial review of the airborne-exposure and dermal-contact provisions. The Fifth Circuit invalidated those portions of the rule, concluding that the Secretary had not adequately justified the standard. The court also discussed a relationship between costs and benefits. The union and the Secretary sought Supreme Court review in the consolidated matters. Specific objections to monitoring, testing, and the priority of engineering controls had not been decided by the Fifth Circuit and were not before the Supreme Court. (613–614, 629–630 & n.30.)

The government argued that the special toxic-substance instruction in §6(b)(5) required protection to the maximum feasible extent, even when cancer risk at the existing limit could not be quantified. Its position treated uncertainty as a reason for precaution: employers should not obtain the benefit of an unproved safe threshold. Industry argued that the Act required a meaningful showing of danger and justification for the regulatory burdens. The decisive plurality ground was the lack of the agency's required significant-risk finding; the Court expressly left the broader cost-benefit question unresolved. Nor could the standard be sustained on a rationale or findings that the agency might have adopted but did not. Judicial review had to examine the grounds on which the Secretary actually acted. (631 & n.31, 638–640, 659.)

## Plurality interpretation: a significant-risk threshold

The four Justices joining Stevens's principal statutory analysis read §3(8) together with §6(b)(5). Safe employment did not mean an environment free from every conceivable risk. Before adopting a permanent standard, the Secretary had to find that existing conditions posed a significant risk of material health impairment and that the new standard could eliminate or lessen that risk. The requirement distinguished an unsafe workplace within the statute from the ordinary minor risks that accompany human activity. The plurality drew support from the statute's structure, priority provisions, legislative changes, and the practical significance of the feasibility limitation. (639–652.)

Without that threshold, evidence that a substance caused cancer at very high exposures could support regulation at arbitrarily low concentrations, regardless of whether the remaining risk was important. Thousands of potential carcinogens could then generate enormous regulatory costs with little discernible benefit. Stevens treated the breadth of that claimed power as a reason to reject the government's construction absent a clear legislative mandate. He also observed that such an open-ended delegation might raise constitutional difficulties and favored an interpretation that avoided them. This was a statutory construction reinforced by constitutional concern, not a holding that Congress had enacted an unconstitutional delegation. (645–646 & n.51.)

In the three-Justice Part III–D, Stevens explained how the threshold could be administered. The agency bore the burden of supporting the significant-risk determination with substantial evidence; it could not make industry prove safety. Yet certainty and mathematically exact estimates were unnecessary. The Secretary could use reputable scientific evidence, reasonable conservative assumptions, and policy judgment at the frontiers of knowledge, including assumptions that erred toward protection. Stevens's comparison between an extremely remote risk and a one-in-a-thousand risk illustrated the significance inquiry rather than establishing a numerical regulatory cutoff. His discussion also recognized monitoring as a possible protective backstop while uncertainty was resolved. These points belong to the three Justices joining that portion, although Powell separately accepted that exact quantification could be infeasible. (652–658 & nn.59–67; 667.)

The central error was therefore not that the agency had insufficient power to respond until workers died or epidemiology became perfect. It was that the Secretary applied a policy moving directly from known carcinogenicity to the lowest feasible exposure limit, shifting the burden of demonstrating safety to industry without making the significant-risk finding required by the plurality's interpretation. Stevens expressly declined to determine what a properly justified agency finding could have established on this record. (658–659.)

Stevens, Burger, and Stewart applied the same threshold to restrictions on dermal contact. The agency relied on potential absorption and carcinogenic effects without the required significant-risk finding. They did not decide the Fifth Circuit's separate objection that additional experiments should have been performed. The judgment affirmed the remand for further agency proceedings, rather than directing adoption of a particular permissible exposure level. (659–662.)

## Separate opinions

**Burger's concurrence.** Burger emphasized that the agency needed an understandable, supported description of significant risk. Courts could enforce that statutory obligation while leaving policy judgment to the Secretary. They should not replace an authorized agency policy merely because they believed it inefficient or imprudent. (662–664.)

**Powell's concurrence in part and in the judgment.** Powell agreed with the threshold requirement and agreed that this record did not sustain the rule, but differed from some of Stevens's treatment of the agency's attempt to meet its burden. An adequately supported conclusion that the benefits were appreciable could embody a significant-risk finding even without numerical precision. He would not say the Secretary had never attempted to demonstrate the relevant danger. He separately concluded that regulatory costs must bear a reasonable relationship to expected benefits under the statutory language and criticized the lack of a reasoned justification for the costs here. That cost-benefit position did not command the Court. Powell, although he joined Part III–B, also expressly stated no view on whether a different interpretation of the statute would violate the nondelegation doctrine. A later formal generic cancer policy was not the basis of this rule and was not being adjudicated. (664–671 & nn.1–8.)

**Rehnquist's concurrence in the judgment.** Rehnquist viewed the unresolved tradeoff between worker protection and economic burden as a fundamental legislative policy choice. Where a safe toxic-exposure level was unknown or infeasible, he believed §6(b)(5)'s first sentence lacked adequate guidance and improperly transferred that choice to the Secretary and ultimately the courts. His reasoning addressed political responsibility, intelligible administrative guidance, and judicial standards of review. He would invalidate the sentence in that setting; his final note preserved situations in which a known safe level was feasible and other statutory sources of authority. No other Justice joined his opinion, so the judgment did not adopt this constitutional invalidation. (671–688 & nn.1–8, especially 685–688 and n.8.)

**Marshall's dissent.** Marshall's four-Justice dissent read the toxic-substance provision as Congress's specific decision to protect workers to the feasible extent despite scientific uncertainty. It emphasized the large hearing record, evidence of carcinogenicity, limits of low-dose epidemiology, and the Secretary's expert assessment. A lack of reliable numerical estimates did not mean that the agency lacked evidence of a serious threat. The dissent argued that the plurality imposed a new statutory obstacle and displaced administrative judgment; it also rejected respondents' argument that the statute's reference to “material” impairment excluded low-probability risks, because the text speaks of material impairment, not material risk of impairment. It disputed both Powell's cost-benefit reading and Rehnquist's constitutional objection, and defended the dermal-contact rule without requiring every potentially informative experiment. Marshall would uphold the standard rather than affirm its invalidation. (688–724 & nn.1–36, especially 695–708, 715–724.)

## Assigned emphasis and limits

The assignment uses Benzene as a historical precursor to modern major-questions reasoning, highlighting the plurality's concern about unprecedented regulatory power and Rehnquist's separate nondelegation position. Its phrase “Writing for the Court” must be read against the official voting structure: Stevens announced the judgment, but no five Justices joined his statutory opinion. The subsequent textbook discussion of quantitative risk assessment is editorial context. It should not be substituted for the opinion's allowance for uncertainty, conservative assumptions, and estimates that do not achieve exact mathematical measurement. This brief describes the 1980 decision and does not certify every later doctrinal development mentioned in that editorial discussion.
