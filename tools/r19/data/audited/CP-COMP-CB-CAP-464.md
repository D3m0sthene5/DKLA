# Hazel-Atlas Glass Co. v. Hartford-Empire Co.

**322 U.S. 238 (1944) · Supreme Court of the United States · May 15, 1944**  
**Course:** Civil Procedure  
**Assigned account:** CivPro, physical PDF page 598, printed pages 1180–1181; following editorial notes run from printed 1181 onto printed 1182 on that same physical page (Note 2’s closing *Philos* paragraph continues onto physical page 599).

Justice Black delivered the Court’s opinion requiring relief from a patent-infringement judgment obtained through an organized deception of the Patent Office and the appellate court. Justice Roberts dissented, joined by Justices Reed and Frankfurter; Chief Justice Stone agreed with the result Roberts proposed. Both sides condemned the fraud. Their disagreement concerned the proper court, procedure, and scope of immediate relief. The result was 5–4, with Stone joining the dissent’s proposed disposition rather than its entire reasoning. (239, 251–252, 271.)

## Fraud and the original litigation

Hartford sought a patent for a glass-working machine using “gob feeding.” Its application faced strong Patent Office opposition. Hartford officials and attorneys prepared a trade-journal article describing the machinery as a revolutionary advance and sought the apparent endorsement of an independent labor expert. After one union president declined to sign, they obtained the signature of William P. Clarke, president of the Flint Glass Workers’ Union. The article appeared in July 1926 and was submitted to the Patent Office that October. The patent issued on January 3, 1928. (240–241.)

Hartford sued Hazel for infringement in June 1928. The district court dismissed because infringement had not been proved. Although the article was part of the patent file history, it was not emphasized in that trial. On appeal, Hartford’s counsel specifically invoked Clarke’s supposed authorship. One participating attorney had helped arrange the publication. The Third Circuit quoted the article extensively as evidence that practical workers recognized the invention’s distinguishing features and held the patent valid and infringed in May 1932. (241.)

At the time of the district-court trial, Hazel’s lawyers heard that a Hartford lawyer actually wrote the article, but did not verify that information then. After the appellate reversal, Hazel hired investigators. Clarke insisted that he was the author, refused to give Hazel an affidavit, and refused access to his files without a subpoena. Unknown to Hazel’s investigator, Hartford had also sent a representative to meet Clarke. On the same day Clarke refused Hazel an affidavit, he signed one for Hartford stating that he had signed and released the article. (241–243.)

Hazel then settled, paid Hartford $1 million, and entered licensing agreements. Hartford subsequently paid Clarke $8,000 in cash, describing a moral obligation while denying a prior payment agreement. The fuller documentary proof—including correspondence, expenses, and testimony—emerged in an antitrust prosecution in 1941. Hazel then sought to undo the 1932 judgment. (243.)

## The proceeding under review

Hazel initially asked the Third Circuit for permission to file a bill of review in the district court. The appellate court decided that the asserted fraud had been practiced on itself and allowed Hazel to amend its prayer to request relief there. Hartford submitted opposing affidavits and exhibits. The Third Circuit ultimately refused relief, reasoning that the fraud was not newly discovered, that the article was not the principal basis of the earlier decision, and that expiration of the earlier court term prevented reopening the judgment and disturbing the district decree entered under its mandate. (239–244.)

The Supreme Court addressed whether the appellate court could vacate its own earlier judgment and direct corresponding relief below. It also had to determine what remedy the established fraud required. The case concerned fraud on the judicial process, not simply whether a losing party had discovered another useful piece of patent evidence.

## Equity, finality, and the public interest

The majority acknowledged the historical rule against modifying final judgments after the relevant court term. Finality ordinarily serves society by ending litigation. But equitable relief against sufficiently serious, later-discovered fraud had developed alongside that rule. Depending on the circumstances, courts had set judgments aside, altered them, or prevented their beneficiaries from obtaining their fruits. The remedy was flexible because its purpose was to correct exceptional injustice despite the normal finality rule. (244–245 & nn.1–3.)

The fraud here met that exceptional standard. It was not a mere allegation that a witness might have committed perjury, supported by later evidence. Hartford’s own sworn admissions and the documentary record established a planned scheme to mislead both the Patent Office and the appellate court. No innocent purchaser had acquired an intervening interest in the patent or judgment. (245–246.)

The majority did not allow Hazel’s possible lack of maximum diligence to excuse Hartford’s conduct. The integrity of patent proceedings and of the administration of justice implicated public interests beyond the litigants’ private stakes. Those interests could not invariably depend on how diligently the injured party investigated. Likewise, the Court refused to calculate precisely how much the false attribution affected the prior judges. The article had impressed the appellate court, Hartford had deliberately deployed it, and Hartford could not now minimize its effect. Nor could the company excuse the deceptive authorship by claiming the article’s substantive statements were true. It should have appeared as Hartford’s advocacy, not an independent endorsement. (246–247.)

## Why the appellate court could grant relief

Hartford conceded that the district court could grant equitable relief on a proper fraud showing but denied that the appellate court had comparable power after the term expired. The majority rejected that asymmetry. Appellate permission to file a bill of review was itself a substantive exercise of judgment, not a ceremony. If the appellate court could finally deny relief long after its judgment, it could also finally grant relief when undisputed fraud on that very court required it. Sending the matter below solely to repeat the conclusion was unnecessary on this record. (247–250 & n.4.)

The critical qualification appears in footnote 5. The Court **did not authorize final resolution of genuinely disputed material fraud facts on ex parte affidavits without examination and cross-examination**. Hartford had not disputed the documents’ accuracy or asserted that a trial could disprove their contents. Evidence merely showing less diligence by Hazel would not change the result here. The Court reserved whether difficult, disputed fraud facts should be tried in the appellate court or sent to the district court. The holding therefore does not dispense generally with an evidentiary hearing whenever fraud is alleged. (249–250 n.5.)

## Roberts’s dissent and Stone’s position

Roberts agreed that the fraud was reprehensible, but wanted permission for an ordinary equitable suit in the district court. His dissent emphasized finality, the appellate court’s statutory role, live testimony, cross-examination, and the customary diligence and clean-hands requirements. Its extensive notes explain the historical term rule, limitations on receiving new appellate evidence, and available independent equitable actions. Footnote 18 characterizes the fraud involving the article as extrinsic and potentially sufficient for review, while preserving the other equitable requirements. (251–262 & nn.1–18.)

He also disputed the majority’s treatment of Hazel’s position. Roberts recounted evidence that Hazel knew which witnesses could establish the article’s actual authorship, deliberately declined to raise the matter, and benefited financially from the settlement and patent arrangements. In his view, a full trial might establish negligence or deliberate withholding that defeated Hazel’s equitable entitlement. Immediate relief on affidavits risked rewarding one participant in anticompetitive arrangements at another’s expense. Those were the dissent’s reasons for a district-court trial, not findings adopted by Black’s majority. (262–271 & nn.19–24.)

Roberts proposed reversing with directions to permit Hazel to file the bill below. Reed and Frankfurter joined his opinion; Stone agreed with that proposed result. (271.)

## Exact judgment and limitations

The Court **reversed** and directed the Third Circuit to set aside its 1932 judgment, recall its mandate, dismiss Hartford’s appeal, and direct the district court to vacate its mandate-based judgment and reinstate its original judgment denying Hartford relief. Additional appropriate action remained possible. This was more specific than a general remand for reconsideration. (250–251.)

The Court separately explained that actually cancelling a fraudulently obtained patent required a direct government proceeding; cancellation was not available in the infringement action. Thus, denying Hartford relief against Hazel and vacating the infringement judgments did **not itself cancel the patent**. (251.)
