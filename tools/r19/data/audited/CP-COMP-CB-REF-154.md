# I.M. Wilson, Inc. v. Otvetstvennostyou “Grichko”
2018 WL 6446601 (E.D. Pa. Dec. 10, 2018) | Civil Procedure

## Decision and sources

District Judge Gene E.K. Pratter authorized service on three foreign defendants through their U.S. counsel, but required hard copies of the summons and complaint rather than the proposed email delivery. The assigned account appears in Civil Procedure PDF page 158, printed page 267, Note 4. The full memorandum contains seven numbered judicial pages and one footnote, followed by a blank eighth physical page. A separate one-page order specifies the permitted service. There are no separate opinions or appendices. Pinpoints below use the memorandum's original numbered pages, without inventing a Westlaw pagination map. ## The trademark relationship and alleged infringement

I.M. Wilson was a Pennsylvania corporation selling dancewear, including pointe shoes. In the late 1980s it entered an agreement with Nicolai Grishko to develop and sell a pointe-shoe brand in the United States. Wilson developed the plans and marketed the brand; Grishko, through Grishko, Inc., developed the shoes. Wilson alleged that it owned the GRISHKO brand in the United States. Pages 1–2.

The defendants were Nicolai Grishko, Russian limited-liability company OOO Grichko, and Czech limited-liability company Grishko Dance, S.R.O. OOO Grichko sold products through third parties worldwide and held territorial trademark rights in some countries outside the United States. Grishko was the principal owner and operator of the Russian and Czech entities and had a Moscow business address. Wilson alleged that, nearly thirty years into the relationship, the defendants infringed its U.S. rights by selling GRISHKO-branded products to U.S. customers through Internet sales and Canadian distributors. Those allegations explained the dispute; the service ruling did not establish infringement or finally resolve trademark ownership. Pages 1–2.

Wilson sought permission to serve all three defendants through Brian Kinder, the Russian defendants' U.S. lawyer. The plaintiff also had a pending preliminary-injunction motion. It argued that domestic delivery to counsel would avoid delay and reduce the further harm it alleged. Pages 2, 5.

## The alternative-service framework

The memorandum described Rule 4(f)'s internationally agreed methods, other specified foreign-service methods, and court-directed alternatives under subsection (3). Rule 4(h)(2) supplied the corresponding route for foreign corporations, subject to the exception for personal delivery under Rule 4(f)(2)(C)(i). Pages 2–3.

Following *Rio Properties* and *Knit With*, the court treated subsection (3) as an available method rather than extraordinary relief available only as a last resort. A court could, in its discretion, require attempted service and a demonstrated need for intervention, but the rule did not invariably impose that preliminary requirement. The court still had to select a method compatible with international agreements and constitutional due process. The purpose was meaningful notice in time to permit the defendants to answer and present defenses, not simply completion of a convenient delivery exercise. Pages 3–4.

## Different obstacles for the Russian and Czech defendants

The court relied on the State Department's account of Russia's suspension of judicial cooperation with the United States in 2003. According to that account, requests sent through diplomatic channels or to Russia's Hague Central Authority were returned unexecuted. Wilson therefore proposed U.S. counsel as the effective route for the Russian defendants. This describes the information the court used in December 2018, rather than independently certifying present Russian practice. Pages 4–5.

The Czech defendant presented a different concern. The Czech Republic was a Convention signatory, and Wilson expected service there to take two to four months. Wilson argued that this delay was particularly harmful while its preliminary-injunction request remained pending. The memorandum did not equate Czech Convention practice with Russia's nonexecution of requests. It accepted the urgency and proposed domestic channel as reasons for using a court-directed alternative. Page 5.

The court stated that, as far as it was aware, service through American counsel was not prohibited by Russian law or international agreement, or by Czech law. It cited decisions permitting delivery to U.S. attorneys for defendants in those countries. That compatibility conclusion was part of this court's historical application, rather than a general declaration that every foreign defendant's American lawyer may always be served. Page 6.

## Counsel's ongoing relationship supported meaningful notice

Kinder reportedly was actively representing OOO Grichko and Nicolai Grishko in opposition and cancellation proceedings before the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board concerning the same marks. Wilson had communicated with him about those proceedings and possessed his correct contact information. That ongoing professional relationship, its connection to this dispute, and counsel's duties supported the expectation that he would notify the Russian clients. Page 6.

The Czech company's connection required an additional explanation. Nicolai Grishko was its sole director and board member, and Kinder already represented Grishko in U.S. litigation. The court reasoned that serving Kinder was therefore reasonably calculated to convey notice to Grishko Dance through that relationship. It did not rest on a finding that Kinder independently represented the Czech company in every proceeding or had voluntarily accepted appointment as its service agent. Pages 6–7.

The pending injunction motion also made prompt notice valuable to the defendants: expeditious service would enable them to represent themselves and protect their interests in an accelerated proceeding. Urgency thus accompanied, rather than replaced, the demonstrated connection between counsel and the foreign parties. Page 7.

## Exact delivery requirement and disposition

The memorandum granted the alternative-service motion and authorized delivery of the summons and complaint to Kinder at his business address in accordance with Rule 4(c). Its sole footnote rejected Wilson's proposed email delivery because an email could be caught by a spam filter or otherwise fail; service had to use hard-copy materials. Page 7 & n.1.

The separate December 10 order granted Motion No. 3 and expressly authorized service on OOO Grichko, Nicolai Grishko, and Grishko Dance by delivering hard copies of the summons and complaint to Kinder at his business address under Rule 4(c). It did not decide the preliminary-injunction motion, award damages, enter default, or determine infringement. Separate order, page 1.

The assigned account accurately captures the distinction between authorizing service through U.S. counsel and refusing email as the delivery medium. The complete reasoning adds the shared trademark proceedings, the Czech company's connection through its sole director, the separate country-specific obstacles, and the need for timely participation. Those circumstances explain the order's tailored authorization. Assigned printed 267; memorandum pages 5–7.
