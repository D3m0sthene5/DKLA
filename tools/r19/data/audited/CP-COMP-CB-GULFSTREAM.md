# Gulfstream Aerospace Corp. v. Mayacamas Corp.

**485 U.S. 271 (1988).** The Supreme Court decided the case on March 22, 1988. Justice Marshall wrote an opinion joined by all eight participating Justices. Justice Scalia also concurred separately; Justice Kennedy did not participate. The assigned account appears in CivPro physical page 626, printed page 1240.

## Facts and procedural path

Mayacamas agreed to purchase an aircraft manufactured by Gulfstream, intending to resell it while demand was high. It later refused payments, alleging that Gulfstream had frustrated the transaction’s purpose by increasing aircraft production and availability. Gulfstream sued for breach of contract in Georgia state court. Mayacamas answered and counterclaimed there rather than removing the suit. About a month later, it brought its own diversity action against Gulfstream in federal court in Northern California for breach of the same contract. These were the parties’ competing contract positions; the Supreme Court did not determine which company breached or whether the asserted frustration defense was valid. (272–273.)

Gulfstream asked the district court to stay or dismiss the federal proceeding under Colorado River, which permits that response to parallel state litigation only in exceptional circumstances. The district court found the circumstances insufficient and denied the motion. Gulfstream appealed, asserting jurisdiction under the final-decision statute, §1291, or the injunction-appeal provision, §1292(a)(1). Alternatively, it asked the Ninth Circuit to treat the notice of appeal as a mandamus application. (273–274 & nn.1–4.)

The circuit dismissed for lack of appellate jurisdiction. It declined to convert the notice into a mandamus application because Gulfstream had not shown serious hardship or prejudice, and added that the district court acted within its discretion anyway. One circuit judge disagreed on appealability but would have affirmed the stay denial. The Supreme Court reviewed a division among the circuits over immediate appeal of these denials. (274–275 & nn.5–6.)

## Final decisions and the collateral-order exception

The Court first held that the order was not immediately appealable under §1291. It plainly did not end the litigation: denying the stay ensured that the federal action would continue. Gulfstream therefore needed the collateral-order exception rather than ordinary finality. That exception required a conclusive determination, an important question entirely separate from the merits, and effective unreviewability after final judgment. Failure of any one requirement was enough to defeat it. (275–276 & n.7.)

The Court distinguished an order **granting** a Colorado River stay. Moses H. Cone had treated such a stay as appealable because the federal court expected the state proceeding to resolve the whole dispute, leaving nothing substantive for the federal court to do. Once the state judgment received preclusive effect, later review would not adequately recover the lost federal adjudication. Although technically revisable, the stay was intended as the final word on that question. (276–277.)

A **denial** did not carry the same expectation. It could reflect a judgment that developments should be awaited before deciding whether the state proceeding was sufficiently comprehensive or had progressed far enough to justify deferral. As litigation developed, the court could reassess the balance. The denial was thus inherently tentative for collateral-order purposes and failed the conclusiveness requirement. The Court expressly did **not** decide whether the other two collateral-order requirements were satisfied. Its reasoning should not be restated as a holding that all three failed. (277–278.)

## Injunction appealability and rejection of the historical shortcut

The Court next rejected jurisdiction under §1292(a)(1). An order regulating only the progress or conduct of the issuing court’s own proceedings ordinarily is not an injunction for purposes of that statute. Gulfstream invoked an older exception, the Enelow-Ettelson doctrine, under which a stay of a historically legal action on historically equitable grounds was treated as an appealable injunction. The contract suit qualified as historically legal, but the parties disputed whether the requested Colorado River stay rested on an equitable defense or merely equitable considerations. (279–282.)

Rather than decide that classification dispute, the Supreme Court overruled the doctrine. It had originated from the separation of law and equity: a chancellor’s restraint of a law action resembled an injunction from one tribunal to another. Even when that reasoning began, the same federal judge administered both systems. After their procedural merger, the fiction that a judge enjoined another side of the same court had even less connection to actual practice. (279–284.)

The doctrine was also difficult to administer. Mixed complaints and modern remedies did not fit the inherited labels. Declaratory judgments required courts to classify a hypothetical action that had never been filed, and arbitration or administrative stays created further classification problems. Those distinctions did not track whether prompt appellate review was needed. Note 10 illustrated the anomaly with a single stay appealable as to a legal counterclaim but not an equitable specific-performance claim. The result depended on historical categories rather than the order’s practical consequences. The opinion surveyed extensive judicial and scholarly criticism, including the authorities and continuation in note 11. (284–287 & nn.8–11.)

The resulting holding was that stays of “legal” proceedings on “equitable” grounds were **not automatically appealable** under §1292(a)(1). It did not eliminate all immediate review of stay orders. Actual grants or denials of injunctions remained within the statute, as did orders with an injunction’s practical effect and serious, perhaps irreparable consequences. Where their separate requirements were met, the collateral-order doctrine, permissive appeals under §1292(b), and mandamus also remained available. Note 13 specifically preserved mandamus protection for a jury right impaired by a stay. Gulfstream could not obtain appealability merely through the abandoned Enelow-Ettelson analogy. (287–288 & nn.12–13.)

## Why mandamus was unavailable here

Gulfstream argued that Mayacamas’s decision to forgo removal and instead file a separate federal suit itself required a stay or dismissal, absent imperative circumstances. The Court rejected that categorical premise. Colorado River imposed an exceptional-circumstances requirement on the party seeking to discontinue federal litigation, not an automatic obligation to defer whenever a defendant chose a separate federal action. Gulfstream identified no other circumstance making the district court’s denial clearly outside its authority. (288–290.)

Mandamus was an extraordinary remedy requiring a clear and indisputable entitlement and exceptional circumstances amounting to a judicial usurpation of power. Gulfstream had not met that burden. The Court did not decide whether the Ninth Circuit appropriately refused to convert the notice of appeal into a mandamus petition; note 14 expressly reserved that procedural question because the asserted entitlement failed regardless. The result therefore cannot be treated as an endorsement of every detail of the circuit’s conversion practice. (289–290 & n.14.)

## Judgment and Scalia’s concurrence

The Supreme Court **affirmed the Ninth Circuit’s judgment**. The denial was not immediately appealable under either asserted statute, and it did not warrant mandamus. Federal contract litigation could continue; the Court did not adjudicate the contract’s merits or hold that reconsideration of a stay would never be permissible. (290.)

Scalia joined the full Court opinion but cautioned against resting finality solely on a possibility of reconsideration. A categorical denial can conclusively reject immediate relief even if another request may later be entertained. His immunity hypothetical illustrated why potential reconsideration cannot, by itself, make every denial nonfinal. For the present type of stay, he emphasized both likely renewal and the availability of equally or nearly equally effective relief later, with limited harm in the interval. He agreed with the conclusion that initial Colorado River denials were not immediately appealable. (290–291.)

Scalia also discussed a possible additional limit requiring an important, unsettled controlling legal question rather than mere review of discretionary judgment. He found support in precedent and policy, but expressly declined to adopt that rationale without adversarial briefing of its consequences. It was a suggested limiting principle, not a new rule announced by the Court. His concurrence had no footnotes or appendix. (291–292.)
