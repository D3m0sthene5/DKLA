# Auditing a case brief against its sources

A law student's study atlas (DKLA) uses case briefs that another system (Codex) wrote from the full
opinions and the assigned casebook pages. Most have passed an independent review. The briefs in your
batch have NOT: they are marked as awaiting independent review. You are that review. The student will
study from these, so the standard is a careful cite-check: every statement in the brief should be
supported by the sources, and anything that is not must be found and corrected.

DIR is the folder containing this file. You are told your batch file, `DIR/batches/aNN.json`, in your
prompt. Each item in it has:

- `id`, `title`, `course`, `codex_status`
- `brief`: the brief to audit (markdown). READ ONLY: do not edit this file.
- `record`: Codex's working record for the brief (JSON): citation, decision date, author, exact
  disposition, which sources it used, which pages are assigned, and its own stated limitations. Read it
  first; it tells you what each source file is. It may be long; read the parts you need.
- `full_text_sources`: text or HTML files holding the opinion (and sometimes the assigned excerpt) as
  Codex saved them. These are your primary evidence.
- `pdf_sources` and `assigned_segments`: the casebook PDF and the page range assigned for this case
  (`first`..`last` are PDF page numbers for the Read tool's `pages` parameter; at most 20 pages per call).
  Read the assigned pages when the text sources do not already contain the assigned excerpt, or when the
  brief makes a claim about what the casebook says.
- `page_images`: scans, where they exist.
- `corrected_out`: where to write a corrected brief, if corrections are needed.

Everything under `/private/tmp/dkla_complete`, `/private/tmp/DKLA_Work_Handoff` and the other Codex
folders is READ ONLY. Write only the files named below. Do not use any browser or web tool: audit against
the saved sources, not against your memory of the case or anything fetched from outside.

## Method, for each brief

1. Read the brief in full. Read the record. Open the sources. Large source files: read them in parts with
   the Read tool's offset and limit, and use Grep to locate names, dates, figures and quoted phrases. Read
   the whole opinion when it is of ordinary length; for a very long file, read every part the brief
   relies on and enough around it to judge context.
2. Check the brief claim by claim. For each paragraph, find the supporting passage. Check in particular:
   - the citation, court, decision date, author, and who joined, concurred or dissented;
   - the parties and which side each was on at each stage;
   - the procedural history and the exact disposition (affirmed, reversed, vacated, remanded, in part);
   - every date, amount, number and name in the facts;
   - every quotation, word for word, and every pinpoint page where the source shows page numbers;
   - the holding and each step of the reasoning: does the court actually say this, and is it attributed
     to the right opinion (majority, plurality, concurrence, dissent, the court below, a party)?
   - statements about what the casebook says or omits, against the assigned pages;
   - anything presented as later history or present law.
3. Classify what you find:
   - **error**: the source contradicts the brief (wrong fact, wrong party, wrong disposition, wrong
     attribution, misquotation, wrong pinpoint).
   - **overstatement**: the brief says more than the source supports, or drops a qualification that
     changes the meaning.
   - **unsupported**: you could not find support in any source you were given. Say whether the sources
     plainly lack it or whether you simply could not check (for example the page is not in the files).
   - A difference of emphasis or wording that does not change the meaning is not a finding.
4. If the brief needs corrections, write a corrected copy to `corrected_out`: the full brief, identical
   to the original except for the minimal edits that fix each error or overstatement, with unsupported
   statements removed or qualified. Keep Codex's structure, headings and style. Do not rewrite, shorten,
   reorganise or add material beyond what a correction requires. If nothing needs correcting, do not
   write the file.

## Output

Write `DIR/out/aNN.json`: an object keyed by `id`, one entry per brief in the batch:

```json
{
  "verdict": "clean | corrected | could-not-audit",
  "sources_read": ["file names you actually read, with which parts if partial"],
  "coverage": "one sentence: what you checked the brief against (complete opinion? assigned excerpt only? which parts could not be checked and why)",
  "findings": [
    {"kind": "error | overstatement | unsupported", "where": "the brief's heading or paragraph",
     "was": "the brief's words", "now": "the corrected words, or 'removed'",
     "evidence": "what the source says, with the file and where in it"}
  ],
  "notes": "anything the student should know that is not a correction, or empty"
}
```

`clean` means you checked every claim you could and found no error or overstatement. `corrected` means
you wrote `corrected_out`. `could-not-audit` means the sources you were given were not enough to check
the brief's substance; say why in `coverage`. Be exact about coverage: do not claim to have checked what
you did not read. A brief can be `clean` and still carry notes about parts you could not verify.

Your final reply: one or two lines giving, for the batch, how many were clean, corrected and
could-not-audit, the total findings by kind, and anything unresolved.
