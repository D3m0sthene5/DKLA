CIVIL PROCEDURE: PERSONAL JURISDICTION
 Gray v. American Radiator & Standard Sanitary Corp. - World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson
 Parties / Procedural History / Material Facts / Question Presented / Holding / Reasoning
 PART I - BRIEFS
1. Gray v. American Radiator & Standard Sanitary Corp.
22 Ill. 2d 432, 176 N.E.2d 761 (Ill. 1961). Supreme Court of Illinois. Klingbiel, J. No. 35872. Opinion filed June 14, 1961; rehearing denied
September 20, 1961. Direct appeal from the Circuit Court of Cook County.
Parties
Appellant and plaintiff: Phyllis Gray, an Illinois resident who sought damages for injuries caused by an exploding water heater.
Nonresident defendant and appellee: Titan Valve Manufacturing Company, an Ohio corporation that manufactured the heater's
safety valve and challenged Illinois personal jurisdiction. Defendant, cross-claimant, and additional appellant: American Radiator
& Standard Sanitary Corporation, which incorporated Titan's valve into its water heater and sought contractual indemnification from
Titan if Gray recovered against it. Other defendants named in the underlying products-liability action were outside the jurisdictional
issue decided on appeal.
Procedural History
Cook County filing and statutory basis: Gray filed a damages action in the Circuit Court of Cook County against Titan, American
Radiator, and others. Her complaint alleged that Titan negligently manufactured the safety valve and that the defect proximately
caused the explosion and her Illinois injuries. Illinois Civil Practice Act section 17(1)(b), Ill. Rev. Stat. 1959, chapter 110, paragraph
17, treated a nonresident's commission of a tortious act within Illinois as submission to Illinois jurisdiction. Section 16, paragraph 16,
authorized personal delivery of summons outside the State to a nonresident who had so submitted.
Out-of-state service: Summons issued and was personally served on Titan's registered agent in Cleveland, Ohio. The service therefore
depended on two distinct propositions: section 17 had to create adjudicatory authority over Titan, and section 16 had to supply a valid
method for notifying Titan outside Illinois. Titan conceded the adequacy and statutory regularity of the section 16 notice for purposes
of the appeal; it contested Illinois's authority to bind it.
Special appearance and motion to quash: Titan appeared specially, preserving its objection to personal jurisdiction while asking the
court to quash service. Its affidavit stated that Titan conducted no business in Illinois, maintained no agent physically present there,
and sold its completed valves to American Radiator outside Illinois. Titan argued that manufacturing conduct in Ohio supplied no
tortious act within Illinois under section 17(1)(b), and that treating the Illinois injury as a sufficient contact would exceed the
Fourteenth Amendment.
Cross-claim and trial-court ruling: American Radiator answered Gray's complaint and cross-claimed against Titan. The cross-claim
alleged that Titan made warranties to American Radiator and owed indemnification if American Radiator became liable to Gray. The
circuit court granted Titan's motion, quashed the out-of-state service, and dismissed both Gray's complaint and American Radiator's
cross-claim against Titan. The court thereby ended Titan's participation before any adjudication of negligence, warranty, indemnity,
causation, or damages.
Direct appeal and final disposition: Gray appealed directly to the Supreme Court of Illinois because Titan's due-process challenge
placed a constitutional question in issue. American Radiator also supported reversal of the order that eliminated its cross-claim. The
Illinois Supreme Court reversed and remanded with directions to deny Titan's motion to quash. The merits returned to the Cook
County circuit court with Titan subject to personal jurisdiction. The court denied rehearing on September 20, 1961.
Material Facts
Titan manufactured the safety valve in Ohio and sold the completed valve to American Radiator outside Illinois. American Radiator
incorporated the valve into a hot-water heater in Pennsylvania. The heater then moved through ordinary commercial distribution to an
Illinois consumer. It exploded in Illinois and injured Gray there. Gray alleged that negligent valve construction proximately caused the
injury. The appellate record identified no direct Illinois sales, employees, office, or agent of Titan and supplied no quantified volume
for Titan products reaching Illinois.
Titan did not characterize this heater's Illinois use as an isolated instance. The court inferred from the ordinary commercial distribution
of appliances that Titan's transactions produced substantial use of its valves in Illinois and that the valves were manufactured for
incorporation into products expected to reach ultimate consumers in multiple States. Illinois was also the place of injury, Gray's
residence, the likely location of injury and damages witnesses, and the jurisdiction whose substantive law would govern the
occurrence.
Question Presented
Two questions: (1) Does Illinois Civil Practice Act section 17(1)(b) classify a tort as committed in Illinois when the defendant's
negligent manufacturing occurred elsewhere and the final injury necessary for tort liability occurred in Illinois? (2) If the statute
reaches that conduct, does the Fourteenth Amendment permit Illinois to exercise in-personam jurisdiction over the component
manufacturer through personal service on its Ohio registered agent?

Holding
Statutory authorization: Yes. A tortious act under section 17(1)(b) includes the injury that completes the alleged tort. Illinois was the
place of the wrong because the final event necessary to liability occurred there. Constitutional authority: Yes. Titan's placement of
valves into the ordinary commercial chain, with ultimate use in Illinois contemplated and with the present claim arising from an
Illinois injury caused by the product, supplied a substantial forum connection. The forum's interests, local evidence, modern
distribution practices, and manageable litigation burden made the exercise reasonable. Notice: Service on Titan's Ohio registered agent
under section 16 furnished the accepted method of notification. Disposition: Reversed and remanded with directions to deny the
motion to quash.
Reasoning
Analytical order and competing positions: The court proceeded through three separate civil-procedure questions. First, it interpreted
the Illinois long-arm statute. Second, it tested the authorized jurisdiction against the Fourteenth Amendment. Third, it confirmed that
the chosen service method supplied reasonable notification. Gray and American Radiator treated the Illinois injury and ordinary
distribution of Titan's product as the forum connection. Titan located the relevant act at its Ohio factory and emphasized the absence of
Illinois personnel, direct sales, and a documented Illinois business volume.
Meaning of 'tortious act': The statutory phrase incorporated the complete tort. Negligent conduct becomes legally tortious through
resulting injury; the injury therefore forms an inseparable part of the statutory event. Using the then-prevailing place-of-wrong rule
from Restatement of Conflict of Laws section 377, the court located the tort where the last event required for liability occurred. Gray's
injury completed the alleged tort in Illinois. This construction allowed section 17(1)(b) to reach Titan even though its employee
performed the manufacturing conduct in Ohio. 22 Ill. 2d at 435-36.
Statutory reach and constitutional limit: Illinois precedent construed section 17 as reaching nonresidents to the boundary permitted
by due process. That construction did not collapse the two inquiries. The legislature supplied the asserted authority through sections 16
and 17; the Fourteenth Amendment independently determined whether Illinois could enter a binding personal judgment. Titan's special
appearance properly presented both issues before the merits.
Notice and adjudicatory power: The court expressly separated minimum contacts from notification. Section 16's personal service on
Titan's registered agent gave Titan a realistic opportunity to appear and defend. The constitutional power question asked whether
Titan's own commercial relationship to Illinois made the resulting judgment legitimate. Titan's concession on notice left the second
question as the live constitutional dispute. 22 Ill. 2d at 436-37.
Development from Pennoyer through International Shoe: The court described personal jurisdiction as moving from Pennoyer's
territorial service model toward International Shoe's evaluation of contacts, fair play, and substantial justice. International Shoe
showed that a systematic claim-producing course of forum business supports jurisdiction; Perkins showed that sufficiently substantial
forum activity can support claims arising elsewhere; Hanson required a connection created through the defendant's invocation of
forum benefits. Gray used those authorities to ask whether this particular transaction and injury formed a substantial Illinois
connection. 22 Ill. 2d at 437-41.
Single transaction and claim relationship: McGee established that one deliberate, continuing transaction can support specific
jurisdiction when the claim arises from that transaction. Gray extended the same claim-centered logic to products: quantified
continuous business supplied one route to minimum contacts, while a product transaction substantially connected to the forum and
producing the lawsuit supplied another. The cause of action arose directly from the valve's Illinois use and injury. 22 Ill. 2d at 438-40.
Commercial distribution and indirect benefits: Modern component manufacturers commonly reach consumers through assemblers
and independent middlemen. Titan elected to manufacture valves for incorporation into heaters sold in interstate commerce. The court
inferred that this commercial design led to substantial Illinois use and allowed Titan to benefit from Illinois law protecting the
marketing and consumption of products containing its valves. The distributor's physical delivery, rather than Titan's own shipment,
preserved the commercial relationship between Titan's conduct and Illinois. 22 Ill. 2d at 441-43.
Forum and litigant interests: Illinois possessed a direct regulatory and remedial interest because an Illinois resident suffered injury in
Illinois from a product used there. Illinois substantive law governed the occurrence, and witnesses concerning injury, damages, and the
explosion were likely located there. Modern transport and communication reduced Titan's defense burden. Those circumstances made
Illinois a convenient and reasonable forum for resolving the product-related dispute. 22 Ill. 2d at 443-44.
Connection to World-Wide Volkswagen: Gray later appears on both levels of World-Wide Volkswagen. The Oklahoma Supreme
Court declined to use Gray's place-of-injury construction for Oklahoma subsection 1701.03(a)(3) because Oklahoma's adjacent
subsection (a)(4) expressly governed out-of-state acts producing in-state injuries. The U.S. Supreme Court cited Gray when describing
jurisdiction over a manufacturer or distributor that delivers products into commercial distribution with an expectation that consumers
will purchase them in the forum. The comparison makes defendant-directed market distribution the important factual bridge.
1L explanation: Start with the Illinois statute. Injury in Illinois completed the alleged tort and activated section 17(1)(b); section 16
then supplied out-of-state personal service. Due process asked whether Titan's commercial placement of the valve, its contemplated
ultimate use in Illinois, and the claim-producing Illinois injury formed a fair forum relationship. The court answered yes and restored
both Gray's claim and American Radiator's cross-claim against Titan.

2. World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson
444 U.S. 286 (1980). U.S. Supreme Court. White, J. Argued October 3, 1979; decided January 21, 1980. No. 78-1078. Certiorari to World-Wide
Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson, 1978 OK 131, 585 P.2d 351 (Okla. 1978).
Parties
Petitioners and jurisdictional objectors: World-Wide Volkswagen Corporation, a New York corporation serving as the regional
distributor for Volkswagen products sold to dealers in New York, New Jersey, and Connecticut; and Seaway Volkswagen, Inc., the
New York retail dealer in Massena that sold the Robinsons' Audi. Underlying plaintiffs and respondents: Kay Robinson, suing for
her own injuries; Harry Robinson, individually and as father and next friend for the injured children; and the children, including Eva
May Robinson and George Samuel Robinson. Named judicial respondent: Charles S. Woodson, District Judge of Creek County,
whose continued exercise of in-personam jurisdiction the petitioners sought to prohibit.
Other underlying defendants: Audi NSU Auto Union Aktiengesellschaft, the manufacturer, and Volkswagen of America, Inc., the
U.S. importer. Volkswagen also entered a special appearance in the trial court, then declined to seek Oklahoma Supreme Court review.
Audi and Volkswagen therefore remained defendants in the Oklahoma action and were outside the jurisdictional judgment reviewed
by the U.S. Supreme Court.
Procedural History
Products-liability filing: After the Oklahoma accident, the Robinsons filed a manufacturers' products-liability action in the District
Court for Creek County, Oklahoma. They alleged defective design and placement of the Audi's gasoline tank and fuel system and
joined the manufacturer, importer, regional distributor, and retail dealer. Oklahoma's Uniform Interstate and International Procedure
Act, 12 Okla. Stat. 1971 sections 1701.01 et seq., supplied the asserted long-arm authority over the out-of-state companies.
Special appearances and trial-court rulings: World-Wide and Seaway entered special appearances to contest personal jurisdiction
under the Fourteenth Amendment. Their papers also used the phrases 'venue of the subject matter' and 'venue over the subject matter.'
The Creek County court treated the appearances as special, rejected their constitutional objection, and denied reconsideration. Those
rulings were unreported. The merits action remained pending.
Preservation and waiver issue: The Robinsons later argued that petitioners had waived their objection by combining
personal-jurisdiction language with a challenge concerning subject matter and by taking merits depositions in Oklahoma. The trial
court retained the special-appearance characterization, and the Oklahoma Supreme Court decided the jurisdictional questions. The
U.S. Supreme Court accordingly treated the objection as preserved and reached the merits. 444 U.S. at 295 n.10.
Original proceeding for prohibition: World-Wide and Seaway invoked the Oklahoma Supreme Court's original jurisdiction and
petitioned for a writ of prohibition directing Judge Woodson to stop exercising personal jurisdiction. Prohibition served as the pretrial
vehicle for obtaining appellate review of adjudicatory authority before the petitioners litigated the products-liability merits. The
Oklahoma Supreme Court assumed original jurisdiction and denied the writ. 1978 OK 131, paragraphs 0-3, 17.
Oklahoma statutory analysis: Section 1701.03(a)(3) covered tortious injury in Oklahoma caused by an act or omission in Oklahoma.
The state court held that the alleged design and distribution acts occurred outside Oklahoma. It declined to classify the place of injury
as the place of the act under Gray, because subsection (a)(4) separately governed an Oklahoma injury caused by an out-of-state act.
Subsection (a)(4) authorized jurisdiction when the defendant regularly did or solicited Oklahoma business, engaged in another
persistent Oklahoma course of conduct, or derived substantial revenue from goods used or consumed or services rendered in
Oklahoma. 585 P.2d at 353-54.
Oklahoma ruling under subsection (a)(4): The state court reasoned that an automobile's designed mobility made Oklahoma use
foreseeable and inferred from the vehicle's value and evidence of petitioners' goods being used in Oklahoma that the petitioners
derived substantial revenue from goods used there. Five justices joined the opinion, two concurred in the result, and one concurred in
part and dissented in part without opinion. The court therefore found statutory authority, assumed original jurisdiction, and denied
prohibition. 585 P.2d at 354-55.
U.S. Supreme Court review and disposition: The Supreme Court granted certiorari, 440 U.S. 907 (1979), to resolve a conflict
between Oklahoma and the highest courts of at least four other States concerning jurisdiction over out-of-state automobile sellers. The
Court reviewed the Fourteenth Amendment question while accepting Oklahoma's authoritative construction of its own long-arm
statute. By a six-to-three judgment, it reversed the Oklahoma Supreme Court. Oklahoma consequently lacked power to bind
World-Wide and Seaway in this action; the claims against Audi and Volkswagen remained pending in Creek County. 444 U.S. at
290-91, 299 & nn.6, 9.
Material Facts
Harry and Kay Robinson, then New York residents, purchased a new Audi from Seaway in Massena, New York, in 1976. In 1977 the
family left New York to establish a new home in Arizona. While they drove through Oklahoma, another vehicle struck the Audi from
behind. The gasoline tank ruptured, fire entered the passenger compartment, and Kay Robinson and the children suffered severe burns.
The other driver played no role in the jurisdictional litigation.
World-Wide's New York office administered a contractual regional distribution territory limited to New York, New Jersey, and
Connecticut. Seaway was a New York corporation operating its dealership in Massena. The record treated World-Wide, Seaway,
Volkswagen, and Audi as independent corporations connected by contracts. World-Wide and Seaway conducted no Oklahoma
activity, closed no Oklahoma sales, performed no Oklahoma services, maintained no Oklahoma agent, shipped no products to
Oklahoma, and purchased no advertising calculated to reach Oklahoma.

The evidentiary record contained one vehicle sold by these petitioners that had entered Oklahoma: the Robinson Audi, carried there by
its purchasers during their interstate move. The petitioners received no direct revenue from Volkswagen service centers in Oklahoma.
The forum relationship therefore depended on the vehicle's mobility, petitioners' role in the national automobile system, the Oklahoma
accident, and the state court's inference of substantial revenue from cars used there.
Question Presented
Does the Fourteenth Amendment permit an Oklahoma court to exercise specific in-personam jurisdiction over a New York automobile
retailer and a regional distributor when they limited their sales and distribution to the New York-New Jersey-Connecticut market, and
their only case-specific Oklahoma connection arose when New York purchasers drove the automobile through Oklahoma and suffered
the accident there?
Holding
Constitutional authority: No. Due process requires defendant-created affiliating circumstances connecting the defendant to the
forum. World-Wide and Seaway directed no commercial activity toward Oklahoma, and the purchasers' unilateral transportation of
one car through the State supplied the record's sole product entry. Designed mobility made interstate travel predictable, while the
constitutionally operative foreseeability asked whether petitioners' own conduct made Oklahoma suit reasonably anticipatable.
Statutory ruling below: The Oklahoma Supreme Court had found subsection 1701.03(a)(4) satisfied; the U.S. Supreme Court held
the resulting assertion beyond the Fourteenth Amendment. Disposition: The judgment denying prohibition was reversed.
Reasoning
Source of law and procedural posture: Oklahoma law defined the reach of the Oklahoma courts; the Due Process Clause supplied an
independent federal ceiling. The Oklahoma Supreme Court was the final expositor of subsection 1701.03(a)(4), so certiorari brought
the constitutional judgment to the U.S. Supreme Court. Because the case arrived through prohibition before trial, the record addressed
forum affiliations rather than products-liability merits. The manufacturer and importer remained in the trial court, and the ruling
concerned World-Wide and Seaway individually.
Minimum contacts and the two functions of due process: International Shoe requires a forum relationship consistent with fair play
and substantial justice. The majority identified two functions for that requirement: protecting a defendant from distant or inconvenient
litigation and maintaining the territorial allocation of coercive authority among coequal States. The latter function allows due process
to withhold state power even where litigation burden is light and forum convenience is strong. 444 U.S. at 291-94.
Sequence of contacts and reasonableness: Forum interests enter the reasonableness inquiry through the forum's regulatory stake, the
plaintiff's interest in convenient and effective relief, interstate judicial efficiency, and shared substantive policies. A defendant-created
forum affiliation remains the predicate for exercising specific jurisdiction. Oklahoma's accident evidence, resident safety interest, and
convenient location could inform fairness after that predicate; those interests could not create World-Wide's or Seaway's forum
contact. 444 U.S. at 292-95.
Defendant-specific affiliations: Neither petitioner served or sought to serve the Oklahoma market. World-Wide's distribution territory
ended with New York, New Jersey, and Connecticut; Seaway's retail sales occurred in Massena. The record showed no Oklahoma
sales, services, agents, shipments, targeted advertisements, or indirect market cultivation by either company. The corporate
relationships with Audi and Volkswagen were contractual, so the broader market presence of the manufacturer, importer, or service
network did not become the contacts of these petitioners. 444 U.S. at 288-89, 295.
Operative foreseeability: The jurisdictional foreseeability described a legal consequence of the defendant's own forum-directed
conduct: that conduct must make being haled into the forum's courts reasonably anticipatable. The physical likelihood that a mobile
product will arrive in a State describes product movement. Here, the Robinsons' decision to transport the Audi supplied that
movement, and Hanson assigned the unilateral activity to the purchasers. Petitioners' commercial conduct supplied no
Oklahoma-directed counterpart. 444 U.S. at 295-98.
Products distributed to a forum market: The majority distinguished consumer travel from a commercial effort to serve a forum. A
manufacturer or distributor that directly or indirectly cultivates a State's market and expects consumers there to purchase its products
receives predictable exposure to suit when the product causes injury there. The opinion cited Gray as an example of products delivered
into commercial distribution with an expectation of forum purchase. World-Wide and Seaway had restricted their own selling territory
to the tristate region, leaving Oklahoma outside the markets they served. 444 U.S. at 297-98.
Substantial-revenue theory: The Oklahoma court inferred substantial revenue from the mobility and retail value of automobiles used
in Oklahoma. The Supreme Court required the revenue to stem from a constitutionally cognizable forum contact. Petitioners received
their sales revenue from their own regional market and received no direct revenue from Oklahoma service centers. Any value
attributable to nationwide travel or collateral service availability remained too attenuated to establish Oklahoma affiliation. 444 U.S. at
298-99.
Predictability and primary conduct: Purposeful availment gives commercial actors advance notice of where their activity creates
exposure to suit. They can structure distribution territories, purchase insurance, price litigation risk, and withdraw from markets whose
costs they reject. Jurisdiction tied to every destination a consumer might choose would make the consumer's travel decision define the
seller's judicial exposure. The majority assigned that exposure to markets the seller itself served. 444 U.S. at 297.
Relationship to Pennoyer, International Shoe, McGee, and Hanson: Pennoyer supplied the territorial and interstate-sovereignty
inheritance; International Shoe translated amenability into minimum contacts and fair play; McGee showed that one defendant-created,
claim-producing forum relationship can suffice; Hanson required purposeful availment and assigned unilateral contacts to the person
who created them. World-Wide synthesized those decisions into a defendant-directed affiliation requirement and treated the fairness
factors as part of a structured analysis. 444 U.S. at 291-98.

Justice Brennan's dissent: Brennan read International Shoe as a fairness-and-reasonableness standard in which contacts give content
to the inquiry alongside the forum's interest and actual defense burden. Oklahoma hosted the accident, injury evidence, emergency
response, and likely witnesses. Petitioners benefited from the national highway and dealership system that made interstate automobile
use valuable. Those concrete interests and benefits made Oklahoma adjudication fair in his analysis. 444 U.S. at 299-313.
Justice Marshall's dissent, joined by Justice Blackmun: Marshall attributed interstate effects to petitioners' deliberate participation
in a nationwide automobile marketing and servicing system. World-Wide was one of seven regional Audi distributors, and Seaway
sold a product designed for long-distance travel whose value depended partly on nationwide service. He viewed out-of-state operation
as the product's intended commercial use and found that purposeful economic structure sufficient when the defect caused injury in
Oklahoma. 444 U.S. at 313-17.
Justice Blackmun's separate dissent: Blackmun emphasized the automobile as a distinct product whose ordinary function includes
interstate movement. Oklahoma roads, police, accident investigation, and emergency aid supported that use and benefited professional
distributors and dealers. He would have upheld jurisdiction over automobile professionals when the sold vehicle caused an Oklahoma
accident, while leaving other product categories for their own factual analysis. 444 U.S. at 317-19.
Narrow holding and doctrinal handoff: The judgment addresses a local retailer and a regional distributor whose proved market
activity stopped outside Oklahoma, coupled with one car carried into Oklahoma by its purchasers. The opinion also recognizes
jurisdiction when a business itself serves the forum market and the distributed product causes injury there. Later product-jurisdiction
disputes build on the boundary between a defendant's market-directed distribution and movement attributable to another actor.
1L explanation: Track the litigation vehicle and the actor responsible for each contact. The Oklahoma trial judge kept World-Wide
and Seaway in the products case; those defendants used an original prohibition action to secure pretrial review; the Oklahoma Supreme
Court found long-arm authority and denied relief; the U.S. Supreme Court accepted the preserved due-process question and reversed.
The Robinsons brought the Audi into Oklahoma. World-Wide and Seaway structured no Oklahoma market activity. That attribution
controlled the constitutional result.
