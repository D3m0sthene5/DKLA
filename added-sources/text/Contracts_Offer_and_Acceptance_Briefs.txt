CONTRACTS: OFFER AND ACCEPTANCE
Offer and acceptance
Reading guide and governing framework
An offer gives its recipient the legal power to form a contract by accepting the proposed exchange in the
invited manner. The person making the offer is the offeror; the person authorized to accept is the offeree. An
offer exists when the words and circumstances reasonably communicate that the recipient's assent will
complete the bargain. A statement that reserves a further decision for its maker ordinarily begins or
continues negotiations. Acceptance expresses assent to the offered terms through the words or conduct that
the offer invites or requires. These questions concern the parties' observable communications and the
reasonable meaning of their conduct. Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§ 24, 26, 30, 50 (1981).
Lefkowitz concerns a definite advertisement and acceptance by complying with its terms. Wucherpfennig
distinguishes a present commitment from continued negotiation. International Filter identifies the operative
offer and the effect of a specified approval method. White examines whether preparations objectively
communicate acceptance. In each case, the wording and circumstances determine what further action
completes the bargain.
A promise can serve as acceptance when the offer seeks a commitment to perform; conduct can also express
that promise. When an offer invites either a promise or performance, beginning the invited performance
ordinarily accepts and carries a promise to finish. When the offer invites acceptance only through
performance, beginning or tendering the invited performance can create an option protecting the opportunity
to finish, while the offeror's duty remains conditional on completion according to the offer. Preparation,
actual beginning, completion, and notice therefore require separate analysis. Restatement §§ 32, 45, 50, 54,
56, 62.
For goods, the applicable state's Uniform Commercial Code supplies additional rules. UCC § 2-206
ordinarily permits acceptance in a reasonable manner and medium unless the offer or circumstances
unambiguously require otherwise. An order for prompt shipment generally invites either a prompt promise
to ship or prompt shipment. These rules should be distinguished from the historical reasoning in the older
opinions. The Restatement synthesizes common-law principles whose adoption depends on the jurisdiction.
UCC § 2-206.

Lefkowitz v. Great Minneapolis Surplus Store, Inc.
251 Minn. 188, 86 N.W.2d 689 (1957). Supreme Court of Minnesota. Murphy, J. Decided December 20, 1957.
The Minnesota Supreme Court held that the store's advertisement for a specifically identified fur stole at a
stated price, offered to the first customer to arrive, created an offer that Morris Lefkowitz accepted by
satisfying those conditions. The store could not then add an eligibility restriction absent from the
advertisement. Lefkowitz, the customer and plaintiff below, was the respondent on appeal and represented
himself. Great Minneapolis Surplus Store, Inc., the merchant and defendant below, appealed an order
refusing to alter the trial court's findings or grant a new trial. The Supreme Court affirmed, leaving
Lefkowitz's $138.50 damages award in place. 251 Minn. at 189-92, 86 N.W.2d at 690-91.
The full opinion concerns two newspaper advertisements published a week apart. On April 6, 1956, the store
advertised three new fur coats for one dollar each, available at 9 a.m. the following Saturday on a first-come,
first-served basis. The advertisement described the coats as worth up to $100. On April 13, the store
advertised two pastel mink three-skin scarfs, described as selling for $89.50, and one black lapin stole,
described as worth $139.50, for one dollar each. The second advertisement again specified Saturday at 9
a.m. and first-come, first-served availability. The lapin stole was the item that ultimately supported the
damages award.
On each Saturday, Lefkowitz arrived first at the appropriate counter, demanded the advertised item, and
indicated that he was ready to pay one dollar. The store refused both times. On the first visit, it told him that
an internal house rule limited the offer to women. On the second, it relied on his knowledge of that rule from
the previous encounter. Neither published advertisement stated the restriction. The trial court's supported
findings established Lefkowitz's compliance with the advertised conditions; the Supreme Court therefore
examined the legal consequences of that conduct.
Lefkowitz sued for breach of contract in the Municipal Court of Minneapolis. The trial court rejected his
claim for the value of the coats because the only evidence of their value was the uncertain upper limit stated
in the first advertisement. A statement that the coats were worth up to $100 did not establish what any
particular coat was actually worth. The stole's advertised value was a definite $139.50, and the court
accepted that figure as established on this record. It awarded that value less the one-dollar purchase price,
producing $138.50. The store moved for amended findings or, alternatively, a new trial; the municipal court
denied the motion, and the store appealed that denial. 251 Minn. at 189-90, 86 N.W.2d at 690.
The formation issue was whether the advertisement itself committed the store to sell on stated terms when a
customer performed the requested acts, or instead invited customers to submit purchase offers that the store
remained free to reject. The related acceptance issue was whether Lefkowitz's arrival and offer to pay
satisfied the advertisement. The store relied on decisions treating advertisements as invitations to bargain
and argued that its advertised proposal could be withdrawn without notice. It also argued that the house rule
restricted which customers could obtain the bargain. Lefkowitz's position depended on the public
advertisement's definite terms and his performance of the acts it requested.

Lefkowitz v. Great Minneapolis Surplus Store, Inc. (continued)
The court recognized that ordinary advertisements often invite buyers to make offers. The legal character of
a particular advertisement nevertheless depends on its language and surrounding circumstances. Here the
advertisement identified a particular article, supplied an exact price, fixed the time, and identified the
eligible purchaser through the order of arrival. Those provisions supplied a way to determine both the store's
obligation and the person entitled to enforce it. The court treated a public advertisement as an offer when it
was sufficiently definite and left no further negotiation necessary to complete the transaction. A customer's
response then could form the contract without a further discretionary approval by the store. 251 Minn. at
190-92, 86 N.W.2d at 690-91.
Lefkowitz accepted by arriving first as requested and offering the stated purchase price. The opinion does
not require that the merchant first take his money: the findings established his readiness and offer to pay,
followed by the merchant's refusal. The store's requested conduct supplied an objective method of
acceptance. Specifying one stole and first-come, first-served availability also made the extent of the
promised sale ascertainable. As an analytical explanation of the holding, those terms address the concern
that an advertisement might otherwise expose a merchant to obligations exceeding its stock. The court's
actual holding rests on this advertisement and Lefkowitz's compliance with it.
The house rule failed because it added a condition after Lefkowitz had accepted the published offer. The
court recognized an advertiser's ability to modify an offer before acceptance but held that the advertiser
could not impose new or arbitrary conditions afterward. Lefkowitz's prior awareness that the store had
asserted a women-only rule did not persuade the court to insert that restriction into the second published
advertisement. The opinion resolved the dispute through the terms and timing of contract formation; it did
not decide a statutory sex-discrimination claim. 251 Minn. at 192, 86 N.W.2d at 691.
The case also separates the existence of an obligation from proof of recoverable loss. The court's refusal to
award the claimed coat value rested on uncertain valuation, while the stole award used the established value
minus the amount Lefkowitz would have paid. That calculation gives him the economic value of the
promised bargain. The opinion does not establish a universal rule that an advertiser's stated value
conclusively fixes damages in every case. It accepts the stole's value on this record and treats the coats'
stated maximum as insufficient proof.
For class analysis, identify the exact words that give the customer power to complete the bargain, the
conduct those words require, and the time at which that conduct occurs. A later refusal to perform an already
formed contract has different consequences from a timely withdrawal of an outstanding offer. In Lefkowitz,
the advertised terms made that sequence decisive. The opinion contains no separate concurrence or dissent.
The full decision is available at Lefkowitz, 251 Minn. 188, 86 N.W.2d 689.

Wucherpfennig v. Dooley
351 N.W.2d 443 (1984). Supreme Court of North Dakota. Sand, J. Decided July 11, 1984.
The North Dakota Supreme Court held that Donald Wucherpfennig failed to accept Elizabeth Dooley's offer
to sell her interest in inherited farmland before she revoked it. The response sent through the estate's
attorney expressed readiness to proceed and sought further information, but it did not communicate a
definite present commitment to buy on the offered terms. The court affirmed dismissal of Donald's claim for
specific performance, the remedy that would have required Elizabeth to convey her interest. Donald was the
plaintiff and appellant; Elizabeth, his sister, was the defendant and appellee. Their sister Louise Grettum and
persons claiming interests in the land were also named defendants, although the disputed sale concerned
Elizabeth's share. 351 N.W.2d at 443-45.
The siblings inherited a family farm consisting of four quarters of land after their father died in 1964 and
their mother died in 1977. Donald received an undivided two-thirds interest in the quarter containing the
farmstead, while Elizabeth and Louise each received an undivided one-sixth interest in that quarter. Each
sibling received an undivided one-third interest in each of the other three quarters. The unequal fractional
interests matter because the parties were negotiating over Elizabeth's total ownership interest, and a price per
acre had to be translated into the amount due for that interest.
During probate of their mother's estate, Elizabeth indicated that she wanted to sell her share to Donald. On
January 4, 1979, she wrote Robert Case, the attorney handling the probate, offering to sell at $200 per acre if
Donald wanted to buy, provided the transaction was for cash and handled promptly. The parties did not
dispute that this letter was an offer. Case replied on January 13 that Donald was interested in purchasing her
interest. That expression of interest preceded the communication on which Donald ultimately relied as his
acceptance.
On February 17, Case wrote that Donald had arranged financing through the Federal Land Bank and that
they were ready to proceed with the transaction. He then asked Elizabeth to state the exact amount she
expected for her interest in the land. He also asked whether she would sign an agreement relating to Special
Use Valuation and requested a response on both subjects. Elizabeth did not answer. On March 9, she
revoked her offer to sell at $200 per acre. Donald contended that the February 17 letter had already created a
contract and that the revocation therefore came too late. 351 N.W.2d at 443-44.
Donald sought specific performance of the alleged sale contract and, alternatively, partition of the jointly
owned property. Partition would address the co-owners' interests independently of the claimed agreement to
buy Elizabeth's share. By consent, the parties tried the specific-performance claim first. The district court
found that Donald had not accepted Elizabeth's offer and that, even if an acceptance existed, the supposed
contract could not be specifically enforced. It gave four independently sufficient grounds for dismissal. On
appeal, Donald therefore needed to defeat every independent ground to obtain reversal. The Supreme Court
agreed with the lack-of-acceptance ground and expressly declined to decide the remaining issues. Its
affirmance concerned dismissal of specific performance; the reported decision does not resolve the
alternative partition claim. 351 N.W.2d at 444.

Wucherpfennig v. Dooley (continued)
The controlling question was whether the February 17 letter expressed assent that would itself complete the
bargain on Elizabeth's terms, or whether it contemplated further negotiation before either side became
bound. Applying N.D. Century Code § 9-03-21 and its precedents, the court required an absolute,
unequivocal, and unconditional acceptance. The offer and acceptance had to concern the same bargain, and
the response had to express an intention to create the contract through that response itself. The court drew
that formulation from Cooke v. Blood Systems, Inc., Grossman v. McLeish Ranch, Greenberg v. Stewart,
and Minnesota decisions including Markmann v. H.A. Bruntjen Co. and Minar v. Skoog.
The court read the letter as a whole. Arranging financing showed that Donald was preparing to buy, and
readiness to proceed showed continued interest. The immediate request for the amount Elizabeth expected,
however, indicated that a material aspect of the proposed exchange remained open. The court understood the
letter as movement toward a future agreement. Donald had not unequivocally committed himself to the
transaction described in Elizabeth's offer. The letter's additional inquiry concerning Special Use Valuation
formed part of the correspondence, but the court did not announce a separate holding that such an inquiry
automatically defeats acceptance in every transaction. 351 N.W.2d at 444.
Donald's own trial testimony strengthened that reading. When asked why the February 17 letter did not
specify a total amount, he said the amount would have been $37,200 and acknowledged that Elizabeth
would have expected more. He also said that she would not have signed a contract stating the figure he
intended to pay. The court used that testimony as further support for its conclusion that the parties had not
assented to the same terms. The problem was therefore more substantial than leaving out a mechanical
calculation that both parties understood identically. Donald knew that the figure he intended to pay would
not secure Elizabeth's agreement. 351 N.W.2d at 444-45.
The opinion should be applied with care to a response that both accepts and requests information. A
response can accept an offer while also asking for a change or clarification, if assent is complete and does
not depend on the offeror agreeing to the request. The Restatement expresses that distinction in §§ 59 and
61: a response conditioning assent on different terms is a counteroffer, while a completed acceptance may
accompany a nonconditional request. In Wucherpfennig, the court found the threshold commitment missing.
It did not hold that every question following words of acceptance destroys the contract. Nor did it need to
classify the February 17 letter as a definite counteroffer; continued negotiations were enough to explain why
it was ineffective as an acceptance.
The transaction also illustrates the limit of reliance as a formation argument. Donald's financing
arrangements showed preparation, but the court did not treat them as acceptance of Elizabeth's cash-sale
offer. It resolved the case on the correspondence and supporting testimony, without deciding the district
court's other grounds. The reported opinion contains no separate dissent; the other justices concurred. The
full opinion is available at Wucherpfennig v. Dooley, 351 N.W.2d 443.

International Filter Co. v. Conroe Gin, Ice & Light Co.
277 S.W. 631 (1925). Texas Commission of Appeals, Section A. Nickels, J. Decided December 10, 1925; disposition adopted
by the Texas Supreme Court.
The court held that Conroe Gin's signed order authorized International Filter to complete the contract
through approval by an executive officer at its Chicago office. The officer's dated approval satisfied that
specified method, and the writing did not require separate notice of approval. The court also concluded that
the company's subsequent acknowledgment sufficiently communicated approval if notice was required.
International Filter, an Illinois manufacturer of water-purification equipment with its principal office in
Chicago, was the plaintiff and party seeking review. Conroe Gin, a Texas company manufacturing ice in
Conroe, was the defendant. International Filter prevailed on the formation issues, but the case returned to the
Court of Civil Appeals for unresolved questions; the reviewing court did not enter a final damages award.
277 S.W. at 631-33.
On February 10, 1920, International Filter's traveling representative, W. W. Waterman, presented a written
proposal to Conroe Gin's manager, Henry Thompson. The proposal described a No. 2 Junior steel-tank water
softener and filter for $1,230, to be furnished f.o.b. Chicago and suitable for water whose characteristics
would be determined from a sample. The decisive language provided that the proposal would become a
contract when accepted by the purchaser and approved by an executive officer of International Filter at its
Chicago office. The form also required modifications to be made through an approved supplementary
agreement signed by both parties and stated that the proposal was submitted for prompt acceptance, with
change without notice possible if prompt acceptance did not occur.
Thompson signed the paper for Conroe Gin on February 10, using an acceptance notation. The document
also requested shipment by March 10. It then went to International Filter's Chicago office. On February 13,
P. N. Engel, identified in the opinion as the company's president and vice president, wrote a dated approval
on the paper. On February 14, International Filter mailed a letter acknowledging and thanking Conroe Gin
for the order, identifying the equipment and its electrical specifications, and referring to March 10 shipment.
The letter asked Conroe Gin to send a water sample promptly so the manufacturer could analyze the water
before shipping the equipment. Conroe Gin received that letter in due course. 277 S.W. at 631-32.
Conroe Gin attempted to cancel the order in a February 28 letter and reiterated that position on March 4.
International Filter answered on March 2, denied a right to cancel, and insisted on performance. It then sued
for breach. Conroe Gin argued that neither Engel's endorsement nor the February 14 letter constituted the
executive approval contemplated by the proposal. It further argued that notice of approval was essential and
had not been given. Thompson testified that, when he signed the order, he had told Waterman that prompt
notification was required. The defense therefore relied both on its reading of the form and on a claimed oral
instruction accompanying the signature.
The trial court heard the case without a jury and entered judgment for Conroe Gin, finding in its favor on the
formation issues and other material issues. The Court of Civil Appeals affirmed at 269 S.W. 210. That court
nevertheless agreed that Engel's endorsement was an approval within the document's terms; the principal
remaining obstacle was the asserted need for notice. International Filter sought further review. The
Commission of Appeals recommended reversal of the intermediate appellate judgment and remand to that
court for questions properly presented but not previously decided. Chief Justice Cureton, for the Texas
Supreme Court, adopted that disposition. This procedural endpoint matters because a favorable ruling on
contract formation did not resolve every issue in the breach action.

International Filter Co. v. Conroe Gin, Ice & Light Co. (continued)
The first analytical step is to identify who made the legally operative offer. International Filter prepared the
form and called it a proposal, while Conroe Gin signed beneath language of acceptance. Even so, the form
reserved a further act of approval for International Filter's executive officer. Conroe Gin's signature
therefore supplied the offer to purchase on those terms. The company became the offeree with authority to
accept through the reserved approval. The court expressly treated the signed paper sent to Chicago as
Conroe Gin's offer. Drafting the form and making the operative offer were separate roles. 277 S.W. at 632.
The court agreed that Engel's dated endorsement constituted approval by an executive officer at the
designated place. No additional ritual was necessary to make that endorsement an approval. The remaining
question was whether the contract also required communication of that approval to Conroe Gin. Although
notice ordinarily protects an offeror who seeks a return promise, the offeror may dispense with it by
authorizing a particular act as sufficient acceptance. Drawing on Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co., the
court reasoned that the offeree completes the required acceptance by following the specified method when
the offer makes that method sufficient.
The signed writing said that the proposal became a contract upon the executive officer's approval in
Chicago. Requiring notice to arrive in Conroe would add another condition and change both the designated
time and place of formation. The separate prompt-acceptance provision addressed the timing of the required
approval; it did not prescribe an additional form of acceptance or a notice requirement. The court treated the
writing as unambiguous and judged the offer by those written terms despite the claimed oral demand for
notification. Its reasoning supports a contractual waiver of notice in this transaction, while leaving the
ordinary notice rule applicable when the offer does not dispense with it. 277 S.W. at 632-33.
The court supplied an alternative ground concerning the February 14 letter. Approval had already occurred
through Engel's endorsement; the letter's possible role was to notify Conroe Gin of that existing act. A notice
can communicate acceptance by reasonable implication without repeating a particular formula. The
acknowledgment thanked the buyer for the specified order, identified the planned shipment, and requested
the water sample needed for performance. The court concluded that a reasonable recipient in Conroe Gin's
position would understand it to communicate approval. Its additional observation that reliance on the letter
could have prevented International Filter from denying approval was hypothetical; the reported facts do not
establish that Conroe Gin actually changed position in such reliance.
The narrow formation holding depends on the particular approval clause and the occurrence of the required
executive act. An offeree's unexpressed decision to agree would not satisfy the prescribed act merely
because the offer waived notification. Engel made an observable written endorsement of the document itself.
This explains the comparison with White: the controlling question in each case is whether the offeree
performed an act that the offer and circumstances made sufficient to express assent. In International Filter,
the writing expressly selected the act that completed the bargain; in White, the builder's general preparations
did not communicate acceptance of the proposed job.
The case concerns common-law formation in a 1920 transaction and should be stated in those terms. A
modern sale of equipment would also require attention to the applicable UCC provisions and to whether the
language unambiguously restricts the means of acceptance. The available online opinion text is linked at
International Filter, 277 S.W. 631; that public text ends during the reasoning. The later reasoning and
remand described here appear in the supplied seventh edition, printed pages 149-151, PDF pages 199-201.

White v. Corlies & Tifft
46 N.Y. 467 (1871). Court of Appeals of New York. Folger, J. Decided November 20, 1871. Also reported as White v. Corlies;
the supplied casebook spells Tift with one final f.
The New York Court of Appeals held that White's private decision to accept a proposed office-fitting job,
followed by buying and working on general-purpose materials, did not sufficiently manifest acceptance to
the merchants who proposed the job. The trial judge had incorrectly instructed the jury that White could
bind the merchants by acting on their note and beginning the job without indicating assent to them. The
Court of Appeals reversed the judgment for White and ordered a new trial, with costs to depend on the
eventual outcome. White was the builder, plaintiff, and respondent on appeal; Corlies and his associates
were the merchants, defendants, and appellants. 46 N.Y. at 467-70.
White operated his building business on Fortieth Street in New York City. The merchants conducted
business on Dey Street and wanted a suite of offices at 57 Broadway fitted up. In September 1865, they gave
White specifications and asked him to estimate the cost. He delivered his estimate on September 28, after
which the merchants were to consider it and communicate their decision. They changed the specifications
that day and sent the revised version to White to obtain his assent under the estimate. White signed his
assent to the changes and returned the specifications. That preliminary agreement about specifications
formed part of the negotiations; the Court of Appeals found no completed agreement before the later note.
The next day, the merchants' bookkeeper sent White a note dated September 29. It stated that, upon an
agreement to finish fitting up the offices within two weeks from that date, he could begin at once. The writer
also said he would probably call again between five and six that afternoon. White did not reply. Immediately
after receiving the note, he bought lumber and began working on it. The merchants countermanded the
proposal the following day. White sued for damages on the theory that his conduct had accepted the
undertaking before the countermand arrived.
The supplied casebook adds details from White's testimony in the record on appeal. The merchants had
initially wanted the offices finished in black walnut within twenty-one days; White said he could not do that
hardwood work within that period. They then requested an estimate for white pine. White's September 28
estimate did not specify a completion time. The subsequent countermand requested another estimate for
black walnut. Those record details help explain why agreement about specifications and price did not
necessarily settle the entire undertaking. They supplement the published opinion and are identified here as
casebook reporting of the appellate record. The opinion itself refers once to a September 30 communication;
the casebook notes that the court apparently meant the September 29 note.
At trial, the judge told the jury that White did not have to return to the merchants and state that he had
begun, and that he could act on the note and commence the job. The defendants took an exception to that
instruction, preserving the claimed legal error. The jury found for White. The General Term of the First
Judicial District affirmed the trial judgment, and the defendants appealed to the Court of Appeals. The issue
on that appeal was whether the instruction treated White's decision and preparations as sufficient acceptance
when his conduct had not communicated assent or been placed in an appropriate course of communication.
The disposition was a new trial, rather than a final appellate award to either party. 46 N.Y. at 467-70.

White v. Corlies & Tifft (continued)
The court first treated the merchants' note as a proposal requiring White's acceptance. It found no earlier
contract in the evidence or the jury's findings. The court then stated that an offer made when the parties are
apart requires acceptance through an appropriate outward act. The act need not always have reached the
offeror's actual knowledge before a contract forms. It must, however, be placed in a proper course of
communication so that it will ordinarily reach the offeror within a reasonable time. A mailed acceptance
illustrates the distinction: dispatch may complete the contract even before delivery because the offeree has
taken an objectively communicative step. 46 N.Y. at 469.
White's conduct failed that test. The lumber he purchased was suitable for other comparable jobs, and the
work he performed on it resembled the work he would do for those jobs. His acts therefore did not identify
this proposed undertaking as the one he had accepted. His private intention could explain the acts to himself,
but it did not give the merchants an outward indication of assent. The court found nothing in his conduct that
communicated acceptance, set such communication in motion, or would necessarily result in
communication to the merchants. His expenditures and labor thus established preparation without
establishing the acceptance required on this record. 46 N.Y. at 469-70.
White's strongest argument arose from the note's permission to begin immediately: a builder could
reasonably understand an instruction to start as inviting responsive conduct. The court nevertheless
examined what White actually did and how those acts would manifest assent. It did not establish a rule that
conduct can never accept an offer. Nor did it hold that actual receipt of notice is always essential, since its
mailed-letter example expressly recognized formation before receipt. Its conclusion was that these general
preparations, accompanied by an uncommunicated intention, were insufficient under this proposal.
The casebook identifies a genuine interpretive question concerning the note's opening phrase. It could
require White to agree to the newly stated completion period before starting, or it could refer to the proposed
work while authorizing commencement. The Court of Appeals treated the note as an offer but did not
develop every possible reading of that phrase. Work at the specified offices, an unequivocal return message,
or other conduct objectively directed to this job could present a different acceptance question. Those are
analytical variations; the court did not decide them. The brief's holding should remain tied to the generic
lumber and uncommunicated work in the actual record.
Under the later Restatement framework, the initial inquiry would be whether the offer invited a promise,
performance, or either. Section 62 may make the beginning of invited performance an acceptance carrying a
promise to complete; § 45 may protect an offeree who begins performance when only completed
performance is invited as acceptance. Both inquiries require identifying the work actually requested and
distinguishing its beginning from preparation. Sections 54 and 56 separately address notice. These later
provisions help organize a modern analysis of similar facts, but the 1871 court did not apply them.
The decision supports an objective account of assent: an act must obtain its contractual meaning from the
communication and circumstances, and a hidden intention cannot supply the missing outward meaning. The
justices concurred in reversal, with Justice Allen not voting. The opinion is available at White v. Corlies, 46
N.Y. 467. The fuller factual statement and the appellate-record note appear in the supplied casebook at
printed pages 152-154, PDF pages 202-204.

Silence not ordinarily acceptance
Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 69; casebook printed pages 166-167, PDF pages 216-217.
Silence acquires contractual significance when the circumstances reasonably give it the meaning of assent.
An unsolicited offer, standing alone, ordinarily leaves the recipient free to do nothing. The offeror cannot
impose a duty to reject merely by announcing that silence will count as acceptance. This rule protects the
recipient's control over whether to enter a bargain and avoids making every uninvited proposal a demand for
action. The assigned discussion uses an offer declaring acceptance automatic after forty-eight hours of
silence to illustrate that limit. Restatement § 69; casebook at 166.
Section 69(1)(a) addresses the knowing acceptance of services. An offeree who takes the benefit of offered
services, has a reasonable opportunity to reject them, and has reason to know that the provider expects
compensation may accept through that conduct. For example, a person who knowingly permits requested
paid work to continue for that person's benefit can communicate assent without a spoken promise. The
opportunity to reject and the reason to understand that payment is expected are essential. If someone
provides an unavoidable benefit without giving the recipient a meaningful choice, those facts require a
different analysis; liability cannot be inferred simply from the existence of a benefit.
Section 69(1)(b) concerns an offeror who gives the offeree reason to understand that assent may be
expressed through silence or inaction. In this situation, silence operates as acceptance only when the offeree
intends to accept. The offer can authorize silence as a convenient way of accepting, but it cannot force an
unwilling recipient into a bargain. The offeree's intention therefore has a specified role within this exception.
The requirement differs from a general rule under which an offeror could impose contractual consequences
by setting an arbitrary deadline for rejection.
Section 69(1)(c) concerns previous dealings and other circumstances that make it reasonable for the offeree
to notify the offeror if the offeree intends to decline. A repeated practice can establish what an unspoken
response means between these parties. If a supplier regularly sends goods of an agreed kind and the buyer
regularly accepts and pays, the supplier may reasonably understand the same pattern to continue unless the
buyer objects. The relevant question is the meaning created by the parties' relationship and current conduct.
A party's undisclosed intention to reject may fail to overcome conduct that objectively communicates
acceptance under that established practice.
The casebook's principal illustration is Hobbs v. Massasoit Whip Co., 158 Mass. 194, 33 N.E. 495 (1893).
Hobbs sought $108.50 for 2,350 eel skins sent to a whip manufacturer. He had sent skins in the same
manner four or five times before, and the manufacturer had accepted and paid for them. In the disputed
transaction, the company retained the skins for months without giving Hobbs notice that it declined them,
and the skins were eventually destroyed. Justice Holmes explained that the established dealings and
retention could support a jury finding of acceptance. The prior transactions gave the shipment an understood
commercial context, and prolonged retention without objection reinforced that meaning. The court overruled
the defendant's exceptions, leaving the plaintiff's recovery intact. 158 Mass. at 196-97, 33 N.E. at 495.

The precise significance of Hobbs is evidentiary: those circumstances permitted the jury to infer assent. The
decision does not establish that delivery of any unrequested goods automatically produces a contract. The
defendant's established relationship with Hobbs and its handling of the shipment supplied the additional
facts. The opinion's objective approach also explains why a recipient cannot always avoid liability by
asserting that it silently meant to refuse. When conduct reasonably indicates assent in the relationship the
parties have created, an undisclosed reservation may carry little weight. The opinion is available at Hobbs,
158 Mass. 194.
The casebook gives a second illustration involving a seller's response to orders. In American Bronze Corp. v.
Streamway Products, 8 Ohio App. 3d 223, 456 N.E.2d 1295, 1300 (1982), the parties had used a practice for
more than twenty years under which the buyer telephoned orders, followed with written purchase orders,
and the seller began production. When the seller refused three orders, the appellate court reversed and
remanded after the buyer's claim had been denied. The established method of filling orders supported the
buyer's belief that production had begun absent notice of rejection. The court relied on UCC § 2-204(1),
which recognizes formation through conduct demonstrating agreement. This account follows the assigned
casebook note; it illustrates how prior practice can supply meaning to a seller's silence as well as a buyer's.
Casebook at 167.
Section 69(2) separately addresses the exercise of ownership over offered property. An offeree who acts
inconsistently with the offeror's ownership can become bound on the offered terms unless those terms are
manifestly unreasonable. If the act is wrongful against the offeror, it operates as acceptance only if the
offeror ratifies it. Using or disposing of offered property may thus carry a contractual consequence, while
simple possession requires attention to the circumstances. A court must also consider any statute that
changes the common-law result.
For merchandise mailed without the recipient's prior expressed request or consent, federal law provides a
specific protection: qualifying unordered merchandise may be retained, used, discarded, or otherwise treated
as a gift without an obligation to the sender. That statute prevents a sender from turning an unsolicited
mailing into a bill merely because the recipient keeps or uses it. A subscription arrangement the recipient
has already accepted presents a different consent question, because a later delivery may occur under that
existing agreement. Whether a particular renewal arrangement is lawful also depends on applicable
consumer-protection rules. 39 U.S.C. § 3009(a)-(d).
In applying this topic, specify which facts make silence meaningful: knowingly taking paid services,
intending to use a permitted silent method of acceptance, an established course of dealings, or exercising
ownership over offered goods. Then identify what the recipient could reasonably have understood and
whether a governing statute changes the result. That method ties the conclusion to consent and conduct
rather than treating silence as having the same meaning in every transaction.

The significance of contract formation for antidiscrimination law
Casebook printed page 156, PDF page 206; 42 U.S.C. § 1981; Perry v. Command Performance and Hampton v. Dillard
Department Stores.
Contract formation can determine the existence and scope of a protected civil right. Section 1981 guarantees
equal rights to make and enforce contracts and reaches racial discrimination in private contracting. Its
current definition covers formation, performance, modification, termination, and enjoyment of the
contractual relationship's benefits, privileges, terms, and conditions. A claimant may therefore challenge
interference with an existing contract or discriminatory obstruction of a proposed contract. The analysis
must identify the contractual activity or right affected and connect its impairment to intentional racial
discrimination. 42 U.S.C. § 1981(a)-(c).
The casebook's example, Perry v. Command Performance, 913 F.2d 99 (3d Cir. 1990), arose under an
earlier interpretation of § 1981. In Patterson v. McLean Credit Union, 491 U.S. 164 (1989), the Supreme
Court limited the statute's protection concerning the making of contracts and generally excluded
discriminatory conduct occurring afterward during performance. That interpretation made the instant of
formation decisive in some service transactions. Congress subsequently expanded the statute through the
Civil Rights Act of 1991. The chronological distinction is necessary to understand both the 1990 decision
and the rule applicable today.
Edith Perry, a Black woman, had used the Command Performance salon in Pennsylvania's King of Prussia
Mall more than five times. On October 12, 1987, her husband telephoned the salon and arranged a noon
appointment for her wash and set. The full opinion identifies her husband as the caller, although the
casebook compresses that detail. When Perry arrived, Helene Kugler, who had previously styled her hair,
explained that she had a bad cold and asked whether another hairdresser could do the work. Perry agreed.
According to Perry's complaint and deposition, the substitute, Beth Abbott, loudly refused because Perry
was Black and made explicitly racial statements about whom she would serve. Perry became distressed,
summoned mall security, and left with her husband. Perry, 913 F.2d at 100.
Perry sued the salon under § 1981 and Pennsylvania's law of intentional infliction of emotional distress. The
district court granted summary judgment for the salon because it assumed the telephone appointment had
already created a contract on racially neutral terms. Under its reading of Patterson, the subsequent refusal
therefore constituted conduct outside the statute's contract-making protection. Perry appealed. The Third
Circuit reviewed summary judgment de novo, meaning that it independently applied the governing legal
standard, with the evidence and reasonable inferences viewed favorably to Perry as the nonmoving party.
The appellate court vacated the order and remanded for further proceedings; it did not find the salon liable or
award damages. 913 F.2d at 100-02.
The first problem was the absence of a sufficient factual basis for treating the telephone appointment as a
completed contract. An appointment might reserve an opportunity to negotiate or obtain service, with
contractual acceptance occurring when the service begins. Alternatively, the call might exchange present
commitments to provide and pay for the service. The record did not establish which meaning applied. The
Third Circuit directed further consideration of industry practice and the parties' expectations. It did not
announce that every appointment is merely preliminary or that no appointment can create a contract. It held
that the district court could not assume formation at the telephone call on this record. 913 F.2d at 101-02.

The second problem remained even if the appointment formed a contract. The salon might have agreed to
serve a Black customer only if a willing hairdresser happened to be available, while undertaking to serve a
white customer without that racial condition. If evidence established such unequal terms at formation, the
claim could fit § 1981 even under Patterson. The court therefore required an opportunity to develop
evidence concerning the terms of the supposed agreement. It also left unresolved whether the conduct could
be attributed to the salon and whether the required intentional discrimination occurred. A factual dispute
over whether Kugler offered to serve Perry after Abbott's outburst likewise remained in the record. 913 F.2d
at 102 & n.1.
Congress's 1991 amendment added § 1981(b), expressly extending the statute to performance and the
enjoyment of contractual benefits. For a comparable transaction governed by that amended provision, an
earlier contract no longer excludes a claim simply because the refusal occurs during performance. Formation
continues to matter because the claimant must identify a relevant contractual relationship or attempted
transaction and the particular right impaired. The amendment changes the consequences of the timing
analysis without eliminating the need to examine the transaction. The full Perry opinion is available at 913
F.2d 99.
The casebook also cites Hampton v. Dillard Department Stores, Inc., 247 F.3d 1091 (10th Cir. 2001), which
illustrates the importance of identifying the contract and its benefits after the amendment. Paula Hampton
bought children's clothing at Dillard's. The sales clerk gave Hampton and her niece, Demetria Cooper,
coupons for fragrance samples. As they attempted to redeem the coupons, a security officer interrupted them
and examined Hampton's shopping bag on suspicion of shoplifting; the goods matched her receipt. Their
litigation included § 1981 claims concerning discriminatory interference with obtaining the samples. The
contractual question was whether receipt of a sample formed part of the purchase's benefits, even though no
separate price was charged for it. 247 F.3d at 1099-1105.
Hampton's sample claim proceeded to a jury, which found that the sample was a benefit of her purchase and
that the store intentionally interfered with her ability to obtain it on racial grounds. The Tenth Circuit upheld
her judgment. Cooper's claim had a different result because she had neither made nor attempted a purchase
and lacked an enforceable third-party-beneficiary interest under the court's analysis. Receiving a coupon and
accompanying a purchaser did not, on that record, establish her own protected contractual interest. The
contrast turns on the legal relationship each plaintiff established. The court's disposition concerning the two
plaintiffs shows why identifying the contracting party and the promised benefit remains consequential. 247
F.3d at 1101-05, 1117-19.
For present-day use, the older opinions also require a causation qualification. Comcast Corp. v. National
Ass'n of African American-Owned Media, 589 U.S. 327 (2020), holds that a § 1981 plaintiff must plead and
prove that race was a but-for cause of the injury. That requires a showing that the relevant injury would not
have occurred in the absence of the racial discrimination; it does not require race to be the only cause. The
earlier Hampton opinion's discussion of a motivating-factor instruction should therefore not be copied as the
current general causation standard. The current qualification is supplemental to the assigned formation topic.
Comcast, 589 U.S. at 341.
The contracts-class inquiry begins with what the parties proposed and promised, what conduct expressed
assent, when any agreement formed, and which benefits followed from that agreement. The civil-rights
inquiry then asks whether intentional racial discrimination impaired a protected contractual activity under
the statute applicable to the conduct. A refusal can prevent formation and still support a statutory claim
concerning the right to contract. Conversely, a completed sale may retain unperformed benefits whose
discriminatory denial remains actionable. Hampton, 247 F.3d 1091; 42 U.S.C. § 1981.

Termination of the power of acceptance
Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§ 25, 36-48, 59, 61, 87; UCC § 2-205; casebook printed pages 167-185, PDF pages
217-235.
The power of acceptance is the offeree's ability to complete the proposed bargain by accepting. That power
lasts only while the offer remains legally available. Under Restatement § 36, rejection or counteroffer, lapse
of time, revocation, and death or incapacity can terminate it. Failure of a condition governing acceptance can
also end the power. An effective acceptance must therefore occur before a terminating event, unless a rule
protecting an option or another irrevocable offer changes the result. The analysis requires a chronological
account of communications and conduct, together with the rule that determines when each event becomes
legally effective.
An offer lapses at its stated expiration time. If it gives no time, it remains open for a reasonable period
determined by its terms and surrounding circumstances. Relevant considerations include the subject matter,
price fluctuations, the communication method, the parties' prior dealings, and the apparent urgency of the
transaction. An offer made during a face-to-face conversation ordinarily expires when the conversation ends
unless the circumstances indicate continuing availability. In the casebook's example, Akers v. J.B. Sedberry,
Inc., 286 S.W.2d 617 (Tenn. Ct. App. 1955), an employer could not wait several days and then accept an
employee's oral resignation offer made during a concluded meeting. Restatement § 41; casebook at 168.
Reasonable duration also depends on the offer's purpose. In Loring v. City of Boston, 48 Mass. (7 Met.) 409
(1844), the casebook describes a reward advertised in response to a period of arson. A claimant sought the
reward for apprehending an offender three years and eight months later. The court concluded that the offer
had lapsed because the original emergency and public notice did not reasonably support continuing
availability that long. Other reward offers may contemplate future events over a longer period. The lesson is
to explain why the particular offer's context supports a particular duration. Casebook at 169, 174-75.
Lapse occurs through expiration without requiring a new communication from the offeror. A response after
lapse ordinarily cannot accept the expired offer, but it may itself operate as a new offer if it sufficiently
proposes a bargain. The original offeror can then accept that new proposal. Whether a delayed
communication creates a contract thus may depend on subsequent conduct and on whether the parties
renewed the offer, rather than on a power to erase the expiration retrospectively. A stated deadline also must
be read with the applicable rule concerning dispatch or receipt of acceptance. Restatement §§ 35, 41;
casebook at 169-70, 187-88.
Revocation occurs when the offeror withdraws an offer before effective acceptance. For a direct revocation
under § 42, the offeree must receive a communication indicating that the offeror no longer intends to enter
the proposed bargain. Dispatch alone ordinarily does not terminate the power. An uncommunicated change
of mind likewise leaves the outward offer in place. The offeror need not use a technical word if the
communication reasonably conveys withdrawal. Once acceptance has already formed a contract, an
attempted withdrawal raises questions about the existing contractual duties rather than the availability of an
outstanding offer.
An ordinary common-law promise to keep an offer open until a particular date may remain revocable before
that date if no consideration or other rule makes the promise enforceable. A stated acceptance deadline
establishes the latest time for acceptance; a binding commitment to maintain availability additionally
restricts revocation. In Dickinson v. Dodds, 2 Ch. D. 463 (C.A. 1876), the seller's promise to leave an offer
open did not create a binding option, and the buyer learned before his attempted acceptance that the seller
had disposed of the property to another person. The casebook uses the decision to connect revocability with
indirect communication. Casebook at 171-75.

Section 43 recognizes indirect revocation when the offeror takes definite action inconsistent with an
intention to enter the proposed contract and the offeree acquires reliable information of that action. A
reliable report of a completed sale to another buyer can satisfy that rule for an offer to sell a particular
parcel. Rumor, an ambiguous statement that the seller is considering other buyers, or information that the
offeree never receives presents different facts. The required analysis includes both the inconsistent action
and the offeree's reliable information about it. A secret sale alone does not automatically establish the
offeree's knowledge.
A public offer can require a different method of withdrawal because the offeror may be unable to identify
everyone who learned of it. Under § 46, an offer made by general public announcement can be revoked
through publicity comparable to the original announcement when no better means is reasonably available. A
person who saw the original announcement may therefore lose the power to accept even without seeing the
revocation. The adequacy of the withdrawal depends on the original publication, the available alternatives,
and the circumstances. This qualification matters when applying advertisement and reward cases such as
Lefkowitz. Casebook at 174-75.
An option contract is an enforceable promise that limits the offeror's power to revoke an offer. A separate
bargained-for payment for keeping an offer open is the familiar example. Restatement § 87(1)(a) also
recognizes, subject to its conditions and jurisdictional adoption, a signed written offer that recites purported
consideration and proposes a fair exchange within a reasonable time. The casebook notes disagreement
among courts concerning an unpaid recital of consideration. The existence of an option must therefore be
established from the relevant facts and law. If an option exists, it protects the opportunity to accept
according to its terms; it does not itself complete the underlying purchase. Restatement §§ 25, 87; casebook
at 176-77.
For goods, UCC § 2-205 makes certain merchant offers irrevocable without consideration. The offer must
be in a signed writing and give assurance that it will be held open. The assurance binds for the stated time or,
if no time is stated, a reasonable time, with a maximum of three months of irrevocability under this
provision. If the assurance appears on a form supplied by the offeree, the offeror must separately sign that
assurance. The three-month limit concerns protection supplied by this rule without consideration; a
separately supported option can have a longer duration. Expiration of the protected period may leave an
offer revocable but still open if it has not otherwise expired or terminated. UCC § 2-205.
The casebook also identifies New York's broader statutory exception. Subject to the UCC's rule for
merchant offers involving goods, General Obligations Law § 5-1109 enforces a signed written assurance
that an offer is irrevocable for a specified period or until a fixed time despite the absence of consideration
for that assurance. If the writing states that the offer is irrevocable without stating a duration, it supplies a
reasonable period. A New York services or real-estate transaction may therefore require a different answer
from the ordinary common-law rule. N.Y. Gen. Oblig. Law § 5-1109; casebook at 177.
Beginning performance and reliance can also affect revocability. Under § 45, when an offer invites
acceptance only by performance, beginning or tendering the invited performance creates an option, while
the offeror's duty remains conditional on completion or tender according to the offer. Preparations do not
automatically constitute that beginning. Section 87(2) separately addresses an offer that the offeror should
reasonably expect to induce substantial action or forbearance before acceptance and that does induce it; the
offer can bind as an option to the extent necessary to avoid injustice. These doctrines require distinct
findings concerning what the offer requested, what the offeree did, and why enforcement is justified.

The casebook illustrates that distinction through Ragosta v. Wilder, 156 Vt. 390, 592 A.2d 367 (1991). The
prospective buyers arranged financing in response to a proposed cash purchase of property, but the seller
withdrew before the specified exchange at the bank. The Vermont Supreme Court treated financing
arrangements as preparation rather than the requested performance and rejected the trial court's contract and
equitable-estoppel grounds. It remanded for further consideration involving promissory estoppel. The
decision therefore separates formation by performance from possible protection of reliance. In a new
problem, a reliance argument requires its own factual and doctrinal support even when acceptance fails.
Casebook at 177-80.
Under § 48, death or loss of legal capacity of either the offeror or offeree ordinarily terminates the power to
accept an ordinary offer, even when the other party has no notice. That rule creates tension with the
objective approach to assent because a surviving offeree may reasonably believe the offer remains available.
The Restatement and casebook nevertheless identify it as the ordinary rule. Under § 37, the offeror's death or
incapacity does not ordinarily terminate the power under an option contract. Death after a completed
contract raises separate questions about surviving duties, the estate, and whether personal performance was
essential. Casebook at 180-81.
Rejection is the offeree's manifestation of an intention to decline the offer. It ordinarily terminates the power
when received, without requiring proof that the offeror relied on it. A counteroffer proposes a substituted
bargain concerning the same subject and ordinarily has the same terminating effect, unless the parties
indicate otherwise. Under the common-law mirror-image principle, a purported acceptance that makes
agreement conditional on changed terms is a counteroffer. A definite acceptance accompanied by a request
for a change may still accept if the requested change is not a condition of assent. That distinction, reflected
in §§ 59 and 61, prevents the mere presence of a question from deciding the case. Restatement §§ 38-40, 59,
61; casebook at 182-84.
Under § 37, rejection, counteroffer, revocation, or the offeror's death or incapacity ordinarily does not end
an option holder's power of acceptance, subject to rules governing discharge of the contractual duty. The
holder has an existing contractual right to the protected opportunity, and an ineffective attempt to exercise it
may leave that right available for timely, conforming exercise. Reliance, release, or other grounds of
discharge may still matter. The casebook cautions that an option supported by consideration and a statutory
merchant firm offer may require separate analysis when an offeree first rejects and then changes course. The
terms and legal basis of irrevocability should be identified before applying an exception. Casebook at
184-85.
For any termination problem, determine whether an offer existed, identify the invited form of acceptance,
establish any protection against revocation, and put the relevant events in order. Then apply the
effective-time rule to each event. A posted revocation, a received revocation, a mailed acceptance, a
rejection, and expiration of an option can have different legal dates. The mailbox rule supplies several of the
timing rules needed to finish that analysis.

The mailbox rule
Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§ 40, 63-68; casebook printed pages 185-188, PDF pages 235-238.
The mailbox rule ordinarily makes an acceptance effective when it is properly dispatched through a manner
and medium invited by the offer. Under Restatement § 63(a), the acceptance can complete mutual assent
when it leaves the offeree's possession, even if the offeror has not yet learned of it and even if it is later lost.
The rule is a default: the offer can specify another condition, such as actual receipt by a deadline. It also has
exceptions for option contracts and for communications that do not satisfy the dispatch requirements. The
essential question is when the relevant communication acquires legal effect. Restatement §§ 63, 66.
The rule gives an offeree a dependable point at which to commit. Once the offeree properly sends an
acceptance of an ordinary offer, a subsequently received revocation ordinarily comes too late. The offeror
bears the transmission risk associated with that invited method, subject to the offer's wording and the rule's
qualifications. The casebook traces the doctrine to Adams v. Lindsell, 1 B. & Ald. 681, 106 Eng. Rep. 250
(K.B. 1818), involving a mailed offer for wool and a posted acceptance. It also explains that treating the
postal service as the offeror's agent is an imperfect rationale; the more useful explanation concerns the
offeree's ability to act on a reliable legal commitment. Casebook at 185-87.
The effective-time rules are asymmetric. An offer must ordinarily reach the offeree before it creates a power
of acceptance. Revocation ordinarily takes effect upon receipt by the offeree. Rejection and counteroffer
sent from a distance ordinarily terminate the power when received by the offeror, subject to § 40's rule for a
later acceptance. A qualifying acceptance alone ordinarily receives the benefit of effectiveness on dispatch.
The fact that every communication travels by mail does not make their legal timing identical. Restatement
§§ 40, 42, 63.
Suppose a seller mails a revocation on Monday. The buyer has not received it when the buyer properly mails
an invited acceptance on Tuesday, and the revocation arrives Wednesday. Under the ordinary mailbox rule,
the contract forms Tuesday. The seller's Monday dispatch does not establish an effective revocation before
acceptance. If the buyer receives the revocation on Monday and then mails an acceptance on Tuesday, the
ordinary offer has already terminated. The sequence that matters compares legally effective events, which
can differ from the chronological order in which the parties wrote or mailed their messages.
Proper dispatch requires an appropriate destination and the ordinary precautions for safe transmission,
including correct addressing and sufficient postage when applicable. The manner and medium must also be
invited by the offer, expressly or reasonably in context. An offer inviting an urgent response may not permit
a slower method that defeats its purpose. Section 67 supplies a qualification for a seasonably but improperly
dispatched acceptance: it is treated as operative on dispatch if it actually arrives within the time in which a
properly dispatched acceptance normally would have arrived. An improperly sent message cannot receive
the ordinary dispatch protection merely because the offeree intended to accept. Restatement §§ 63(a), 65-67.

The mailbox rule (continued)
The offeror can make receipt controlling by requiring that acceptance arrive by a specified time. If an offer
requires a signed acceptance in the offeror's hands by 5 p.m. Friday, posting it Friday afternoon ordinarily
does not satisfy that requirement. By contrast, a deadline stated without specifying receipt must be
interpreted with the offer's other language, the authorized medium, and the default timing rules. A clear
receipt clause shifts the risk of delay to the accepting party and gives both parties an observable deadline.
The offeree must satisfy that condition while the offer remains available. Restatement §§ 60, 63.
Acceptance under an option contract is effective upon receipt by the offeror under § 63(b), unless the offer
provides otherwise. The holder already has a protected period to decide and does not need the ordinary
mailbox rule's protection against intervening revocation. An option expiring Friday therefore ordinarily
requires receipt of the exercise by Friday, even if a letter was properly mailed earlier. The option's terms
may alter that default. The casebook's example, Pennsylvania Academy of the Fine Arts v. Grant, 590 A.2d 9
(Pa. Super. Ct. 1991), enforced an exercise mailed within the required period under a lease provision
authorizing notices by delivery or specified forms of mail. That example makes the drafting relevant; it
should not erase the Restatement's receipt default for options. Casebook at 187.
If the offeree dispatches an effective acceptance first and later sends a rejection, the contract ordinarily
already exists. A faster rejection that arrives before the acceptance generally cannot unilaterally undo the
contract. The offeror may choose to release the offeree or agree to rescission, but that requires a further
basis. There is also a reliance qualification: if the offeror receives the overtaking rejection, reasonably relies
on it, and materially changes position before learning of the earlier acceptance, the offeree may be estopped
from enforcing the contract. Estoppel in this setting prevents the offeree from using the earlier acceptance to
defeat reasonable reliance on the offeree's later misleading communication. Restatement § 63 cmt. c;
casebook at 186.
If the offeree sends a rejection or counteroffer first and then sends an acceptance, § 40 changes the treatment
of the later message. The later acceptance is effective only if it reaches the offeror before the earlier
rejection or counteroffer. If the rejection arrives first, the later message can operate only as a counteroffer
requiring the original offeror's assent. For example, a buyer who mails a rejection Monday and an
acceptance Tuesday does not obtain the ordinary dispatch rule for Tuesday's acceptance. The order of
receipt controls under this special rule. This is why the two sequences, acceptance followed by rejection and
rejection followed by acceptance, must be analyzed separately.

The mailbox rule (continued)
For receipt, § 68 looks to possession or authorized delivery rather than requiring proof that the recipient
actually read the message. A written communication is received when it comes into the possession of the
addressee, someone authorized to receive it, or an authorized place for such communications. The same
principle explains why ignoring a properly delivered revocation cannot preserve an ordinary offer
indefinitely. The meaning of authorized delivery depends on the setting, including the recipient's
arrangements for receiving business communications. Restatement § 68.
The rule concerns formation and does not generally turn mailing into timely performance of an existing
obligation. If an established contract requires payment by a specified date, whether mailing a check by that
date constitutes payment depends on the contract and applicable payment rules. The mailbox rule for
accepting offers does not answer that question by itself. The casebook uses a tailor's unpaid bill to expose
the error of extending the dispatch rule to every communication or payment sent through the post. Casebook
at 187.
Near-instantaneous communications require care. Section 64 treats acceptance by telephone or another
medium of substantially instantaneous two-way communication under principles applicable to parties
communicating in each other's presence. Email is not governed by a universal rule that every click on send
creates a contract; its treatment depends on applicable law, the offer's terms, and the communication's
characteristics. An email requiring a signed document to be received by a stated hour can make actual
receipt decisive even when ordinary mailed acceptance would receive dispatch protection. The assigned
materials raise electronic communication as a question about the continued scope of the postal rule.
Restatement § 64; casebook at 185, 187-88.
The casebook also contrasts the United Nations Convention on Contracts for the International Sale of
Goods. Under CISG Articles 18(2) and 23, acceptance ordinarily forms the contract when the indication of
assent reaches the offeror. Article 16(1), however, ordinarily requires a revocation to reach the offeree
before the offeree dispatches acceptance. This combination can protect an acceptance in transit against
revocation even though formation awaits receipt. It demonstrates that the rules governing formation and the
ability to revoke can be separated. Application of the CISG depends on the transaction and the parties'
choice of governing rules; the ordinary domestic mailbox rule should not simply be assumed to control an
international sale. Casebook at 187.
A complete answer identifies the communication, the authorized method, whether the offer requires receipt,
whether an option exists, whether dispatch was proper, and the order of legally effective events. The
connection to White is direct: White's example of a posted acceptance shows that an outward manifestation
can become binding before actual knowledge. The connection to International Filter is the parties' ability to
specify the act that completes acceptance. The Restatement's quoted acceptance and dispatch provisions are
reproduced in Contracts Doctrine, Theory and Practice, Acceptance.
