 PART I - PROCEDURE-CENTERED CASE BRIEFS
1. World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson
444 U.S. 286 (1980). U.S. Supreme Court. White, J. No. 78-1078; argued October 3, 1979; decided January 21, 1980. Certiorari to
World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson, 1978 OK 131, 585 P.2d 351 (Okla. 1978).
Parties
Petitioners: World-Wide Volkswagen Corporation, the regional wholesale distributor for Volkswagen and Audi
vehicles in New York, New Jersey, and Connecticut, and Seaway Volkswagen, Inc., the New York retail dealer that
sold the Robinsons' Audi. They were defendants in the products-liability action, applicants for prohibition in
Oklahoma, and petitioners in the U.S. Supreme Court. Respondents: Charles S. Woodson, the Creek County district
judge whose exercise of personal jurisdiction the petitioners sought to prohibit; Kay Eloise Robinson, who drove the
Audi and sued for her injuries; Harry Robinson, her husband, who sued individually and as next friend for their
children; and the injured children. Underlying defendants outside the Supreme Court appeal: Audi NSU Auto
Union Aktiengesellschaft manufactured the car, and Volkswagen of America, Inc. imported it. Both remained parties in
Oklahoma.
Procedural History
Underlying tort action: The Robinson family filed a manufacturers' products-liability action in the District Court for
Creek County, Oklahoma. They alleged that defective design and placement of the Audi's gasoline tank and fuel system
caused the post-collision fire and their burn injuries. They sued the German manufacturer, the American importer, the
regional distributor, and the retail dealer in the same action.
Special appearances and preservation: World-Wide and Seaway entered special appearances to contest Oklahoma's
power to exercise in-personam jurisdiction over them. Their papers also used the phrase 'venue of the subject matter,'
and they took depositions touching the merits. The Robinsons later argued that those steps waived the
personal-jurisdiction objection. The trial court classified the appearances as special, and the Oklahoma Supreme Court
decided the jurisdictional issues. The U.S. Supreme Court therefore treated the objection as preserved. Volkswagen of
America also entered a special appearance in the trial court. It stopped its challenge at that level and consequently
remained outside the U.S. Supreme Court proceeding.
Trial-court ruling: World-Wide and Seaway submitted evidence defining the geographic limits of their businesses.
The Creek County court rejected their due-process objection. It later denied reconsideration. Those unreported rulings
left the tort action pending and required the two companies to defend on the merits if immediate review remained
unavailable.
Original writ proceeding in Oklahoma: The companies commenced a separate original proceeding in the Supreme
Court of Oklahoma. They asked that court to assume original jurisdiction and issue a writ of prohibition directing Judge
Woodson to stop exercising personal jurisdiction over them. A writ of prohibition supplies immediate supervisory
review when a lower court is asserted to be acting beyond its adjudicatory power; the requested remedy addressed the
jurisdictional ruling before a products-liability trial occurred.
On October 10, 1978, the Oklahoma Supreme Court assumed original jurisdiction but denied the writ. 1978 OK 131,
585 P.2d 351. It applied Oklahoma's Uniform Interstate and International Procedure Act, commonly called the
long-arm statute. Section 1701.03(a)(3) covered tortious injury caused by an act or omission inside Oklahoma; the
court required a further statutory basis because the alleged design and distribution conduct occurred elsewhere. Section
1701.03(a)(4) covered in-state tortious injury caused outside the state when the defendant regularly conducted business,
pursued a persistent course of conduct, or derived substantial revenue from goods used or consumed in Oklahoma. The
court inferred substantial Oklahoma-derived revenue from the retail value and mobility of automobiles and therefore
found statutory authority. Oklahoma precedent construed the provision to extend through the federal constitutional
limit, so the state court's statutory and due-process analyses converged. Five justices joined the opinion, two concurred
in the result, and one concurred in part and dissented in part.
U.S. Supreme Court review and disposition: The Supreme Court granted certiorari, 440 U.S. 907 (1979), from the
completed Oklahoma writ proceeding to resolve a conflict among state high courts concerning jurisdiction over remote
vehicle sellers. The underlying tort action remained pending. By a 6-3 vote, the Court reversed the Oklahoma Supreme
Court. The judgment established that Oklahoma lacked personal jurisdiction over World-Wide and Seaway on this
record. The disposition applied to those two petitioners; Audi and Volkswagen of America remained defendants in
Creek County.

Scope of the Supreme Court's decision: Adequacy of notice was uncontested. The Court decided adjudicatory power
under the Fourteenth Amendment. It reviewed the jurisdictional record and the constitutional question generated by the
interlocutory trial ruling and the state supreme court's writ judgment. It left product defect, causation, damages, venue,
choice of law, and the liabilities of the manufacturer and importer for later proceedings.
Material Facts
Harry and Kay Robinson bought a new Audi from Seaway in Massena, New York, in 1976 while the family lived in
New York. In 1977, they left New York to establish a new home in Arizona. While Kay drove through Oklahoma,
another driver struck the Audi from behind. The impact ruptured the gasoline tank, ignited a fire in the passenger
compartment, and severely burned Kay and two children. The other driver played no part in the Supreme Court
litigation.
World-Wide was incorporated and maintained its business office in New York. Under its contract with Volkswagen, it
distributed vehicles, parts, and accessories to dealers in New York, New Jersey, and Connecticut. Seaway was
incorporated and operated in New York. The record treated World-Wide, Seaway, Volkswagen of America, and Audi
as independent entities connected through contracts.
The companies' commercial activity reached their northeastern territory. Their sales, shipments, agents, advertising,
and services were directed there. They closed no Oklahoma sale and performed no Oklahoma service. The record
identified the Robinson Audi as the single World-Wide or Seaway vehicle shown to have entered Oklahoma.
Volkswagen and Audi maintained a nationwide service-center system, while World-Wide and Seaway held no financial
interest in those Oklahoma facilities, received no service revenue from them, and exercised no control over them.
Question Presented
Does the Fourteenth Amendment permit Oklahoma to exercise specific personal jurisdiction over a New York
automobile retailer and a regional distributor when their own commercial conduct served New York, New Jersey, and
Connecticut, and their connection to Oklahoma arose when their customers drove the purchased car through Oklahoma
and suffered an accident there?
Holding
No. Specific jurisdiction requires a defendant-created affiliation with the forum that gives reasonable advance notice of
exposure to suit there. World-Wide and Seaway directed their sales and distribution activity toward a defined
northeastern market. The Robinsons created the Oklahoma connection by driving the Audi there. Purposeful forum
conduct supplies the required affiliation; the accident's location, Oklahoma's regulatory interest, and litigation
convenience then inform reasonableness. The Court reversed the judgment denying prohibition.
Reasoning
Constitutional source and historical position: The Fourteenth Amendment Due Process Clause limits a state court's
power to enter a binding personal judgment against a nonresident. Pennoyer v. Neff expressed that power through
territorial presence and warned that a judgment entered without jurisdiction is void and receives no full-faith-and-credit
effect. International Shoe Co. v. Washington translated the corporate-jurisdiction inquiry into minimum contacts whose
quality and relation to the litigation make suit consistent with traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.
World-Wide Volkswagen identifies the defendant's forum-directed conduct as the constitutional anchor within that
flexible standard.
Two functions of minimum contacts: Minimum contacts protect a defendant against the burdens of litigating in an
affiliated but distant forum and allocate adjudicatory authority among coequal states. The first function concerns
individual fairness. The second preserves interstate federalism by tying a state's coercive judicial power to a
defendant-created relationship with that state. A convenient forum with a substantial regulatory interest therefore still
needs a constitutionally adequate defendant-forum connection.
The five reasonableness interests: Once the defendant has established a forum affiliation, a court evaluates the burden
on the defendant, the forum state's interest in adjudication, the plaintiff's interest in convenient and effective relief, the
interstate judicial system's interest in efficient resolution, and the several states' shared interest in advancing substantive
social policies. Oklahoma possessed strong accident, evidence, witness, highway-safety, and plaintiff-convenience
interests. Those interests measure the fairness of exercising authority already grounded in a defendant-created contact.
Purposeful availment and operative foreseeability: Hanson v. Denckla requires an act through which the defendant
purposefully affiliates itself with the forum and invokes the benefits and protections of its law. The useful foreseeability
is the defendant's ability to anticipate suit from its own conduct and connection with the state. That notice permits a
business to insure, distribute expected costs, alter distribution, or sever a forum relationship. Product mobility predicts
physical travel; forum-directed commercial conduct predicts amenability to suit.
Application to the retailer and distributor: World-Wide selected a three-state distribution territory, and Seaway
operated a New York dealership. Their business conduct created affiliations within that territory. Their customers,
agents, and controlled distribution remained concentrated there. The Robinsons' relocation supplied the Oklahoma link
by transporting the product after a completed retail sale.

Stream of commerce: The Court explained that a manufacturer or distributor may create forum contacts by delivering
products into a distribution stream with the expectation that consumers in the forum will purchase them. Regular efforts
to serve a state's market provide clear notice when the product later causes injury there. World-Wide and Seaway
occupied different positions on this record: their market-facing conduct ended within the northeastern territory, and the
vehicle reached Oklahoma through its owners' post-sale travel. This distinction later generated the stream-of-commerce
dispute in Asahi.
Substantial-revenue theory: Oklahoma inferred that expensive automobiles sold by the petitioners would from time to
time be used in Oklahoma and would therefore produce substantial revenue connected with in-state use. The Supreme
Court found that economic link too attenuated. The record tied the petitioners' revenue to transactions in their
northeastern territory and tied the Oklahoma use to a consumer's later travel.
Automobile history and Hess: Automobiles earlier expanded jurisdiction through statutes appointing a state official to
receive process for claims arising from a nonresident's act of driving inside the forum, as in Hess v. Pawloski. There,
the defendant personally used the forum's roads and the suit arose from that use. The Robinsons used Oklahoma's roads
here. The commercial defendants remained outside Oklahoma and directed their conduct toward another region. The
Court therefore applied the ordinary chattel rule to the automobile.
Brennan dissent: Justice Brennan's dissent covered this case and the companion decision in Rush v. Savchuk. For the
Volkswagen dispute, he emphasized Oklahoma's accident site, hospitalized plaintiffs, witnesses, evidence, and
highway-safety interest, together with modern transportation and insurance. He viewed the minimum-contacts inquiry
as a flexible implementation of fairness and argued that the forum's strong interests reduced the quantum of defendant
contact required.
Marshall dissent, joined by Blackmun: Justice Marshall characterized World-Wide and Seaway as deliberate
participants in a nationwide marketing and service network. The economic value of a car included long-distance travel
and nationwide authorized service. In his analysis, selling a mobile product for its intended interstate use and benefiting
from that network supplied purposeful commercial conduct tied to Oklahoma when the car caused injury there.
Blackmun dissent: Justice Blackmun separately treated the automobile's long-distance function as a critical feature.
State highways, police, accident investigation, and victim assistance supported the vehicle market and the defense of
resulting claims. He would have upheld jurisdiction over the professional distributor and dealer when the accident
occurred in Oklahoma.
Doctrinal significance and exam sequence: Identify the defendant whose contacts are under review; preserve separate
treatment for every member of a distribution chain. Ask what that defendant deliberately did to affiliate with the forum,
whether the claim arose from or related to that conduct, and then apply the five fairness interests. World-Wide
Volkswagen locates the constitutional dividing line between defendant-directed market activity and a consumer's
post-sale movement of the product.

2. Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz
471 U.S. 462 (1985). U.S. Supreme Court. Brennan, J. No. 83-2097; argued January 8, 1985; decided May 20, 1985. Review of Burger
King Corp. v. MacShara, 724 F.2d 1505 (11th Cir. 1984), rehearing en banc denied, 729 F.2d 1468.
Parties
Petitioner / plaintiff: Burger King Corporation, a Florida corporation headquartered in Miami. It franchised
restaurants, leased standardized facilities, supervised the national system, sued in Florida federal court, and obtained
the trial judgment. Respondent / defendant-appellant below: John Rudzewicz, a Michigan resident and senior partner
in a Detroit accounting firm. He supplied capital, signed the franchise documents personally, challenged personal
jurisdiction, and pursued the appeal. Original codefendant: Brian MacShara, a Michigan resident with restaurant and
construction experience who managed daily operations and attended training in Miami. He did not appeal the judgment.
Procedural History
Federal complaint and subject-matter jurisdiction: Burger King filed in the U.S. District Court for the Southern
District of Florida in May 1981. The complaint invoked diversity jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. Section 1332(a) for the
contract dispute and federal-question jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. Section 1338(a) for trademark claims. Burger King
alleged that the franchisees breached obligations requiring payment at its Miami headquarters and continued using
Burger King marks and confidential operating information after termination. It sought contract damages, injunctive
relief, costs, and attorney's fees.
State long-arm authority in federal court: The district court relied on Florida Statutes Section 48.193(1)(g), which
extended personal jurisdiction over a person who breached a contract in Florida by failing to perform acts the contract
required there, for claims arising from that breach. The federal court therefore asked whether the Florida statute reached
the franchisees and whether the Fourteenth Amendment allowed Florida to bind them. Rudzewicz later conceded that
his conduct fell within the statute, leaving constitutional due process as the contested limit.
Special appearance and denial: Rudzewicz and MacShara entered special appearances and argued that their Michigan
residence, Michigan restaurant, and local operations placed the dispute outside Florida's personal jurisdiction. After a
hearing, the district court denied their motions. It held that a nonresident Burger King franchisee was subject to
jurisdiction in an action arising from franchise obligations requiring performance in Florida. The defendants then
answered and asserted a counterclaim under the Michigan Franchise Investment Law. Their participation after the
adverse ruling preserved the previously raised objection.
Bench trial and final judgment: Judge Kehoe conducted a three-day bench trial. The court reaffirmed jurisdiction,
found breach of the franchise agreements and infringement of Burger King's trademarks and service marks, rejected the
Michigan statutory counterclaim, and entered joint and several contract damages of $228,875.40. It awarded $2,151.06
in costs and $30,000 in attorney's fees. It also ordered the franchisees to close Restaurant No. 775 immediately or
surrender possession and the keys to Burger King. The final judgment made the preserved jurisdictional issue
reviewable together with the merits.
Scope of Rudzewicz's appeal: MacShara accepted the judgment. Rudzewicz appealed to the Eleventh Circuit and
challenged personal jurisdiction, the contract judgment, and the rejection of his counterclaim. He reached a
compromise that waived appellate review of the trademark-infringement finding and the associated injunction. The
surviving personal-jurisdiction issue concerned Florida's contract long-arm provision and Burger King's claim for
performance due in Miami.
Eleventh Circuit: A divided panel reversed. 724 F.2d 1505 (11th Cir. 1984). Judge Vance's majority treated the
minimum-contacts inquiry as a balance of commercial responsibility and fair preparation for suit. It emphasized that
Burger King's Michigan office conducted the face-to-face negotiations, supervised the restaurant, and supplied the
contractual services; Rudzewicz lived and operated exclusively in Michigan; the restaurant's sales occurred there; and
the printed Florida terms arrived through standardized documents. The majority found inadequate warning and
substantial litigation disadvantage, held Florida jurisdiction fundamentally unfair, and stopped before reaching the
substantive appellate claims. Judge Johnson dissented. He emphasized Rudzewicz's sophistication, five months of
negotiations, personal undertaking exceeding $1 million, Florida choice-of-law clauses, payment obligations to Miami,
and Florida's interest. Rehearing en banc was denied. 729 F.2d 1468.
Route into the Supreme Court: Burger King attempted a direct appeal under then-28 U.S.C. Section 1254(2), a route
available when a federal court of appeals held a state statute unconstitutional as applied. The Supreme Court found the
Eleventh Circuit's basis unclear because Florida courts had construed Section 48.193(1)(g) to extend through the
due-process boundary. A statute defined by that boundary may yield an unconstitutional exercise without itself being
invalidated. The Court dismissed the statutory appeal, treated Burger King's jurisdictional statement as a petition for
certiorari under 28 U.S.C. Section 2103, granted certiorari, and reached the federal question.

Supreme Court disposition and standard of review: By a 6-2 vote, the Court reversed the Eleventh Circuit and
remanded for further proceedings. Justice Powell took no part. The constitutional sufficiency of the forum relationship
presented a legal question. The district court's trial findings concerning sophistication, misrepresentation, fraud,
adhesion, and economic duress remained governed by Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 52(a), which permitted reversal
only for clear error. The record supported those findings, and the Court used them in the fairness analysis.
Material Facts
Burger King operated a nationwide franchise system from Miami. Franchisees received a twenty-year license to use its
trademarks and operating system in standardized restaurants. Miami headquarters set policy and controlled major
decisions; district offices monitored daily operations and reported to Miami. The agreements required franchise fees,
rent, royalties, tax refunds, and other payments to Miami, directed relevant notices there, stated that final acceptance
occurred there, and selected Florida law. The choice-of-law clause expressly stated that it did not require every suit to
be filed in Florida.
In autumn 1978, Rudzewicz and MacShara jointly applied through Burger King's Birmingham, Michigan district
office. Rudzewicz supplied capital and MacShara planned to manage the restaurant. The Michigan office evaluated the
application, recommended an existing restaurant in Drayton Plains, Michigan, negotiated local matters, and attended
the closing. Miami headquarters approved the application, mailed documents for execution and completion, supplied
the final agreements, and retained authority over site-development fees, building design, rent calculation, personal
liability, and other disputed terms.
MacShara attended Burger King University in Miami. The franchisees bought and installed approximately $165,000 of
equipment before the final rent was calculated. Rudzewicz sought a lower rent after learning the final amount, and
Miami granted a limited concession. The parties also disputed whether liabilities could be assigned to their corporation;
Burger King required continued personal responsibility. Rudzewicz and MacShara signed the lease and franchise
agreements in Michigan on June 4, 1979. Rudzewicz personally undertook payments exceeding $1 million over the
twenty-year term.
The store initially performed steadily, then patronage fell during the 1979 recession. The franchisees quickly became
delinquent. Miami sent default notices. The franchisees, the Michigan office, and Miami headquarters conducted
extended negotiations by telephone and mail. The Michigan office acted as a channel while Miami made the decisive
concessions and enforcement decisions. Miami eventually terminated the franchise and ordered the franchisees to
vacate. They remained in the premises and continued operating under Burger King's marks.
Question Presented
When a Michigan businessman deliberately establishes a negotiated, twenty-year, interdependent franchise relationship
with a corporation headquartered in Florida; accepts continuing payment, notice, regulatory, and choice-of-law
obligations tied to Florida; communicates with Miami to resolve major disputes; and allegedly breaches the
Florida-linked obligations, does specific personal jurisdiction in Florida satisfy the Fourteenth Amendment even
though he operates the restaurant and resides outside Florida?
Holding
Yes. Rudzewicz purposefully created a substantial and continuing relationship with Burger King's Miami headquarters,
and the litigation arose directly from that relationship. Physical entry into Florida supplied one possible form of contact
and was unnecessary because the defendant deliberately directed interstate contractual activity toward a Florida
resident. The course of dealing and documents gave fair warning of Florida-linked obligations. After those contacts
were established, Rudzewicz failed to present the compelling showing of unreasonableness required to defeat
jurisdiction. The Court reversed and remanded.
Reasoning
The two-stage due-process structure: A court first identifies defendant-created minimum contacts connecting the
defendant, the forum, and the litigation. For specific jurisdiction, the defendant purposefully directs activity toward the
forum and the claim arises out of or relates to that activity. The court then evaluates whether exercising the resulting
authority accords with fair play and substantial justice. This sequence gives the defendant fair warning and then
measures the practical fairness of the particular forum.
Purposeful availment: The requirement selects deliberate conduct carrying a substantial connection with the forum. It
excludes contacts generated by a plaintiff, customer, or third person and contacts that arise by chance. A defendant who
creates continuing obligations with a forum resident obtains commercial benefits supported by the forum's law and can
anticipate accountability there for disputes produced by those obligations. World-Wide Volkswagen supplies the
contrast: the Robinsons moved the product to Oklahoma after a completed New York sale, while Rudzewicz himself
established and maintained the Florida-centered relationship.

Physical presence: Interstate commerce occurs through mail, telephone, payments, licenses, and continuing
administration. A defendant's deliberate direction of those activities toward a forum resident supplies a meaningful
affiliation even when the defendant remains physically elsewhere. Rudzewicz's own travel history therefore carried
limited weight. MacShara's Miami training reinforced the interstate relationship, while the contract, negotiations, future
obligations, and course of dealing carried the analysis.
Contract analysis: The existence of a contract with a forum resident supplies the starting fact. Courts examine the
prior negotiations, contemplated future consequences, contractual terms, and actual course of dealing. This highly
realistic inquiry treats the written agreement as an intermediate step connecting negotiations with the future relationship
the parties sought to create. It prevents party residence or the formal place of signature from controlling a multistate
transaction.
Application of the contract factors: Rudzewicz pursued affiliation with a national enterprise headquartered in
Florida, negotiated issues that the Michigan office lacked authority to resolve, accepted twenty years of detailed
regulation and payment obligations administered from Miami, and communicated continuously with Miami during
default. The claims arose from missed Miami payments and post-termination use of Florida-controlled marks and
information. The quality, duration, and claim relationship of these contacts gave Rudzewicz advance notice that Florida
could adjudicate the resulting dispute.
Choice of law and forum selection: The clause selected Florida's substantive law and left the litigation forum open. It
reinforced purposeful affiliation because the parties chose Florida's legal protections for a long-term Florida-centered
relationship. The Court treated the clause as one component of the full course of dealing.
Reasonableness factors and allocation of proof: After purposeful contacts exist, the defendant may defeat
jurisdiction by presenting a compelling case that the forum would be unreasonable. Courts assess the burden on the
defendant, the forum's adjudicatory interest, the plaintiff's interest in effective relief, interstate efficiency, and shared
substantive policies. Strong forum and plaintiff interests may support jurisdiction on a smaller set of purposeful
contacts. An extreme litigation burden can defeat jurisdiction despite purposeful activity. Ordinary inconvenience is
commonly handled through venue transfer, choice-of-law rules, and other procedural devices.
Application of fairness: Florida had a legitimate interest in adjudicating a dispute arising from obligations owed to a
Florida corporation and governed by Florida law. Michigan's franchise-regulation interest could be addressed through
choice-of-law analysis. The record did not substantiate the Eleventh Circuit's concern that Florida litigation impaired
access to essential Michigan witnesses. Rudzewicz was an experienced accountant represented by counsel, negotiated
concessions, and accepted personal obligations exceeding $1 million. The district court found informed action,
commercial sophistication, and an absence of misrepresentation or imposed economic duress. Those Rule 52(a)
findings supported a fair opportunity to anticipate and defend the suit.
Case-specific reach: The Court adopted a transaction-specific inquiry. A franchise's decision structure, bargaining
history, contractual allocation, duration, sophistication, and course of performance determine the forum relationship.
Burger King's proposed franchise-wide presumption therefore gave way to this individualized framework, which also
governs modest consumer transactions.
Stevens dissent, joined by White: Justice Stevens focused on the economic and operational center of the franchise.
Rudzewicz's restaurant, employees, sales, property, taxes, and day-to-day supervision were located in Michigan;
Burger King's Michigan office conducted the direct negotiations and supplied contractual services. Stevens viewed the
printed Florida provisions as insufficient fair warning when Burger King had structured the human relationship around
its Michigan office. He would have affirmed the Eleventh Circuit's fairness judgment.
Procedural and doctrinal significance: Burger King makes the specific-jurisdiction inquiry sequential and assigns an
evidentiary consequence to purposeful contacts: the defendant then carries the burden of showing a compelling
constitutional unfairness. The case also demonstrates three separate review questions - state long-arm authorization,
constitutional adjudicatory power, and trial-fact deference under Rule 52(a). Its reasonableness framework supplies the
analysis that produced the controlling holding in Asahi.

3. Asahi Metal Industry Co. v. Superior Court
480 U.S. 102 (1987). U.S. Supreme Court. O'Connor, J. No. 85-693; argued November 5, 1986; decided February 24, 1987. Certiorari to
Asahi Metal Industry Co. v. Superior Court, 39 Cal. 3d 35, 702 P.2d 543 (1985).
Parties
Petitioner / cross-defendant: Asahi Metal Industry Co., Ltd., a Japanese corporation that manufactured tire-valve
assemblies in Japan and sold them to tire-tube manufacturers abroad. It moved to quash service, sought mandate relief,
and obtained reversal in the U.S. Supreme Court. Respondent in the writ caption: The Superior Court of California
for Solano County, whose jurisdictional order Asahi sought to vacate. A trial judge appears as respondent in an
extraordinary-writ case because the writ would command the court to change its ruling. Real party in interest: Cheng
Shin Rubber Industrial Co., Ltd., a Taiwanese corporation that manufactured the motorcycle tube and pursued
indemnification against Asahi. Original claimant: Gary Zurcher, a California resident injured in the motorcycle
accident; his passenger and wife, Ruth Ann Moreno, died. Their resolved product-liability claims were outside the
dispute that reached the Supreme Court.
Procedural History
Accident and original complaint: The September 23, 1978 accident occurred on Interstate 80 in Solano County. In
September 1979, Zurcher filed a products-liability action in the Solano County Superior Court. He alleged defects in
the motorcycle tire, tube, and sealant and named Cheng Shin, the tube's Taiwanese manufacturer, together with a
California retailer and other defendants.
Cross-complaint: Cheng Shin filed a cross-complaint seeking indemnification from its codefendants and from Asahi,
which manufactured the tube's valve assembly. Indemnification reallocates an underlying liability from the party that
paid the injured claimant to another party asserted to bear responsibility. Cheng Shin's claim therefore concerned the
commercial relationship between the Taiwanese tube maker and the Japanese component maker. Zurcher's claims
against Cheng Shin and the other defendants eventually settled and were dismissed. At the stage reviewed by the U.S.
Supreme Court, Cheng Shin's indemnity claim against Asahi was the sole remaining controversy.
Long-arm statute and motion to quash: California Code of Civil Procedure Section 410.10 authorizes personal
jurisdiction on every basis consistent with the California and United States Constitutions. The statutory inquiry
therefore reached the constitutional boundary. Asahi moved to quash Cheng Shin's service of summons, asserting that
the Fourteenth Amendment denied California adjudicatory authority over it. The motion attacked the court's power over
Asahi and left the indemnity merits for later adjudication. Under California law, Cheng Shin carried the burden of
establishing personal jurisdiction through competent evidence.
Jurisdictional evidentiary record: The parties submitted declarations concerning Asahi's sales to Cheng Shin, Cheng
Shin's sales in California, a Solano County store survey, discussions between the companies, and Asahi's understanding
of the distribution path. Asahi's president stated that the company never contemplated California litigation from the
Taiwanese sales. Cheng Shin's manager stated that worldwide and United States distribution, including California, had
been discussed with Asahi. The affidavit-based preliminary record contained the parties' full jurisdictional showing and
included no written Asahi-Cheng Shin contract.
Superior Court: On April 20, 1983, the trial court denied the motion to quash. It relied on the number of Asahi valves
incorporated into tubes sold in California, the volume of Asahi's sales to Cheng Shin, Cheng Shin's substantial
California distribution, and Asahi's awareness of that distribution. The court reasoned that a business operating
internationally could reasonably defend international product claims.
California Court of Appeal: Asahi sought immediate extraordinary relief because the denial of a motion to quash left
the action pending. The Court of Appeal issued a peremptory writ of mandate directing the Superior Court to quash
service. It treated worldwide product foreseeability as an inadequate basis for forcing Asahi to defend in California.
The writ would have terminated California's authority over Asahi before adjudication of indemnity.
California Supreme Court: The state high court reversed the Court of Appeal's writ disposition and restored the
Superior Court's jurisdictional ruling. 39 Cal. 3d 35, 702 P.2d 543 (1985). Chief Justice Bird's five-Justice opinion
concluded that Asahi intentionally placed components into a regular distribution stream, knew finished tubes would be
sold in California, and indirectly benefited from those sales. It also credited California's consumer-safety and
efficient-litigation interests. The court denied mandate relief and discharged the alternative writ. Justice Lucas, joined
by Justice Mosk, dissented. He emphasized Cheng Shin's evidentiary burden, the small share of Asahi revenue
traceable to Cheng Shin, the foreign indemnity claim that remained after settlement, and the overseas commercial
relationship.
U.S. Supreme Court review: The Supreme Court granted certiorari, 475 U.S. 1044 (1986), from the completed state
extraordinary-writ proceeding while the indemnity action remained pending below. It reviewed the jurisdictional record
and the Fourteenth Amendment question. The Court unanimously agreed on the judgment reversing California, while
its reasoning divided into three writings. The formal disposition reversed the California Supreme Court and remanded
for proceedings consistent with the opinions, requiring California to release Asahi from the indemnity action.

Vote structure and precedential force: Justice O'Connor's Part I recited the case and commanded unanimous
agreement. Eight Justices joined Part II-B's holding that California jurisdiction was unreasonable and unfair; Justice
Scalia withheld his vote from that part. Four Justices - O'Connor, Chief Justice Rehnquist, Powell, and Scalia - joined
Parts II-A and III, which required stream-of-commerce-plus conduct and found deficient minimum contacts. Four
Justices - Brennan, White, Marshall, and Blackmun - found minimum contacts under an awareness-based stream theory
and concurred because Part II-B made jurisdiction unreasonable. Three Justices - Stevens, White, and Blackmun - also
reasoned that fairness resolved the case and treated volume, value, and hazardous character as relevant to any contacts
analysis. The eight-Justice fairness conclusion constitutes the Court's controlling holding. The competing stream
formulations each lacked a majority.
Scope of decision: The Court adjudicated California state-court authority under the Fourteenth Amendment. It
expressly reserved whether Congress could authorize a federal court, consistently with the Fifth Amendment, to
exercise jurisdiction over an alien defendant based on that defendant's aggregate contacts with the United States. It also
left the indemnity merits, governing substantive law, and allocation of product responsibility undecided.
Material Facts
Asahi manufactured valve assemblies in Japan and sold them to Cheng Shin and other tire manufacturers for
incorporation into finished tubes. Its sales to Cheng Shin occurred in Taiwan, and Asahi shipped the components from
Japan to Taiwan. Cheng Shin sold tubes throughout the world. It represented that approximately twenty percent of its
United States sales occurred in California.
Cheng Shin bought 150,000 Asahi valve assemblies in 1978; 500,000 in 1979; 500,000 in 1980; 100,000 in 1981; and
100,000 in 1982, for a five-year total of 1,350,000. These sales represented 1.24 percent of Asahi's income in 1981 and
0.44 percent in 1982. Cheng Shin also used valves from other suppliers, and other tube makers incorporated Asahi
valves into products that reached California.
In 1983, Cheng Shin's lawyer informally examined approximately 115 tubes offered in one Solano County motorcycle
store. Ninety-seven appeared to have been made in Japan or Taiwan. Twenty-one of those bore Asahi's circled-A valve
marking, and twelve of the twenty-one were installed in Cheng Shin tubes. The survey established the presence and
recurring volume of Asahi components in a local retail channel; it did not identify a direct Asahi sale or
California-directed distribution contract.
Asahi located its business operations, property, offices, agents, employees, advertising, solicitation, manufacturing,
contracting, shipment, and direct sales outside California. Cheng Shin controlled the manufacture and distribution of its
finished tubes. The declarations supported an inference that Asahi knew some finished tubes would reach the United
States and California. Cheng Shin's distribution supplied the path into California; the record attributed product design,
advertising, customer advice, and sales-agency decisions to activity outside any California program organized by
Asahi.
Question Presented
Two related questions: (1) Does a foreign component manufacturer's regular placement of components into an
international distribution stream, with awareness that finished products incorporating them will be marketed in
California, create purposeful minimum contacts with California? (2) After the California residents settle and dismiss
their product-liability claims, does fair play and substantial justice permit California to retain jurisdiction over the
remaining indemnity dispute between a Taiwanese purchaser and a Japanese seller concerning sales and shipments
made in Asia?
Holding
The Court unanimously reversed California's assertion of personal jurisdiction over Asahi. Eight Justices held that the
international burden on Asahi, California's diminished interest after settlement, Cheng Shin's limited California interest,
the overseas location of the relevant commercial transaction, and the interests of other nations made jurisdiction
unreasonable and unfair. That fairness conclusion is the Court's majority rule. Justice O'Connor's four-Justice plurality
required conduct purposefully directing the distribution toward the forum in addition to awareness. Justice Brennan's
four-Justice concurrence treated awareness of a regular and anticipated flow into the forum as sufficient. Justice
Stevens resolved the case on fairness and identified volume, value, and hazardous character as relevant if minimum
contacts were reached.
Reasoning
Relationship to World-Wide Volkswagen and Burger King: World-Wide Volkswagen required a defendant-created
affiliation and described products delivered into a distribution stream with an expectation of forum purchases. Lower
courts divided over whether that passage required awareness of regular forum sales or additional conduct directed at the
forum. Burger King supplied the two-stage structure: purposeful minimum contacts create presumptive authority, and
the five fairness interests may still make the exercise unreasonable. Asahi exposed disagreement at the first stage and
produced broad agreement at the second.

O'Connor plurality - stream of commerce plus: The plurality required both awareness of distribution and an act
purposefully directed toward the forum. Forum-specific intent can be shown through conduct such as designing for the
forum market, advertising there, establishing regular customer-advice channels there, or using a distributor that agreed
to serve as a sales agent there. This approach treats placement into the distribution stream as commercial participation
and additional forum targeting as purposeful direction.
Plurality application: Asahi made and sold the valve assemblies in Asia. Cheng Shin determined where the finished
tubes would be sold. Asahi maintained an ongoing, high-volume component relationship and could be charged with
awareness of California sales. Its design, advertising, service, solicitation, agents, controlled distribution, property,
personnel, and direct transactions remained outside a California-directed program. The O'Connor plurality therefore
found the purposeful-direction element unsatisfied.
Brennan concurrence - regular and anticipated flow: Justice Brennan, joined by White, Marshall, and Blackmun,
treated the stream as the regular and anticipated movement from manufacture through distribution to retail sale. A
participant that knows the final product is being marketed in the forum benefits economically from those sales and from
the forum's commercial legal system. Awareness of that regular flow gives advance notice of litigation and fulfills
purposeful availment.
Brennan application: Asahi sold Cheng Shin hundreds of thousands of valves annually over a continuing relationship;
the store survey found Asahi valves in a substantial share of the Japanese and Taiwanese tubes offered locally; and the
parties had discussed United States and California distribution. Brennan therefore found minimum contacts. He joined
the judgment because the fairness balance defeated California jurisdiction despite those contacts.
Stevens concurrence: Justice Stevens, joined by White and Blackmun, concluded that the fairness holding supplied a
complete ground for reversal and allowed the Court to leave the stream test open. He also questioned the plurality's
characterization of Asahi's activity as bare placement. A purposeful-availment analysis could account for the volume,
value, and hazardous character of the components. Asahi's five-year sales volume and the safety role of tire valves
could support a greater quantum of forum-relevant conduct than the plurality acknowledged.
Controlling fairness holding - burden on Asahi: Defending in California required a Japanese company to cross a
substantial geographic distance and submit an Asian commercial dispute to a foreign nation's judicial system.
International litigation adds language, legal-system, evidence, diplomatic, and coordination burdens. The Court gave
those transnational burdens significant weight.
Forum and claimant interests: Settlement transformed the litigation. California residents no longer sought
compensation, and the product-liability claims enforcing California safety standards had ended. Cheng Shin, a
Taiwanese corporation, sought indemnity from a Japanese corporation. The component sale occurred in Taiwan and the
shipment traveled from Japan to Taiwan. Cheng Shin offered no showing that California provided a more convenient
forum than Taiwan or Japan for this remaining dispute. California's interest therefore became slight.
Efficiency, governing law, and international policy: California's former interest in consolidating defendants and
avoiding inconsistent product-liability judgments diminished with dismissal of the main case. The remaining claim
could require interpretation of an Asian commercial relationship, and the record gave no clear basis for applying
California indemnity law. The several-states factor from World-Wide Volkswagen expands in an international case to
include the procedural and substantive policies of foreign nations and the federal government's foreign-relations
interest. Comity called for careful restraint when local interests were small and foreign burdens were heavy.
Why the claim's procedural identity matters: Specific jurisdiction evaluates the relationship among this defendant,
this forum, and this claim at the stage presented. The California accident explained the original products-liability suit.
The controversy surviving at Supreme Court review concerned indemnity between foreign companies arising from their
Asian transactions. That procedural change reduced the forum's regulatory and remedial stakes and drove the
unanimous judgment.
Precedential rule: The eight-Justice Part II-B establishes that fair play and substantial justice can defeat jurisdiction
even when a purposeful-contact theory is available. The stream-of-commerce-plus and awareness approaches each
received four votes and form distinct analytical positions. The international reasonableness holding binds lower courts;
the minimum-contacts standard for stream cases remains unsettled.
Exam sequence: Begin with the forum's long-arm statute and the procedural burden for proving jurisdiction. Identify
the claim remaining at the time of decision. Attribute forum conduct to the particular defendant. In a distribution case,
analyze O'Connor's additional-conduct approach, Brennan's regular-flow awareness approach, and Stevens's
volume-value-hazardous-character considerations. Then apply every fairness factor, with special attention to foreign
legal systems, the claimant's citizenship and convenience, the governing transaction, comity, and federal
foreign-relations interests. State separately the proposition supported by a Court majority and the propositions
supported by pluralities or concurrences.
