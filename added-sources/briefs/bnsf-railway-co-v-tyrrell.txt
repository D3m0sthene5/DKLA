BNSF Railway Co. v. Tyrrell
BNSF Railway Co. v. Tyrrell, 581 U.S. 402 (2017). United States Supreme Court, decided May 30, 2017.
Justice Ginsburg wrote for eight Justices. Justice Sotomayor concurred in the statutory analysis and dissented from the due-process analysis and judgment. The case consolidated two Montana actions under the
Federal Employers' Liability Act, or FELA.
Full judicial opinion.

== Parties ==
BNSF Railway Company was the defendant in both actions and petitioner in the Supreme Court. It was incorporated in Delaware and had its principal place of business in Texas. Robert Nelson, a North Dakota resident, was one plaintiff. Kelli Tyrrell, a South Dakota resident acting as administrator of the estate of
Brent Tyrrell, was the other. Both opposed BNSF's effort to obtain dismissal for lack of personal jurisdiction.

== Material Facts ==
Nelson alleged knee injuries sustained during his work for BNSF as a fuel-truck driver. Tyrrell alleged that her husband developed fatal kidney cancer from exposure to carcinogenic chemicals while working for
BNSF. Neither plaintiff alleged an injury connected to BNSF's Montana operations, and neither injured employee appeared to have worked for BNSF in Montana. The claims were therefore unrelated to the business contacts relied upon in that state.
BNSF nevertheless had a substantial physical operation in Montana: approximately 2,061 miles of track, about 2,100 employees, and an automotive facility. The railroad operated in twenty-eight states. Montana accounted for roughly 6 percent of its total track, less than 5 percent of its employees, and less than 10 percent of its revenue. Those facts supplied the plaintiffs' argument that BNSF was sufficiently present in
Montana. The Court had to determine whether that operating presence authorized all-purpose jurisdiction, given BNSF's incorporation and headquarters elsewhere. 581 U.S. at 405-07.

== Procedural History ==
Nelson filed his Montana state-court action in March 2011. Tyrrell filed hers in May 2014. BNSF moved to dismiss both actions for lack of personal jurisdiction. The trial court granted the motion in Nelson's case but denied it in Tyrrell's case. The Montana Supreme Court consolidated the ensuing appeals and held that both actions could proceed, reversing the dismissal in Nelson and affirming the denial in Tyrrell. Its decision was reported at 2016 MT 126, 383 Mont. 417, 373 P.3d 1.
The state supreme court offered two grounds. It interpreted 45 U.S.C. §56 as authorizing jurisdiction over a railroad wherever it did business. It also relied on Montana Rule of Civil Procedure 4(b)(1), which authorizes jurisdiction over persons found within the state. The court treated Daimler as distinguishable because that case concerned foreign corporations and foreign claims, whereas these cases concerned an
American railroad sued under FELA. Justice McKinnon dissented.
The United States Supreme Court granted certiorari to address both the federal statute and the constitutional sufficiency of the Montana contacts. BNSF did not contest that its business satisfied the state rule's “found within” language. That concession established the state-law authorization assumed for decision, but left the federal constitutional objection intact. The employees also advanced consent as an alternative jurisdictional basis. Because the Montana Supreme Court had not addressed that theory, the Supreme Court left it open on remand. The litigation remained at the jurisdictional stage; there had been no trial establishing injury, negligence, or damages. 581 U.S. at 407-09, 415.

== Issue ==
Does FELA authorize a state court to exercise personal jurisdiction over any railroad doing business in the state? If the statute does not do so, do BNSF's extensive Montana tracks and employees make it subject to general jurisdiction there for employment claims unrelated to Montana?

== Holding ==
FELA's provision specifying federal districts in which an action may be brought is a venue provision. Its statement that federal and state courts have concurrent jurisdiction addresses subject-matter jurisdiction.
Neither provision establishes personal jurisdiction over a railroad in a particular state. Separately, Daimler's at-home requirement applies to domestic railroads and FELA claims as well as foreign corporations and other causes of action. BNSF's Montana operations did not make it at home there. 581 U.S. at 409-15.

== Judgment ==
The Court reversed the Montana Supreme Court and remanded. Neither FELA nor BNSF's Montana business operations supported the grounds for jurisdiction upheld below. The consent theory remained for consideration because it had not been resolved by the state supreme court. The Supreme Court therefore did not order a final rejection of every possible jurisdictional basis or adjudicate the merits of either employee's claim.

== Reasoning ==
The Court separated three legal questions that the Montana opinion had combined. Subject-matter jurisdiction concerns the court's authority to hear the category of dispute; venue identifies a permissible geographic location within the judicial system; personal jurisdiction concerns the court's authority over the particular defendant. Section 56's statement that an action may be brought in certain federal districts uses the language of venue. Its provision for concurrent state and federal jurisdiction ensures that state courts can hear FELA claims as a class. Neither provision supplies authority over every railroad defendant in every state where it does business.
The Court also distinguished earlier railroad cases that had addressed venue, the Commerce Clause, or injunctions restraining parallel litigation. Those decisions did not settle the due-process question presented here. When Congress intends to confer broad personal jurisdiction, it commonly addresses service of process. The FELA language on which the plaintiffs relied lacked such a grant. A federal substantive right to compensation for negligence does not itself identify every court that may adjudicate that right against a given employer.
Turning to due process, the Court applied the same at-home framework used in Goodyear and Daimler.
Delaware and Texas were BNSF's ordinary corporate homes. Montana hosted real and substantial business, but it was one part of a much broader railroad system. The record did not present an exceptional circumstance resembling the relocation of central corporate operations in Perkins. A company does not become at home in every state in which it lays track or employs a substantial workforce.
Those same operations could support specific jurisdiction over a claim arising from or relating to activity in
Montana. The difficulty here was that these plaintiffs' claims had no such relationship. The Court also rejected the state court's attempt to limit Daimler to multinational corporations or particular kinds of claims.
Constitutional limits on all-purpose adjudicatory authority do not disappear when the defendant is a domestic railroad or the plaintiff invokes FELA. 581 U.S. at 409-15.

== Separate Opinions ==
Justice Sotomayor agreed that FELA did not confer personal jurisdiction and that the type of claim did not eliminate the constitutional inquiry. She continued to object to Daimler's restrictive conception of corporate homes, particularly its comparison of forum contacts with operations elsewhere. She regarded that approach as giving large multistate companies an advantage despite their substantial enjoyment of a forum's protections. Even accepting the at-home test, she would have returned the case for a fact-intensive assessment under the proper standard rather than having the Supreme Court itself reject exceptional-case jurisdiction. She warned that limiting the exception to facts almost identical to Perkins would deprive it of practical meaning. 581 U.S. at 415 (Sotomayor, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part).

== Notes ==
The remand matters when reading this case with Mallory. BNSF rejects general jurisdiction based on the railroad's ordinary business contacts but expressly leaves alleged consent unresolved. Mallory later addresses consent under Pennsylvania's express registration scheme. The cases concern separate grounds for authority.
A state's permission to hear FELA claims and a railroad's substantial local operations do not, by themselves, answer whether that particular defendant can be sued there on an unrelated claim.
