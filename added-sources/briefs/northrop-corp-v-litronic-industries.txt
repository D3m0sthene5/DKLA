Northrop Corp. v. Litronic Industries
Northrop Corp. v. Litronic Industries, 29 F.3d 1173 (7th Cir. 1994). United States Court of Appeals for the
Seventh Circuit, Nos. 93-3912 and 93-4000, argued May 17, 1994, decided July 18, 1994. Chief Judge
Posner wrote the opinion for a panel that included Judge Ripple and Judge Hill of the Eleventh Circuit, sitting by designation. Judge Ripple concurred separately. The case concerns what happens under UCC § 2-207 when a term in the acceptance conflicts with, rather than adds to, a term in the offer. Full judicial opinion.
Source: added-sources/Contracts_Battle_of_the_Forms_and_Rolling_Contracts_Briefs.pdf, pages 5–8

== Parties ==
Northrop Corporation, a large defense contractor that builds weapons systems incorporating electronic components, was the buyer, the plaintiff below, and the appellee and cross-appellant. Litronic Industries, a much smaller manufacturer of electronic components including printed wire boards, was the seller, the defendant below, and the appellant and cross-appellee. Northrop sought to recover what it had paid for boards that failed inspection. Litronic contended that its 90-day warranty governed the sale and had expired before Northrop rejected the boards. Both parties were merchants, and Illinois law governed the diversity action.

== Material Facts ==
In 1987 Northrop solicited bids for four customized printed wire boards, designated the 1714 board, for use in a defense system. Litronic mailed an offer to sell four boards at $19,000 each with delivery in six weeks.
The offer stated a 90-day warranty that was to be in lieu of any other warranties, and it provided that
Litronic's terms would take precedence over any terms proposed by the buyer. Northrop's purchasing officer,
Lynch, telephoned Litronic's representative, Lair, and said that Northrop accepted the offer up to the limit of his authority, $24,999, and that a formal purchase order for all four boards would follow. Lynch said enough on the phone to make clear that Northrop's acceptance carried terms different from Litronic's. About a month later Lynch sent a "turn on" letter authorizing production and again promising a purchase order, which arrived some months after that. Northrop's purchase-order form called for a written acknowledgment from the seller and contained a warranty with no time limit.
The first three boards were delivered in July 1988, more than a year after the telephone call. Northrop's testing was protracted. After five or six months, in December 1988 and January 1989, Northrop concluded that the boards were defective and tried to return them. Litronic refused to take them back on the ground that its 90-day warranty had lapsed. 29 F.3d at 1174-76.

== Procedural History ==
Northrop sued Litronic in federal district court in Illinois under diversity jurisdiction, and the case was tried before a magistrate judge. The magistrate judge held that the conflicting warranty terms in the offer and the acceptance cancelled each other under § 2-207 and that the Code's gap-filling provision, § 2-309, supplied a reasonable time in their place; found that six months was reasonable because of the complexity of the testing the boards required; and entered judgment for Northrop for $58,000, the amount Northrop had paid for the three delivered boards. The magistrate judge disbelieved Northrop's claim that it had returned other defective boards and denied recovery for boards that Northrop had neither returned nor accounted for.
Litronic appealed, arguing that its 90-day warranty controlled and that Northrop's rejection came too late.
Northrop cross-appealed, arguing that it had in fact returned the other boards and, alternatively, that §
2-711(3) entitled it to hold rejected goods as security for its damages without returning them. The factual
findings were reviewed for clear error. The § 2-207 question was unsettled under Illinois law, so the Seventh
Circuit had to predict how the Illinois Supreme Court would resolve it, the standard task of a federal court applying state law under Erie. Id. at 1176-77.

== Issue ==
Section 2-207(1) makes a response an acceptance even though it states terms "additional to or different from" those offered, but § 2-207(2) explains what happens only to "additional" terms. When the acceptance contains a term that conflicts with a term in the offer, does the offeror's term control, does the different term drop out under the materiality analysis of § 2-207(2), or do both conflicting terms fall away and yield to the
Code's gap-fillers? Which rule would Illinois adopt, and under that rule did Northrop's rejection five or six months after delivery come within the warranty? On the cross-appeal, may a buyer keep rejected goods as security under § 2-711(3) without accounting for them?

== Holding ==
Illinois would follow the majority rule: when the offer and the acceptance contain conflicting terms, the discrepant terms cancel each other out and the Code's gap-filling provisions supply the missing term. This approach is commonly called the knockout rule, a standard label in the case law and treatises, though the opinion itself does not use the phrase. Litronic's 90-day warranty and Northrop's unlimited warranty were materially different, so both dropped out, and § 2-309's reasonable-time standard governed. The finding that six months was reasonable for these boards was not clearly erroneous, so Northrop's rejection was timely.
On the cross-appeal, a buyer who rightfully rejects goods holds a security interest in them under § 2-711(3) and may hold or resell them, but a buyer that neither returns the goods nor explains what became of them cannot recover their price, because the omission leaves open the inference of a double recovery. Id. at
1177-80.

== Judgment ==
The Seventh Circuit affirmed the judgment in full: $58,000 to Northrop for the three boards it had paid for, and no recovery for the boards it had not returned or accounted for.

== Reasoning ==
Judge Posner opened with the problem § 2-207 was written to solve. Under the common-law mirror-image rule, any discrepancy between the forms prevented the offeree's response from operating as an acceptance, so an exchange of nonmatching boilerplate, the situation commercial lawyers call the battle of the forms, produced no contract until performance. Section 2-207(1) lets a response operate as an acceptance despite additional or different terms, and § 2-207(2) then decides which additional terms enter the contract. The drafting gap is that subsection (2) says nothing about different terms. Official Comment 3 states that whether additional or different terms become part of the agreement depends on subsection (2), and the drafting history suggests that the omission of "or different" from subsection (2) may have been an error. Id. at 1174-75,
1177-78.
Three views have developed. Under the first, followed by California in Steiner v. Mobil Oil Corp., 20 Cal. 3d
90 (1977), different terms are treated exactly like additional terms, so the question is simply whether the term materially alters the contract. Posner called this possibly the most sensible approach, because every different term is additional and every additional term is different, and a manageable inquiry into materiality replaces hair-splitting over which label applies. Under the second, which he called the leading minority view and
which the Fourth Circuit applied in Valtrol, Inc. v. General Connectors Corp., 884 F.2d 149 (4th Cir. 1989), the discrepant terms in the acceptance are ignored, so the offeror's terms control. Under the third, the majority view, applied by the Tenth Circuit in Daitom, Inc. v. Pennwalt Corp., 741 F.2d 1569 (10th Cir. 1984), and by courts in North Dakota and Michigan, the discrepant terms fall out and a suitable UCC gap-filler takes their place. Id. at 1178.
Posner evaluated the competing rules without adopting any as his own. The minority view may tempt the offeror to spring a surprise on the offeree, because the offeror's boilerplate prevails even when the offeree's response signals that it does not accede to those terms. The offeror also goes first and has a little more time for careful specification, and an offeror that wants to do business only on its own terms can protect itself by specifying that the offeree must accept all of them. Posner declared none of the three rules correct; the court's task was to predict Illinois law, and Judge Ripple's concurrence added that the policy choice among the views belongs to the states and to the drafting committee then revising Article 2.
Because the question was one of Illinois law, the court reasoned as a predictor. Illinois had not resolved the issue, but its courts in other UCC cases had tended to adopt majority rules, as in Rebaque v. Forsythe Racing,
Inc., and the Illinois Appellate Court in Tecumseh International Corp. v. City of Springfield had recognized that an offeror may condition acceptance on compliance with all of its terms. The Code's own goal of uniformity among the states pointed the same way. The court therefore began with a presumption that Illinois would adopt the majority view and found nothing to rebut it. Id. at 1178-79.
Applying the rule, the court found that the unlimited warranty in Northrop's acceptance was materially different from the 90-day warranty in Litronic's offer. Lynch had not made the broader warranty a condition of acceptance, so the expressly-conditional proviso of § 2-207(1) was not in play, and the limit on Lynch's authority, once satisfied, dropped out of the analysis. Under the majority view both warranty terms vanished, and § 2-309, which requires action within a reasonable time when the parties have not agreed on a period, filled the gap. The magistrate judge's finding that six months was reasonable in light of the complex testing these custom boards required was not clearly erroneous. Northrop thus received, in Posner's words, not the unlimited warranty its purchase order provided but a warranty of reasonable duration, "courtesy the court."
The court noted that the exchange could instead be analyzed under § 2-207(3) as a contract formed by conduct with terms supplied by the Code, but that route led to the same result and was not considered separately. Id. at 1179-80.
On the cross-appeal, § 2-711(3) gives a buyer who rightfully rejects goods a security interest in goods in its possession or control and permits the buyer to hold or resell them. That interest does not excuse the buyer from explaining what became of the goods. If the buyer does not return defective goods, it must account for them to dispel any inference that it is seeking a double recovery, and Northrop offered no explanation of what happened to the boards it kept. Id. at 1176-77.

== Separate Opinions ==
Judge Ripple concurred. He joined the judgment and agreed that the majority view was the correct prediction of Illinois law, but he declined to express an institutional view about which reading of § 2-207 is best. In his view that question belongs to the state courts and to the National Conference of Commissioners on Uniform
State Laws, whose drafting committee was then considering changes to § 2-207 intended to eliminate the battle of the forms, and federal judges with views on the subject should offer them in that process rather than in opinions predicting state law.

== Notes ==
The choice of rule determined the outcome. Under the minority view, Litronic's 90-day warranty in the offer would have controlled and Northrop's rejection would have been late. Under the California view, Northrop's unlimited warranty would have been a material alteration that failed to enter the contract, again leaving
Litronic's 90-day term in place. Only the knockout rule removed both terms and let the Code's reasonable-time standard rescue Northrop. A brief that says the court simply preferred the majority rule misstates the reasoning: Posner thought the California approach most sensible and applied the majority rule because he predicted that Illinois would.
Two further points connect the case to the rest of the assignment. First, the additional-versus-different distinction must be drawn before § 2-207(2) is applied in a majority-rule jurisdiction; in Bayway the Tax
Clause was analyzed as an additional term, so the knockout rule never arose. Second, Official Comment 6 states that where clauses on confirming forms conflict, each party is assumed to object to the other's clause and the conflicting terms do not become part of the contract; courts adopting the knockout rule frequently cite that comment, although Posner's opinion rested on the case law and the Code's uniformity policy rather than on Comment 6.
