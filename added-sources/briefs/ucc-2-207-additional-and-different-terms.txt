UCC § 2-207: Additional and Different Terms

Source: added-sources/Contracts_Battle_of_the_Forms_and_Rolling_Contracts_Briefs.pdf, pages 18–20

== Sources ==
UCC § 2-207 and its Official Comments 1 through 7, with §§ 2-104, 2-206, and 2-309 as supporting provisions. The earlier note on §§ 2-204 through 2-207 in this packet introduces the section; this note works through the four questions the assigned cases raise: who must prove material alteration and what surprise and hardship mean (Bayway); how different terms are treated (Northrop); whether the section applies when only one form exists (Hill and Klocek); and how the merchant rule changes the analysis for consumers (Klocek).
The text reproduced by Cornell is the model Code; the enacted version of the governing state controls an actual dispute, and the cases applied New York (Bayway), Illinois (Northrop), Illinois or South Dakota (Hill), and Kansas or Missouri (Klocek) law. § 2-207; § 2-206; § 2-104; § 2-309.

== Text ==
Subsection (1): a definite and seasonable expression of acceptance, or a written confirmation sent within a reasonable time, operates as an acceptance even though it states terms additional to or different from those offered or agreed upon, unless acceptance is expressly made conditional on assent to the additional or different terms. Subsection (2): the additional terms are construed as proposals for addition to the contract;
between merchants they become part of the contract unless (a) the offer expressly limits acceptance to the terms of the offer, (b) they materially alter it, or (c) notification of objection has already been given or is given within a reasonable time after notice of them is received. Subsection (3): conduct by both parties recognizing the existence of a contract establishes one even though the writings do not, and the terms then consist of those on which the writings agree together with supplementary terms incorporated under the Code.
Official Comment 1 identifies two situations the section covers: an agreement reached orally or by informal correspondence and followed by one or both parties sending formal memoranda that add terms, and an exchange of forms in which the offeree's response adds or changes terms. Comment 3 sends the question whether additional or different terms become part of the agreement to subsection (2). Comments 4 and 5 give examples of terms that do and do not materially alter a contract. Comment 6 addresses conflicting clauses in confirming forms. Comment 7 explains subsection (3).
The Merchant Rule and Consumers
Subsection (2)'s first sentence applies to everyone: additional terms are proposals. The second sentence, which makes proposals part of the contract by default, applies only between merchants, a term § 2-104 defines to include a person who deals in goods of the kind or who by occupation holds itself out as having relevant knowledge or skill. When either party is not a merchant, a proposal becomes part of the contract only if the other party assents to it. Klocek applied that rule to a consumer buyer and required express assent, holding that silence, failure to object, and retention of the goods beyond a stated period did not supply it on the record before the court. The merchant rule is therefore the hinge between the commercial cases and the consumer cases: Bayway's presumption of inclusion has no counterpart when the buyer is a consumer.
Material Alteration: Burden, Surprise, and Hardship
Comment 4 defines a material alteration as a term that would result in surprise or hardship if incorporated without the other party's express awareness, and lists examples such as a disclaimer of the implied warranties of merchantability and fitness, a clause reserving the seller's power to cancel on the buyer's failure to meet any invoice when due, and a clause requiring complaints in a time materially shorter than customary or reasonable. Comment 5 lists terms that ordinarily do not materially alter a contract, such as a clause setting a reasonable time for complaints, a clause fixing interest on overdue invoices at customary rates, and a clause limiting remedies in a reasonable manner. The examples are illustrations; Dorton treated materiality as a question of fact for the trial court.
Bayway settled two operational questions for the Second Circuit. The party opposing inclusion of the term bears the burden of proving material alteration, because subsection (2) makes inclusion the default and the exceptions must be established by the party invoking them. Surprise has a subjective component, what the party actually knew, and an objective component, what a reasonable merchant should have known; the objective component is proved through trade usage, course of dealing, and the parties' own forms. Hardship in Bayway was treated as at least doubtful as an independent ground, on Judge Posner's view that hardship is a consequence of a material alteration rather than a criterion, and a party's inability to afford a term it could have avoided did not qualify.
Different Terms
Subsection (1) refers to additional or different terms, and subsection (2) refers only to additional terms.
Northrop catalogues the three responses. Some courts treat different terms as additional terms and apply the materiality analysis of subsection (2); a materially different term in the acceptance then fails and the offeror's term stands. Others hold that different terms in the acceptance are simply ignored, so the offeror's terms control. The majority, applying what is commonly called the knockout rule, hold that conflicting terms cancel each other and the Code's gap-fillers supply the missing term; Comment 6, which states that where clauses on confirming forms conflict each party is assumed to object to the other's clause and the conflicting terms do not become part of the contract, is frequently cited for this approach. Northrop predicted that Illinois would follow the majority, so the seller's 90-day warranty and the buyer's unlimited warranty both dropped out and
§ 2-309's reasonable-time standard governed. A brief should identify the jurisdiction's approach before classifying a term, because the classification decides the outcome only in a knockout jurisdiction.
One Form, Confirmations, and Contracts by Conduct
Hill holds that § 2-207 is irrelevant when there is only one form, treating the section as confined to a battle between two forms. Klocek holds the opposite, relying on the statutory reference to "a written confirmation" and on Comment 1's first situation, in which one party's memorandum follows an oral agreement. Bayway illustrates the confirmation setting between merchants: the parties agreed by telephone, each sent a confirmation, and the seller's confirmation added a term that became part of the contract under subsection (2) because the buyer could not prove material alteration. The Hill and Klocek split also reflects a disagreement about who the offeror is. If the vendor is master of the offer and retention is acceptance, no acceptance with additional terms ever occurs; if the purchaser's order is the offer and shipment is acceptance, the terms in the box are additional terms under subsection (2) and, for a consumer, require express assent.
Subsection (3) supplies the fallback when the writings do not form a contract, as in C. Itoh, or when a court prefers to rest on conduct. Both parties' performance establishes the contract, and its terms are those on which the writings agree plus the Code's supplementary provisions; a term found in only one writing is excluded.
Northrop observed that analyzing the exchange under subsection (3) would have produced the same result as the knockout rule under subsection (2), because both routes replace the conflicting warranties with the Code's default.

== Application ==
Work in order. Identify the offer and the acceptance, or the prior agreement and the confirmation, and decide which document is being measured against which; in a consumer purchase, decide who the offeror is and be prepared to defend the choice, since Hill and Klocek diverge at that step. Ask whether the response is a definite and seasonable expression of acceptance or a timely confirmation, and whether it is expressly conditional in the strict sense Dorton and C. Itoh require. If a contract forms under subsection (1), classify each disputed term as additional or different and identify the jurisdiction's approach to different terms.
Determine merchant status; for a non-merchant, additional terms require assent. Between merchants, apply subsection (2)(a) through (c), placing the burden of proving material alteration on the party opposing the term and proving surprise objectively through usage and dealing. If the writings do not form a contract but the parties performed, apply subsection (3) and exclude any term found in only one writing. For a consumer transaction with terms delivered after payment, note that the Restatement of the Law, Consumer Contracts §
2(b) replaces this analysis with a notice-and-exit test that the business must satisfy.
