Zippo Manufacturing Co. v. Zippo Dot Com, Inc.
Zippo Manufacturing Co. v. Zippo Dot Com, Inc., 952 F. Supp. 1119 (W.D. Pa. 1997). United States District
Court for the Western District of Pennsylvania, decided January 16, 1997. District Judge Sean J.
McLaughlin issued the opinion. This is a federal trial-court decision on a pretrial motion, rather than a
Supreme Court or court-of-appeals ruling.
Full judicial opinion.

== Parties ==
Zippo Manufacturing Company, a Pennsylvania corporation headquartered in Bradford, manufactured Zippo lighters and owned the trademarks at issue. It was the plaintiff. Zippo Dot Com, Inc., a California corporation headquartered in Sunnyvale, operated an internet news service and website. It was the defendant moving to dismiss or transfer the action. The shared word in their names reflected the trademark dispute; the entities were separate businesses.

== Material Facts ==
Dot Com used the domain names zippo.com, zippo.net, and zipponews.com. Its website provided information, advertising, and an application for its news service. Users could obtain different membership levels, including paid subscriptions permitting access to and downloading of newsgroup messages stored on
California servers. Applicants supplied their names and addresses, paid by credit card over the internet or telephone, and received passwords after their applications were processed.
Dot Com had approximately 140,000 paying subscribers worldwide. Roughly 3,000, about 2 percent, were
Pennsylvania residents. It also had contracts with seven Pennsylvania internet-access providers allowing their subscribers to use its news service; two providers were in the Western District of Pennsylvania. Dot
Com had no Pennsylvania offices, employees, or agents, and its servers were in California. Its Pennsylvania relationships nevertheless involved repeated, knowing delivery of services to customers whose addresses it collected.
Manufacturing alleged trademark infringement, dilution, and false designation arising from Dot Com's use of “Zippo” in its domains, website, and message headings. Messages downloaded into Pennsylvania carried the allegedly infringing designation. Some messages contained sexually explicit material, which was relevant to the dilution allegations. The jurisdictional connection therefore involved delivery of the very messages challenged in the lawsuit, rather than mere availability of an unrelated website. 952 F. Supp. at 1121-22.

== Procedural History ==
Manufacturing filed a five-count complaint in the Western District of Pennsylvania. It asserted federal trademark claims, a Pennsylvania dilution claim, and requests for an accounting and constructive trust. Dot
Com moved to dismiss for lack of personal jurisdiction under Rule 12(b)(2) and improper venue under Rule
12(b)(3). Alternatively, it requested transfer under 28 U.S.C. §1406(a), which addresses cases filed in an improper venue.
The district court considered the motion before deciding infringement or damages. Once personal jurisdiction was challenged, Manufacturing bore the burden of presenting sufficient facts to make a prima facie showing of jurisdiction. Manufacturing expressly relied on specific jurisdiction and conceded that it was not asserting general jurisdiction. The court thus had to connect Dot Com's Pennsylvania conduct with the trademark claims, rather than decide whether its operations made it generally answerable in
Pennsylvania.
Pennsylvania's long-arm statute expressly authorized jurisdiction over a defendant contracting to supply services in the state and extended to the constitutional limit. The court examined purposeful contacts, the claims' connection to those contacts, and reasonableness. It then separately addressed venue under the version of 28 U.S.C. §1391 applicable in 1997. The published opinion denied the dismissal and transfer motion; it did not review a lower-court judgment or conclude the trademark litigation. 952 F. Supp. at
1121-23, 1127-28.

== Issue ==
Does an out-of-state internet service provider purposefully establish contacts supporting specific jurisdiction when it knowingly contracts with thousands of forum residents and local access providers and transmits material bearing the allegedly infringing mark into the forum? Is venue proper in the plaintiff's chosen district under the applicable federal venue statute?

== Holding ==
The court described a spectrum based on the nature and quality of commercial activity conducted online.
Knowing, repeated transmission of files under contracts with forum residents supports jurisdiction; merely posting information accessible from the forum does not by itself. For intermediate interactive sites, the degree of interaction and commercial character of the exchanges matter. Dot Com's actual subscriptions and continuing deliveries placed it at the commercial end of that spectrum. Those purposeful contacts gave rise to the asserted infringement, and jurisdiction was reasonable. Venue was also proper. 952 F. Supp. at
1124-28.

== Judgment ==
The district court denied Dot Com's motion to dismiss for lack of personal jurisdiction and improper venue, as well as its alternative request to transfer under §1406(a). The action could proceed in the Western District of Pennsylvania. The decision did not establish that the marks were infringed or diluted, impose liability, or determine the requested equitable remedies.

== Reasoning ==
The court treated internet transactions as a way to conduct ordinary interstate business. Physical entry into
Pennsylvania was unnecessary when the defendant deliberately created continuing relationships with people there. Dot Com did more than advertise a website or invite future contact: it processed Pennsylvania customers' applications, accepted payment, issued passwords, and repeatedly transmitted the service they purchased. Because customer addresses were collected, these were knowing forum relationships.
Dot Com argued that Pennsylvania residents had initiated contact by finding the website, making the resulting business fortuitous. The court rejected that characterization. A user's first approach did not compel
Dot Com to accept the subscription or continue delivering services. The defendant controlled those choices.
The opinion distinguished a service provider's deliberate Pennsylvania sales from an out-of-state subscriber independently forwarding a message into Pennsylvania or bringing a computer there. The former conduct belonged to the defendant; the latter would depend on a customer's unilateral action.
The company also emphasized that Pennsylvania customers were only 2 percent of its subscribers. The court looked to the quality and nature of the relationships rather than treating a small share of worldwide business as a jurisdictional exemption. Three thousand continuing customer contracts and seven provider agreements were substantial purposeful activity for a dispute arising from those transactions. This was a specific-jurisdiction analysis, so it did not require that Pennsylvania be the center of the company's overall operations.
Relatedness came from delivery of the challenged material. The messages carried the disputed “Zippo” designation into Pennsylvania, where subscribers viewed or downloaded them. Those alleged acts of infringement and dilution, together with the claimed injury to a Pennsylvania trademark owner, linked the litigation to the forum business. Pennsylvania's interest in protecting a resident company's marks and the plaintiff's interest in effective relief outweighed the burden of requiring the California business to defend consequences of its deliberately undertaken transactions.
Venue required its own statutory analysis. Under the then-applicable provisions of §1391(b)(1) and (c), the sole corporate defendant could be sued in a district in which it was deemed to reside through its amenability to personal jurisdiction. Dot Com's contrary authority involved a different venue basis, the location of substantial events under §1391(b)(2), and did not eliminate the corporate-residence alternative. With venue proper, the requested dismissal or transfer to cure improper venue was unwarranted. 952 F. Supp. at
1125-28.

== Separate Opinions ==
There were no separate opinions. Judge McLaughlin decided the motion as a single district judge.

== Notes ==
The holding rests on actual, repeated commercial relationships with Pennsylvania customers and claims arising from the delivered content. It does not mean that every interactive website creates jurisdiction in every place from which it can be accessed. The court's discussion of passive and intermediate sites explains its framework; the facts before it involved completed commercial transactions. Its status as a district-court opinion also matters: the sliding scale is not itself a Supreme Court holding. Read alongside Ford, the enduring questions remain the defendant's deliberate forum contacts and the connection between those contacts and the particular lawsuit. The venue discussion describes the statute applied in this 1997 decision.
