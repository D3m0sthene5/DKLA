International Filter Co. v. Conroe Gin, Ice & Light Co.
277 S.W. 631 (1925). Texas Commission of Appeals, Section A. Nickels, J. Decided December 10, 1925; disposition adopted by the Texas Supreme Court.
Source: added-sources/Contracts_Offer_and_Acceptance_Briefs.pdf, pages 6–7

== Preamble ==
The court held that Conroe Gin's signed order authorized International Filter to complete the contract through approval by an executive officer at its Chicago office. The officer's dated approval satisfied that specified method, and the writing did not require separate notice of approval. The court also concluded that the company's subsequent acknowledgment sufficiently communicated approval if notice was required.
International Filter, an Illinois manufacturer of water-purification equipment with its principal office in
Chicago, was the plaintiff and party seeking review. Conroe Gin, a Texas company manufacturing ice in
Conroe, was the defendant. International Filter prevailed on the formation issues, but the case returned to the
Court of Civil Appeals for unresolved questions; the reviewing court did not enter a final damages award.
277 S.W. at 631-33.
On February 10, 1920, International Filter's traveling representative, W. W. Waterman, presented a written proposal to Conroe Gin's manager, Henry Thompson. The proposal described a No. 2 Junior steel-tank water softener and filter for $1,230, to be furnished f.o.b. Chicago and suitable for water whose characteristics would be determined from a sample. The decisive language provided that the proposal would become a contract when accepted by the purchaser and approved by an executive officer of International Filter at its
Chicago office. The form also required modifications to be made through an approved supplementary agreement signed by both parties and stated that the proposal was submitted for prompt acceptance, with change without notice possible if prompt acceptance did not occur.
Thompson signed the paper for Conroe Gin on February 10, using an acceptance notation. The document also requested shipment by March 10. It then went to International Filter's Chicago office. On February 13,
P. N. Engel, identified in the opinion as the company's president and vice president, wrote a dated approval on the paper. On February 14, International Filter mailed a letter acknowledging and thanking Conroe Gin for the order, identifying the equipment and its electrical specifications, and referring to March 10 shipment.
The letter asked Conroe Gin to send a water sample promptly so the manufacturer could analyze the water before shipping the equipment. Conroe Gin received that letter in due course. 277 S.W. at 631-32.
Conroe Gin attempted to cancel the order in a February 28 letter and reiterated that position on March 4.
International Filter answered on March 2, denied a right to cancel, and insisted on performance. It then sued for breach. Conroe Gin argued that neither Engel's endorsement nor the February 14 letter constituted the executive approval contemplated by the proposal. It further argued that notice of approval was essential and had not been given. Thompson testified that, when he signed the order, he had told Waterman that prompt notification was required. The defense therefore relied both on its reading of the form and on a claimed oral instruction accompanying the signature.
The trial court heard the case without a jury and entered judgment for Conroe Gin, finding in its favor on the formation issues and other material issues. The Court of Civil Appeals affirmed at 269 S.W. 210. That court nevertheless agreed that Engel's endorsement was an approval within the document's terms; the principal remaining obstacle was the asserted need for notice. International Filter sought further review. The
Commission of Appeals recommended reversal of the intermediate appellate judgment and remand to that court for questions properly presented but not previously decided. Chief Justice Cureton, for the Texas
Supreme Court, adopted that disposition. This procedural endpoint matters because a favorable ruling on contract formation did not resolve every issue in the breach action.
The first analytical step is to identify who made the legally operative offer. International Filter prepared the form and called it a proposal, while Conroe Gin signed beneath language of acceptance. Even so, the form reserved a further act of approval for International Filter's executive officer. Conroe Gin's signature therefore supplied the offer to purchase on those terms. The company became the offeree with authority to accept through the reserved approval. The court expressly treated the signed paper sent to Chicago as
Conroe Gin's offer. Drafting the form and making the operative offer were separate roles. 277 S.W. at 632.
The court agreed that Engel's dated endorsement constituted approval by an executive officer at the designated place. No additional ritual was necessary to make that endorsement an approval. The remaining question was whether the contract also required communication of that approval to Conroe Gin. Although notice ordinarily protects an offeror who seeks a return promise, the offeror may dispense with it by authorizing a particular act as sufficient acceptance. Drawing on Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co., the court reasoned that the offeree completes the required acceptance by following the specified method when the offer makes that method sufficient.
The signed writing said that the proposal became a contract upon the executive officer's approval in
Chicago. Requiring notice to arrive in Conroe would add another condition and change both the designated time and place of formation. The separate prompt-acceptance provision addressed the timing of the required approval; it did not prescribe an additional form of acceptance or a notice requirement. The court treated the writing as unambiguous and judged the offer by those written terms despite the claimed oral demand for notification. Its reasoning supports a contractual waiver of notice in this transaction, while leaving the ordinary notice rule applicable when the offer does not dispense with it. 277 S.W. at 632-33.
The court supplied an alternative ground concerning the February 14 letter. Approval had already occurred through Engel's endorsement; the letter's possible role was to notify Conroe Gin of that existing act. A notice can communicate acceptance by reasonable implication without repeating a particular formula. The acknowledgment thanked the buyer for the specified order, identified the planned shipment, and requested the water sample needed for performance. The court concluded that a reasonable recipient in Conroe Gin's position would understand it to communicate approval. Its additional observation that reliance on the letter could have prevented International Filter from denying approval was hypothetical; the reported facts do not establish that Conroe Gin actually changed position in such reliance.
The narrow formation holding depends on the particular approval clause and the occurrence of the required executive act. An offeree's unexpressed decision to agree would not satisfy the prescribed act merely because the offer waived notification. Engel made an observable written endorsement of the document itself.
This explains the comparison with White: the controlling question in each case is whether the offeree performed an act that the offer and circumstances made sufficient to express assent. In International Filter, the writing expressly selected the act that completed the bargain; in White, the builder's general preparations did not communicate acceptance of the proposed job.
The case concerns common-law formation in a 1920 transaction and should be stated in those terms. A modern sale of equipment would also require attention to the applicable UCC provisions and to whether the language unambiguously restricts the means of acceptance.
