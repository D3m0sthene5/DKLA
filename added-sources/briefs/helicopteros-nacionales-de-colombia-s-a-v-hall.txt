Helicopteros Nacionales de Colombia, S.A. v. Hall
Helicopteros Nacionales de Colombia, S.A. v. Hall, 466 U.S. 408 (1984). United States Supreme Court, decided April 24, 1984. Justice Blackmun wrote for eight Justices. Justice Brennan dissented. The Court reviewed a Texas Supreme Court judgment sustaining jurisdiction after a wrongful-death trial.
Full judicial opinion.

== Parties ==
Helicopteros Nacionales de Colombia, S.A., usually called Helicol, was a Colombian corporation headquartered in Bogota and engaged in helicopter transportation in South America. It was a defendant below and the Supreme Court petitioner. The respondents were survivors or representatives of four United
States citizens killed in a helicopter crash in Peru. The decedents worked for a Peruvian consortium associated with Williams-Sedco-Horn, a joint venture headquartered in Houston. Other defendants included
Bell Helicopter and the related consortium or joint-venture entities. The Supreme Court's ruling concerned jurisdiction over Helicol, rather than the liability of every defendant.

== Material Facts ==
On January 26, 1976, a Helicol helicopter crashed in Peru while transporting workers connected to a pipeline project, killing four American passengers. Neither the decedents nor the plaintiffs were domiciled in Texas, although the workers had been hired in Houston. The alleged wrongful deaths arose from the operation of a helicopter abroad.
Helicol's chief executive had made a trip to Houston in 1974 to discuss transportation services with representatives of the consortium. The negotiations concerned matters including prices, fuel, and housing.
The resulting contract was executed in Peru, written in Spanish, and provided for disputes in Peruvian courts.
Payments to Helicol were drawn on a Houston bank, but were received in accounts in New York or Panama
City, Florida. Helicol had not selected the bank on which its customer's checks were drawn.
Between 1970 and 1977, Helicol purchased more than $4 million in helicopters, parts, and accessories from
Bell in Texas. These purchases represented about 80 percent of its helicopter fleet. It sent prospective pilots to Texas for training and to ferry purchased helicopters to South America; maintenance and management personnel also visited for training or consultation. Helicol had no Texas office, employees, operations, bank account, real estate, or regular sales activity. It was not licensed to do business there and had not appointed a
Texas agent for service. Its Texas contacts thus consisted principally of purchases, associated visits, one negotiation trip, and its customer's payment arrangements. 466 U.S. at 409-12.

== Procedural History ==
The plaintiffs brought wrongful-death suits in a Texas state district court in Harris County. Helicol made special appearances and moved to dismiss for lack of personal jurisdiction. The trial court overruled the objections. The cases were consolidated and tried to a jury, which returned a verdict against Helicol totaling
$1,141,200. The procedural posture therefore involved review of a jurisdictional objection preserved before an actual merits judgment.
The Texas Court of Civil Appeals reversed, concluding that Texas lacked personal jurisdiction over Helicol,
616 S.W.2d 247 (1981). The Texas Supreme Court initially affirmed that decision, over three dissents. On rehearing seven months later, it withdrew its earlier opinion and reversed the intermediate appellate court, again with three dissenters. The rehearing decision, 638 S.W.2d 870 (Tex. 1982), treated the Texas long-arm statute as reaching as far as federal due process permitted and held Helicol's contacts sufficient for jurisdiction.
The United States Supreme Court granted certiorari. A critical limitation on review came from the parties:
both treated the claims as neither arising out of nor relating to Helicol's Texas activities. The Court therefore considered only whether Texas had general jurisdiction over an unrelated controversy. It expressly declined to decide whether “arise out of” and “relate to” describe different connections or whether a sufficiently related claim might support specific jurisdiction. Those issues were not the basis on which the parties had presented the case. 466 U.S. at 412-16 & n.10.

== Issue ==
Can Texas exercise general personal jurisdiction over a Colombian helicopter operator in wrongful-death actions arising from a Peruvian crash when the company's Texas contacts consist of substantial purchases, related training trips, one negotiation visit, and acceptance of checks drawn on a Texas bank, and the parties treat the claims as unrelated to those contacts?

== Holding ==
The company's contacts did not constitute the continuous and systematic general business contacts necessary to support jurisdiction over an unrelated cause of action. Purchases from a forum seller, even in substantial amounts and accompanied by training trips, do not alone authorize general jurisdiction. The Court decided this as a general-jurisdiction case because of the parties' concession about relatedness. Goodyear and
Daimler later articulate the general-jurisdiction standard as whether the corporation is essentially at home in the forum. 466 U.S. at 416-18.

== Judgment ==
The Supreme Court reversed the Texas Supreme Court. Texas could not sustain the judgment against Helicol on the asserted general-jurisdiction basis. The decision did not find the helicopter safe, reject the wrongful-death allegations on their merits, or determine the liability of the other defendants. It held that this
Texas court lacked the asserted adjudicatory authority over Helicol.

== Reasoning ==
The Court compared Helicol's activities with the extensive Ohio management operations in Perkins. Helicol had no comparable office or corporate center in Texas. The single Houston negotiation trip did not resemble continuing management of its business from the forum. Nor did the place where its customer banked materially establish purposeful corporate activity by Helicol. The customer's unilateral choice of a Texas bank could not be attributed to Helicol as a meaningful jurisdictional contact.
The purchases were substantial, but their commercial importance did not make Texas a forum for all claims against the buyer. Relying on Rosenberg Bros. & Co. v. Curtis Brown Co., the Court reasoned that purchasing goods and sending representatives to the seller's state did not establish the kind of corporate presence needed for an unrelated action. The training visits were connected to the purchases and did not independently transform that relationship. Helicol conducted transportation operations in South America; it did not provide those services in Texas.
The plaintiffs' interest in a common forum for all defendants also did not establish jurisdiction. The Court rejected a suggested necessity rationale because the record did not show that all defendants could not be sued together in Peru or Colombia. It did not decide whether jurisdiction by necessity might ever be constitutionally permissible. The absence of proof was enough to reject its use here.
The distinction between purchasing helicopters and litigating claims connected to a helicopter matters because the majority did not resolve the latter as a specific-jurisdiction question. Its holding cannot be expanded into a conclusion that these same transactions would be legally irrelevant to every possible claim.
The parties' concession defined the issue, and the Court confined the controlling ruling to general jurisdiction. 466 U.S. at 415-18 & nn.10, 13.

== Separate Opinions ==
Justice Brennan dissented. He believed Helicol's Texas dealings, considered together, were sufficiently substantial for jurisdiction. More importantly, he argued that the dispute was sufficiently related to those dealings to permit specific jurisdiction even if it did not technically arise from them. The transportation arrangement was negotiated in Houston; the helicopter involved had been purchased in Texas, and the pilot had trained there. In his view, those links made Texas a fair and foreseeable forum for claims arising from the transportation relationship. He criticized treating relatedness as identical to strict causation and regarded the majority as overlooking the actual relationship between the forum transactions and the accident. 466 U.S.
at 418-28.

== Notes ==
The procedural concession explains why a case with seemingly relevant Texas transactions became a leading restriction on general jurisdiction. Brennan's distinction between claims that arise from and claims that relate to forum contacts anticipates the issue later resolved in Ford, but his proposed application in this case was a dissent. Ford does not retroactively turn the majority in Helicopteros into a specific-jurisdiction decision.
The proper comparison is between the different grounds presented and decided in the two cases.
