C. Itoh & Co. (America) Inc. v. Jordan International Co.
C. Itoh & Co. (America) Inc. v. Jordan International Co., 552 F.2d 1228 (7th Cir. 1977). United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit, decided April 4, 1977. Judge Sprecher wrote the opinion. == Parties ==
C. Itoh & Co. (America) Inc. was the buyer, plaintiff, and appellee. The Jordan International Company was the steel seller, defendant, and appellant. Itoh sought judicial relief for allegedly defective steel and late delivery. Jordan contended that the dispute had to be arbitrated under a provision in its acknowledgment and sought to stay the litigation pending arbitration.

== Material Facts ==
Itoh sent Jordan a purchase order for steel coils. Jordan responded with its acknowledgment form. Its language expressly conditioned the seller's acceptance on the buyer's assent to the additional or different terms appearing in the form and on its reverse side. It also directed the buyer to notify the seller immediately if the terms were unacceptable. One reverse-side provision required arbitration. Itoh's purchase order contained no corresponding arbitration clause.
The writings therefore did not establish agreement on arbitration. Jordan nevertheless delivered the steel, and
Itoh paid for it. Itoh later complained that the steel was defective and had arrived late. The parties' performance showed that they were carrying out a sales transaction even though the seller had never obtained the assent required by its expressly conditional acknowledgment. The question became which terms governed the contract demonstrated by that performance.

== Procedural History ==
Itoh filed suit against Jordan based on the alleged defects and late delivery. Jordan moved to stay the proceedings pending arbitration, relying on its acknowledgment. The trial court denied that motion. Jordan appealed the denial to the Seventh Circuit.
The appellate inquiry addressed whether the arbitration clause belonged to the sales contract under § 2-207.
The question did not require a final decision about the quality of the steel, the timeliness of delivery, or the amount of damages. The Seventh Circuit affirmed the denial of the stay on the ground that the contract created by conduct did not include the unagreed arbitration provision.

== Issue ==
When a seller expressly conditions acceptance on the buyer's assent to additional terms, the buyer does not assent, and both parties nevertheless perform, does § 2-207(3) create a contract? If so, can the seller's arbitration clause become one of that contract's terms through the buyer's receipt and payment or through the provision for supplementary Code terms?

== Holding ==
The seller's acknowledgment satisfied the expressly-conditional proviso in § 2-207(1). The exchange of writings therefore did not itself create a contract; the acknowledgment operated as a counteroffer. The parties' subsequent performance nevertheless established a contract under § 2-207(3).
That contract included the terms on which the writings agreed and applicable supplementary Code terms.
The parties' writings did not agree to arbitration, and Article 2 did not supply arbitration as a gap-filling term. The unaccepted clause was therefore excluded. Delivery and payment established the sales contract without establishing assent to that additional clause.

== Judgment ==
The Seventh Circuit affirmed the denial of Jordan's motion to stay the proceedings pending arbitration. Itoh could continue litigating its claims in court. The decision resolved the asserted obligation to arbitrate; it did not establish that Itoh had proved breach or its claimed damages.

== Reasoning ==
Jordan's form used the condition that Dorton's forms lacked: it expressly tied acceptance to the buyer's assent to additional or different terms. That language prevented contract formation through the writings unless the condition was satisfied. The seller could not rely on § 2-207(1)'s ordinary rule treating a nonmatching acknowledgment as an acceptance while simultaneously insisting that its acceptance was withheld pending further assent.
The failure of the writings to form a contract did not end the analysis. Section 2-207(3) expressly recognizes contracts demonstrated by both parties' conduct. Jordan delivered and Itoh paid. Those reciprocal acts supplied objective evidence of a sales relationship. The Code thus avoided treating a completed commercial exchange as legally nonexistent merely because the parties' forms had failed to match or form a contract on their own.
The same subsection determined the terms. Its agreed-terms rule excluded Jordan's additional arbitration provision because the buyer's purchase order did not agree to it. Requiring an express contradiction in the buyer's form would defeat that rule: a term present only in the seller's form still was not a term on which both writings agreed. Neither the seller's last word in the exchange nor the buyer's ordinary performance automatically made that provision binding.
Jordan also could not reintroduce the arbitration clause as a supplementary term. The court interpreted that language as referring to the standardized gap-filling provisions of Article 2. Such provisions can complete a contract by supplying applicable default obligations. They do not serve as a route for importing disputed boilerplate that the agreed-terms rule has just excluded. Arbitration was not a necessary missing term supplied by the Code. The sales contract could operate with disputes resolved through the ordinary judicial process.
The court also addressed fairness to a seller choosing an expressly conditional form. Before the buyer assents or the parties proceed with conduct creating a contract, the seller benefits from not being committed through the writings alone. If the additional terms truly are essential, it can withhold delivery until it obtains the required assent. If it elects to deliver without that assent, it accepts the risk that the conduct-based contract will omit its unagreed terms. Giving the seller both freedom from a contract when it wishes to withdraw and automatic inclusion of its boilerplate when it wishes to perform would distort the statutory allocation.

== Notes ==
C. Itoh illustrates the cost of making acceptance expressly conditional. That drafting can prevent formation through the forms, but it does not guarantee that the seller's preferred terms will govern if the parties proceed anyway. If agreement to arbitration is essential, the seller must obtain the requisite assent before relying on the conditional language and performing.
The comparison with Dorton turns on the formation route. Dorton required further examination under subsections (1) and (2), including material alteration, because the acknowledgments did not clearly condition acceptance on assent. C. Itoh proceeded under subsection (3), where the additional arbitration term was excluded because it was not agreed upon and was not supplied by the Code. Its exclusion did not depend on deciding the material-alteration question that remained open in Dorton.
