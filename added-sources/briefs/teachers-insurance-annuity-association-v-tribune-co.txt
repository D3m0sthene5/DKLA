Teachers Insurance & Annuity Association v. Tribune Co.
Teachers Insurance & Annuity Ass'n of America v. Tribune Co., 670 F. Supp. 491 (S.D.N.Y. 1987). United
States District Court for the Southern District of New York, No. 83 Civ. 0047, decided May 27, 1987, as amended June 26, 1987. Judge Pierre N. Leval wrote the opinion after a bench trial. The case concerns whether a signed loan commitment letter that left terms open bound the borrower, and it states the now-standard distinction between two kinds of binding preliminary agreements. Full judicial opinion.
Source: added-sources/Contracts_Definiteness_and_Preliminary_Agreements_Briefs.pdf, pages 3–5

== Parties ==
Teachers Insurance and Annuity Association of America (TIAA), a large institutional lender, was the plaintiff. It had issued a commitment to lend $76 million to the Tribune Company. Tribune, the newspaper publisher, was the defendant. TIAA sought damages for Tribune's refusal to go forward with the loan;
Tribune maintained that the commitment letter was not binding and that its obligation was conditioned on favorable accounting treatment that it never obtained.

== Material Facts ==
In 1982 Tribune sought to sell the Daily News Building at 220 East 42nd Street in Manhattan. The buyer,
LaSalle Partners, would pay largely through a long-term purchase-money mortgage, meaning a loan from the seller secured by the property sold. To obtain cash immediately without showing the transaction as new debt,
Tribune planned to borrow a matching amount from a lender and to use "offset" accounting, under which the mortgage owed to it and the loan it owed would cancel each other on its balance sheet. Tribune circulated an offering to lenders in August 1982 and sought a firm commitment by mid-September. 670 F. Supp. at
492-93.
On September 22, 1982, TIAA issued a commitment letter for a $76 million loan with a fourteen-year term at a 15.25 percent yield, accompanied by a two-page term sheet covering the principal economic terms. The letter stated that upon TIAA's receipt of an accepted counterpart, the parties' agreement "shall become a binding agreement between us," and it contemplated the preparation and execution of documents satisfactory to TIAA. Nothing in the letter or term sheet made the loan contingent on Tribune's ability to use offset accounting. Tribune's lawyers had warned against signing a letter with binding-agreement language, but
Tribune, fearing loss of the commitment, accepted on October 6, adding that its acceptance was subject to approval by its board of directors and to preparation of documentation satisfactory to Tribune. The board met on October 28 and authorized the borrowing, leaving final terms to its finance committee. TIAA allocated the
$76 million within its lending budget. Id. at 493-95, 502-03.
Meanwhile the Financial Accounting Standards Board had issued a proposal in mid-October questioning offsetting of this kind, and Tribune's accountants grew concerned. Interest rates also fell sharply, making the
15.25 percent loan much less attractive. On December 2, Tribune proposed to proceed only if the loan were conditioned on assurance that offset accounting would be available, and TIAA refused. Tribune closed the sale of the building on December 6 and did not proceed with the loan. Id. at 495-96.

== Procedural History ==
TIAA sued Tribune in the Southern District of New York for breach of the commitment. After a bench trial,
Judge Leval issued findings of fact and conclusions of law. Liability was decided in this opinion, and the measure of damages was reserved for later determination. New York law governed.

== Issue ==
Did the commitment letter, which stated that it would become binding on acceptance but left terms open and contemplated final documentation, create an enforceable obligation? If it did not bind Tribune to the loan itself, did it bind Tribune to negotiate the open terms in good faith, and did Tribune breach that obligation by insisting that the loan be conditioned on offset accounting?

== Holding ==
Preliminary agreements can create binding obligations of two types. In the first, the parties have reached complete agreement on all the issues perceived to require negotiation, and the contemplated formal document is only a memorial of an agreement already binding; either party may demand performance of the transaction.
In the second, the parties express mutual commitment to a contract on agreed major terms while recognizing that other terms remain to be negotiated. That agreement does not commit the parties to their ultimate contractual objective; it commits them to negotiate the open issues in good faith within the framework they have settled. It bars a party from renouncing the deal, abandoning the negotiations, or insisting on conditions that do not conform to the preliminary agreement, but it does not guarantee that a final contract will result.
Whether the parties intended to be bound is determined from their language, the context of the negotiations, the existence of open terms, partial performance, and the customary practice in the market. Id. at 497-99.
The commitment letter was a binding preliminary commitment of the second type. Offset accounting was not a condition of Tribune's obligation, and Tribune breached its duty to negotiate in good faith when it refused to proceed unless TIAA accepted that new condition. Tribune's refusal was primarily attributable to the decline in interest rates. Id. at 499-507.

== Judgment ==
Judgment on liability was entered for TIAA, with damages to be determined in further proceedings.

== Reasoning ==
Judge Leval began with the risks on both sides. Courts must avoid trapping parties in surprise contractual obligations they never intended, because parties commonly exchange preliminary writings while reserving the freedom to walk away. But courts should also enforce commitments parties did intend, because giving legal recognition to preliminary binding commitments serves a valuable function in the marketplace, particularly for relatively standardized transactions such as loans. A borrower that obtains a firm commitment gains certainty about its financing and can plan around it; a lender that issues one forgoes other opportunities.
If either could walk away whenever the market moved, the commitment would be worthless. Id. at 497-99.
The first factor, the language of the agreement, strongly favored TIAA. The commitment letter called itself a binding agreement upon acceptance, and Tribune signed it as accepted and agreed. Tribune's reservations, board approval and satisfactory documentation, did not negate that intent. The board did approve the borrowing, and the court read the reservation of satisfactory documentation as consistent with a commitment on the major terms and a duty to negotiate the rest, rather than as a right to abandon the deal for any reason.
Id. at 499-500, 503.
The context of the negotiations pointed the same way. Tribune had sought a firm commitment by a deadline because it needed certainty about the financing for the building sale. Its lawyers warned it about the binding-agreement language, and Tribune signed anyway rather than risk losing the commitment, which showed that it understood the letter to bind it. Id. at 500-01.
The open terms did not defeat binding intent. The term sheet covered the major economic terms, including the amount, rate, and term of the loan. The terms left open were the kind of secondary provisions that a binding preliminary commitment contemplates the parties will negotiate. Open terms are fatal to a claim that the parties reached a fully binding final agreement, but they are consistent with a commitment to negotiate.
TIAA's partial performance, allocating $76 million of its lending capacity to the loan, and evidence that the financial community recognizes binding loan commitments, though not uniformly, supported the conclusion.
Id. at 501-03.
Tribune's principal defense was that offset accounting was an essential condition of the deal. The court rejected it. The commitment letter contained no basis whatever for the proposition that Tribune's obligation was conditioned on assurance that it could report the loan off its balance sheet. Tribune knew the importance of the accounting treatment and could have demanded such a condition, negotiated for a right to prepay if the treatment was disallowed, or paid for an option. It instead accepted a firm commitment without the condition, because it did not want to lose the commitment. Id. at 504-05.
Tribune breached its obligation. When interest rates fell, it refused to proceed unless TIAA agreed to a condition outside the scope of the preliminary agreement, and when TIAA declined, it abandoned the negotiations. The court found that Tribune's position was primarily attributable to the decline in rates, which had made the loan unattractive, rather than to its accounting concerns. Insisting on a condition the preliminary agreement did not contain was exactly what the duty to negotiate in good faith forbids. The court noted that Judge Weinfeld had reached a similar result in Teachers Insurance & Annuity Ass'n v. Butler, 626
F. Supp. 1229 (S.D.N.Y. 1986), where a borrower refused to negotiate a prepayment term within the scope of its commitment. Id. at 505-08.

== Separate Opinions ==
A single district judge decided the case after a bench trial. There is no separate opinion.

== Notes ==
The two categories Judge Leval described are now commonly called Type I and Type II preliminary agreements; the labels come from later Second Circuit decisions applying this opinion and are standard vocabulary in the case law, although Leval described the categories without numbering them. The first type obligates the parties to the transaction itself; the second obligates them only to negotiate in good faith within the agreed framework, so the injured party's remedy is for the failed negotiation, and the measure of damages is contested. The opinion reserved damages, so it does not settle whether TIAA could recover the benefit of the lost loan or only its reliance losses.
The case connects to Sun Printing. Cardozo held that an agreement to agree on price and term left the seller free to refuse, but he noted that the buyer had not sued for an arbitrary refusal to negotiate. Tribune recognizes that claim when the parties intended to commit to negotiate. The limits matter: the duty does not require either party to accept terms it reasonably rejects, and a party may fail to reach agreement without liability if it negotiated in good faith. The breach lies in renouncing the deal, abandoning negotiations, or demanding terms outside the agreed framework.
