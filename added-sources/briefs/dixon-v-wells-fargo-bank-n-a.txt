Dixon v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A.
Dixon v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A., 798 F. Supp. 2d 336 (D. Mass. 2011). United States District Court for the
District of Massachusetts, Civil Action No. 11-10585, memorandum and order of July 22, 2011, by Judge
William G. Young. The case concerns whether homeowners stated a promissory estoppel claim based on a lender's promise to consider them for a loan modification if they stopped making payments. Full judicial opinion.
Source: added-sources/Contracts_Definiteness_and_Preliminary_Agreements_Briefs.pdf, pages 6–8

== Parties ==
Frank and Deana Dixon, homeowners in Scituate, Massachusetts, were the plaintiffs. Wells Fargo Bank,
N.A., which held the mortgage on their home, was the defendant. The Dixons sought to stop the foreclosure of their home and to hold Wells Fargo to its promise; Wells Fargo moved to dismiss, arguing that no enforceable promise existed and that federal law preempted the claim.

== Material Facts ==
The facts come from the complaint, which the court accepted as true on a motion to dismiss. On or about
June 8, 2009, the Dixons and Wells Fargo orally agreed to take the steps necessary to enter into a modification of their mortgage loan. Wells Fargo instructed them to stop making payments on the loan and told them that the unpaid payments would be added to the modified note. It also asked for financial information, which the Dixons promptly supplied. The Dixons stopped paying. Wells Fargo never completed the modification process. On or about December 8, 2010, the Dixons received notice from the Massachusetts
Land Court that Wells Fargo was proceeding with foreclosure. The value of their home exceeded the loan balance and any arrearage. 798 F. Supp. 2d at 339.

== Procedural History ==
The Dixons sued in Massachusetts Superior Court, which issued a preliminary injunction against the foreclosure on February 14, 2011. Wells Fargo removed the case to federal court and moved to dismiss under
Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6), which tests whether the complaint states a claim on which relief can be granted. At a hearing on May 9, 2011, Judge Young dismissed the breach of contract claim as insufficiently pleaded and took the promissory estoppel claim and Wells Fargo's preemption defense under advisement. This opinion explains the ruling on those remaining issues. Id. at 338-39.

== Issue ==
Does a lender's promise to consider borrowers for a loan modification if they stop making payments and provide financial information support a claim for promissory estoppel under Massachusetts law, even though the promise is too indefinite to create a contract to modify the loan? Is such a claim preempted by the Home
Owners' Loan Act and its regulations?

== Holding ==
Massachusetts enforces promises by virtue of reliance under Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 90. The promise must be definite enough to show a commitment, but it need not be so comprehensive that it would qualify as an offer that could ripen into a contract. A promise to consider borrowers for a loan modification, conditioned on their defaulting and supplying financial information, is sufficiently definite when the borrowers took those steps in reasonable reliance and the lender then used the default to foreclose. Recovery is confined to the value of the borrowers' reliance, which avoids converting preliminary negotiations into a binding modification. The claim was not preempted, because it seeks to enforce a general duty to honor promises that applies to all businesses rather than to regulate lending. Id. at 340-59.

== Judgment ==
The court denied Wells Fargo's motion to dismiss as to the promissory estoppel claim and placed the case on the September 2011 trial list. The breach of contract claim had already been dismissed.

== Reasoning ==
The contract claim failed because the Dixons alleged at most an agreement to enter into a loan modification whose terms were left for future negotiation, and such an agreement is too indefinite to be enforced. The
Dixons clarified that they did not seek a modification; they sought to hold Wells Fargo to its promise to consider them for one. Id. at 339, 341.
Massachusetts courts had avoided the label "promissory estoppel" since Loranger Construction Corp. v. E.F.
Hauserman Co., 376 Mass. 757 (1978), but they enforce promises by virtue of reliance and treat such a claim as equivalent to a contract claim without consideration. They have adopted § 90, under which a promise that the promisor should reasonably expect to induce action or forbearance, and that does induce it, is binding if injustice can be avoided only by enforcement. Some Massachusetts decisions required the promise to be definite and certain in its terms. Judge Young read § 90 and the Restatement's definition of a promise in § 2 to require a commitment, meaning a manifestation of intention to act or refrain from acting in a specified way, but not a promise comprehensive enough to be an offer, citing Hoffman v. Red Owl Stores, Inc. for the same point. Id. at 340-44.
Massachusetts cases had applied reliance-based recovery where one party's pattern of conduct kept the other on a string during negotiations, as in Pappas Industrial Parks, Inc. v. Psarros and Greenstein v. Flatley. A
Superior Court decision had allowed a similar claim where a bank encouraged a borrower to default to obtain a discounted payoff and the loan was then sold to a buyer who foreclosed. The Dixons' allegations fit that pattern. Wells Fargo told them that stopping payment and submitting financial information were the steps necessary to obtain a modification; it should have known, and must have intended, that they would take those steps expecting a modification. Its promise to consider them therefore carried, as a matter of fair dealing, an undertaking not to foreclose on the basis of the very default its promise had produced. Only because the
Dixons relied and stopped paying was Wells Fargo able to begin foreclosure, and foreclosing without warning was, the court wrote, unseemly conduct at best. Id. at 344-46.
The court addressed the concern that enforcing promises made during negotiations would restrict parties' freedom to negotiate by limiting the remedy. If the Dixons proved their allegations, their damages would be confined to the value of their expenditures in reliance on the promise. A footnote illustrated the idea: the
Dixons would be restored to their position before the default by resuming their original payments, with the missed payments added to the loan balance and amortized over the life of the loan; if they could not resume
payments, Wells Fargo could foreclose. The remedy protects reliance without imposing a modification the parties never agreed to. Id. at 346-47, 349 n.2.
The court then rejected preemption under the Home Owners' Loan Act (HOLA) and the Office of Thrift
Supervision regulation, 12 C.F.R. § 560.2. The Dixons did not seek a modification or any particular loan terms, so the claim did not purport to impose requirements on loan terms or servicing. Contract and tort law are presumptively not preempted, and promissory estoppel is not designed to regulate lending; all businesses owe the duty to honor promises made. Because HOLA provides no private right of action, Congress could not have intended to deny borrowers traditional state-law remedies for a lender's unfair conduct. Id. at
354-59.

== Separate Opinions ==
A single district judge decided the motion. There is no separate opinion.

== Notes ==
The case separates two questions that indefiniteness can blur. The alleged promise to modify the loan was too indefinite to be a contract, because its terms were left for negotiation. The narrower promise to consider the
Dixons for a modification, together with the instruction to stop paying, was definite enough to support reliance-based recovery. The remedy tracks that distinction: the Dixons could not obtain the modification they hoped for, but they could be protected against the loss caused by defaulting at the bank's direction.
The decision arose on a motion to dismiss, so it holds only that the allegations stated a claim; the facts remained to be proved at trial. It also reflects the context of the post-2008 foreclosure crisis, which the court discussed at length. Compared with Cyberchron, Dixon relies more heavily on the lender's opportunistic conduct in using a default it induced, while Cyberchron emphasizes a buyer's demands for continued performance during a negotiation it then abandoned; both cases limit recovery to reliance.
