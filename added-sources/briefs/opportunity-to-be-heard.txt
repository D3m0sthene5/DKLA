== Sources ==
Casebook Chapter 3, Part D, pp. 278-296, assigned to be read with care although it will not be covered by case presentation. The section applies Mullane's statement that due process requires notice and an opportunity for hearing appropriate to the nature of the case. The Court's decisions since the 1960s fall into two groups: challenges to private creditors' use of provisional remedies (Sniadach, Fuentes, Mitchell, North
Georgia Finishing, and Doehr), and claims to a hearing before the government withdraws a benefit (Goldberg and Mathews). Sniadach, Fuentes, and Doehr are briefed separately.
Provisional Remedies
A provisional remedy is a court order, obtained at the start of a suit or while it is pending, that seizes or encumbers the defendant's property for the plaintiff's benefit before any judgment. Attachment seizes or places a lien on the defendant's property to secure satisfaction of a possible judgment. Garnishment is attachment of property that a third party, called the garnishee, holds for the defendant, such as wages owed by an employer or funds in a bank. Replevin is an action to recover possession of specific personal property, and a prejudgment writ of replevin lets the plaintiff take the property before trial. Sequestration, as used in
Louisiana, is a court order holding property in the court's custody during the suit. These remedies derive from common-law writs and are now statutory; they were traditionally treated as exceptions to the requirement of prior notice and hearing.
The Prejudgment Remedy Cases
Sniadach v. Family Finance Corp. (1969) struck down Wisconsin's prejudgment wage garnishment, which froze wages on a summons issued at the creditor's lawyer's request with no opportunity for a hearing before trial. Justice Harlan's concurrence framed the requirement as notice and a hearing aimed at establishing the validity, or at least the probable validity, of the claim before the deprivation. The decision left open whether its rationale reached property other than wages and what extraordinary situations could justify seizure without a prior hearing.
Fuentes v. Shevin (1972) answered broadly. By a vote of four to three, it invalidated Florida and
Pennsylvania statutes that allowed a creditor to obtain a writ of replevin for household goods on a conclusory application and a bond, with no prior hearing. Temporary deprivations count, a buyer's possessory interest in goods bought on installment is property, the goods need not be necessities, and seizure without a hearing is permissible only in truly unusual situations involving an important public interest, a special need for prompt action, and a government official's decision under a narrowly drawn statute.
Mitchell v. W.T. Grant Co., 416 U.S. 600 (1974), upheld a Louisiana sequestration statute allowing ex parte seizure (seizure on one party's application without notice to the other) where the applicant claimed a possessory interest and the defendant could conceal, waste, remove, or dispose of the property. The suit concerned an overdue installment balance, and the Court emphasized the vendor's interest in preventing waste of the property, the court's involvement in authorizing the writ, the suitability of the dispute for documentary proof, and the immediate availability of a post-seizure hearing. North Georgia Finishing, Inc. v.
Di-Chem, Inc., 419 U.S. 601 (1975), then invalidated a Georgia garnishment statute because the writ issued on conclusory allegations without an early hearing or other safeguard against mistaken repossession.
Connecticut v. Doehr (1991) invalidated, as applied, a statute allowing prejudgment attachment of a home on a tort plaintiff's affidavit, without prior notice or hearing, extraordinary circumstances, or a bond. It adapted
the Mathews factors to disputes between private parties, giving principal weight to the interest of the party seeking the remedy.
Factors the Cases Used
The casebook observes that the cases never stated an overall test and identifies the factors they used. The first factor is who decides: a judge or a clerk. The second is what interest the applicant has: whether it has a pre-existing interest in the property. The third is when the hearing occurs: before the seizure or immediately afterward. The fourth is why the seizure is sought: to establish jurisdiction or to provide security, and, if for security, whether exceptional circumstances such as a risk of destruction of the asset exist. The fifth is how the applicant must proceed: whether it must show probable cause or meet a lesser standard, whether its allegations must be sworn and based on personal knowledge, and whether the dispute can be resolved by uncontested documents. The casebook also asks whether the Sniadach line should apply when attachment is used to obtain jurisdiction, as in Shaffer.
Government Benefits and the Mathews Test
Until the mid-twentieth century, government benefits such as welfare payments, civil service jobs, and licenses were not treated as property protected by due process. Their inclusion, associated with Charles
Reich's article The New Property, was a major constitutional development. In Goldberg v. Kelly, 397 U.S.
254 (1970), the Court held that a welfare recipient is entitled to an evidentiary hearing before benefits are terminated, because termination pending resolution of a dispute may deprive an eligible recipient of the very means by which to live while waiting; the hearing need not be a full trial but must be tailored to the capacities and circumstances of those to be heard.
In Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S. 319 (1976), the Court held that a pretermination hearing was not required before Social Security disability benefits were cut off, and it stated a three-factor balancing test: first, the private interest affected by the official action; second, the risk of an erroneous deprivation through the procedures used and the probable value of additional or substitute safeguards; and third, the government's interest, including the function involved and the fiscal and administrative burdens that additional procedures would entail. The casebook asks whether applying Mathews would have changed the outcome in Fuentes and how the Sniadach factors fit the Mathews framework; Doehr supplies the Court's own answer for prejudgment remedies.
Limits and Later Applications
Due process hearing rights attach to adjudicative action, not legislation. In Jones v. Governor of Florida, 975
F.3d 1016 (11th Cir. 2020) (en banc), the court reversed an injunction against Florida's requirement that people with felony convictions pay outstanding fines, fees, and restitution before voting. Relying on
Bi-Metallic Investment Co. v. State Board of Equalization, the majority held that the deprivation occurred through legislative action, meaning a general law applying prospectively to more than a few people, so the affected persons were entitled to no process beyond the legislative process and Mathews did not apply. A dissent argued, through an example about unpaid traffic citations, that denying people the information needed to comply with a general law could not be constitutionally permissible.
Other notes show the factors at work. Shaumyan v. O'Neill, 987 F.2d 122 (2d Cir. 1993), upheld the same
Connecticut attachment statute as applied to a contractor seeking payment for repairs, because the dispute turned largely on written documentation and the contractor had a substantial pre-existing interest in the home once its labor and materials were incorporated. Diaz v. Paterson, 547 F.3d 88 (2d Cir. 2008), upheld New
York's lis pendens statute, which lets a plaintiff claiming an interest in real property record a notice of the pending suit, applying the Mathews factors as construed in Doehr. Bennis v. Michigan, 516 U.S. 442 (1996), rejected a wife's due process challenge to forfeiture of a jointly owned car used by her husband in a crime, relying on a long line of cases permitting forfeiture of an innocent owner's interest. Nelson v. Colorado, 137
S. Ct. 1249 (2017), held under Mathews that Colorado could not require defendants whose convictions were invalidated to prove innocence by clear and convincing evidence to recover fees, costs, and restitution they had paid.
The final note addresses counsel. In Lassiter v. Department of Social Services, 452 U.S. 18 (1981), the Court recognized a presumption that an indigent litigant has a right to appointed counsel only when loss of physical liberty is at stake and held that due process did not require counsel before termination of a mother's parental rights on the facts presented. In Turner v. Rogers, 564 U.S. 431 (2011), the Court held that due process does not automatically require counsel for an indigent parent facing civil contempt incarceration for nonpayment of child support, where the opposing parent is unrepresented and the State provides alternative safeguards, but it found those safeguards missing and held that the incarceration violated due process.

== Application ==
First decide whether there is a deprivation of property or liberty by state action through an adjudicative process; Jones shows that legislative action falls outside the hearing requirement. For a prejudgment remedy between private parties, apply Doehr's version of Mathews: weigh the defendant's property interest, including partial and temporary impairments; the risk of error given who decides, what showing is required, and whether the dispute is documentary or fact-intensive; the value of safeguards such as a prior hearing, an immediate post-deprivation hearing, or a bond; and the plaintiff's interest, especially any pre-existing interest in the property or evidence that the defendant will dissipate it. For a government benefit, apply the three
Mathews factors directly. In either setting, extraordinary circumstances may justify postponing the hearing, but a hearing must follow promptly.
