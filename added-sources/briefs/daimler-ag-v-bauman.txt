Daimler AG v. Bauman
Daimler AG v. Bauman, 571 U.S. 117 (2014). United States Supreme Court, decided January 14, 2014.
Justice Ginsburg wrote the opinion of the Court, joined by seven other Justices. Justice Sotomayor concurred in the judgment. All nine agreed that the California litigation could not proceed against Daimler on the jurisdictional theory sustained below, but they disagreed about the reason.

== Parties ==
Twenty-two Argentinian residents sued DaimlerChrysler Aktiengesellschaft, later known as Daimler AG, a
German company headquartered in Stuttgart. They were the plaintiffs below and respondents in the Supreme
Court; Daimler was the defendant and petitioner. Plaintiffs sought to hold Daimler responsible for conduct of
Mercedes-Benz Argentina, an Argentinian subsidiary. The forum contacts relied upon belonged principally to a different indirect subsidiary, Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC, or MBUSA. MBUSA was organized in
Delaware and had its principal place of business in New Jersey. The operative complaint named Daimler as the sole corporate defendant.

== Material Facts ==
Plaintiffs alleged that Mercedes-Benz Argentina collaborated with Argentinian security forces during the
1976-1983 Dirty War to kidnap, detain, torture, or kill workers and their relatives. The relevant conduct, victims, and perpetrators had no California connection. Plaintiffs asserted claims under the Alien Tort
Statute, the Torture Victim Protection Act, and California and Argentinian law, including wrongful death and intentional infliction of emotional distress.
MBUSA imported Daimler vehicles from Germany and distributed them to independent American dealerships. It operated several California facilities, including a regional office, a vehicle preparation center, and a classic-car center. Its California sales represented about 2.4 percent of Daimler's worldwide sales. The distributor agreement characterized MBUSA as an independent contractor buying and selling vehicles for its own account and disclaimed authority to bind Daimler. Plaintiffs nevertheless contended that MBUSA acted as Daimler's agent because its distribution work was essential to Daimler's business. Their theory sought to make California a forum for any claim against Daimler, despite the absence of a relationship between
California and the alleged abuses in Argentina. 571 U.S. at 120-25.

== Procedural History ==
Plaintiffs filed in the United States District Court for the Northern District of California in 2004. Daimler moved to dismiss for lack of personal jurisdiction. Plaintiffs presented declarations and exhibits about
Daimler's own California connections and alternatively relied on MBUSA's activities. After permitting jurisdictional discovery on the agency theory, the district court dismissed. It found Daimler's own contacts insufficient for general jurisdiction and declined to attribute MBUSA's contacts to Daimler.
A Ninth Circuit panel initially affirmed in 2009, with Judge Reinhardt dissenting. On plaintiffs' petition for rehearing, the panel withdrew that opinion and issued a new decision written by Reinhardt, 644 F.3d 909 (2011). The new opinion treated MBUSA as an agent for jurisdictional purposes because the services it performed were sufficiently important that Daimler would undertake them itself if MBUSA did not. The court also found the exercise of jurisdiction reasonable. Rehearing and rehearing en banc were denied over the dissent of eight judges.
The Supreme Court granted certiorari. Plaintiffs did not pursue specific jurisdiction, did not challenge the insufficiency of Daimler's own contacts, and did not rely on an alter-ego theory. Daimler had not disputed below that MBUSA itself was subject to general jurisdiction in California. The Supreme Court therefore assumed, for purposes of decision, both that MBUSA was at home there and that its contacts could be attributed to Daimler. Those assumptions let the Court resolve the case without definitively deciding the proper agency test or whether MBUSA truly qualified for general jurisdiction under the standard being articulated. Federal Rule 4(k)(1)(A) and California's long-arm provision made the state-law reach, extending to constitutional limits, the relevant jurisdictional framework. 571 U.S. at 124-26, 134-36.

== Issue ==
May California exercise general jurisdiction over a German parent corporation for alleged misconduct in
Argentina merely because an American subsidiary conducts substantial business in California, even assuming that the subsidiary's contacts are attributable to the parent? Is substantial, continuous local business enough to make the parent answerable there on every claim?

== Holding ==
General jurisdiction ordinarily exists over a corporation in its state of incorporation and principal place of business. Another forum may qualify in an exceptional case when the corporation's operations there are so substantial and of such a nature that it is essentially at home. A substantial volume of business, considered in isolation, is insufficient. Even crediting all MBUSA California contacts to Daimler, Daimler was not at home in California. The relevant assessment considers the corporation's activities in their entirety, nationwide and worldwide. 571 U.S. at 137-39 & nn.19-20.

== Judgment ==
The Supreme Court reversed the Ninth Circuit. The asserted general-jurisdiction theory could not sustain the action against Daimler in California. The Court did not enter judgment on whether the alleged human-rights abuses occurred or whether Daimler bore substantive responsibility. Its comments on other obstacles to the federal claims did not change the jurisdictional ground of decision.

== Reasoning ==
The Ninth Circuit's agency formulation was too broad. Almost any subsidiary performs work important enough that a parent would need some way to perform it if the subsidiary disappeared. The formulation could attribute the activities of numerous separately incorporated entities to a parent and expose it to general jurisdiction wherever an affiliate operated. The Supreme Court expressed concern about that consequence but did not need to settle every possible attribution theory. Even the assumed attribution failed to show that
Daimler was at home in California.
The at-home standard gives all-purpose jurisdiction a more definite scope than a rule allowing suit wherever a corporation carries on a substantial, continuous course of business. Incorporation and principal place of business generally identify readily ascertainable places in which a company can anticipate suit on any claim.
They also leave room for an exceptional situation such as Perkins, where Ohio functioned as a corporation's temporary center of operations. The Court did not announce an absolute rule that only two states can ever exercise general jurisdiction.
California was a significant market, but the same could be said of many states and countries in which
Daimler sold vehicles. Evaluating the company's entire operation showed why these commercial activities did not make California its home. The analysis does not establish a numerical percentage threshold: the character of the operation, including whether it is a corporate center, matters along with its place in the company's overall business. Treating every substantial market as a home would undermine predictability and effectively restore jurisdiction over unrelated claims everywhere a large enterprise operates.
The international setting reinforced the problem. Foreign plaintiffs sought to litigate foreign conduct by foreign entities through a California connection supplied by automobile distribution. Broad jurisdiction of that kind could produce friction with other nations and expose American companies to similarly expansive rules abroad. The Court's constitutional holding nevertheless rested on Daimler's lack of an at-home relationship, rather than a categorical rule excluding foreign plaintiffs or international claims. 571 U.S. at
135-42.

== Separate Opinions ==
Justice Sotomayor would have assumed the contacts sufficient and rejected jurisdiction as unreasonable given the foreign plaintiffs, German defendant, Argentinian events, and availability of a more appropriate foreign forum. She objected that the Court decided a question not properly developed below and effectively penalized plaintiffs because Daimler had extensive activities elsewhere. In her view, the comparative approach allowed a larger corporation to avoid jurisdiction that similar local activity might support over a smaller company. She also questioned resolving such a fact-sensitive issue after Daimler had conceded the sufficiency of MBUSA's California contacts, limiting development of the record. Her concurrence supports the judgment, not the majority's at-home rationale. 571 U.S. at 142 (Sotomayor, J., concurring in the judgment).

== Notes ==
Two assumptions must remain distinct from holdings: the Court assumed MBUSA was subject to general jurisdiction and assumed its contacts were attributable to Daimler. It did not decide either proposition. The decision also concerns jurisdiction based on corporate contacts. Mallory later addresses consent under an explicit registration statute as a separate basis, leaving the at-home rule applicable when jurisdiction is asserted over a nonconsenting corporation because of its business operations.
