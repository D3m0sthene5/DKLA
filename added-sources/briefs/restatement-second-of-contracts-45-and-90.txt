Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§ 45 and 90

Source: added-sources/Contracts_Formation_Reliance_and_UCC_Briefs.pdf, pages 3–4

== Sources ==
Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§ 45 and 90. The Restatement describes common-law principles and is persuasive authority unless adopted by the relevant court. Section 45 addresses beginning the performance invited by an offer that permits acceptance only through performance. Section 90 addresses promises inducing foreseeable reliance. Drennan applied the First Restatement's versions; the Second Restatement's wording should be distinguished from the language quoted in that 1958 opinion. The assigned casebook reproduces and discusses these rules. Online reproductions appear in Revocation of Offers and Promissory
Estoppel.
Section 45
Section 45 applies when the offer invites acceptance through performance and does not invite a promise as acceptance. An option arises when the offeree tenders or begins the invited performance, or tenders its beginning. The resulting protection limits the offeror's ability to revoke while the offeree has the opportunity to complete the requested performance. The offeror's duty to perform the proposed exchange remains conditioned on completion or tender of the invited performance according to the offer's terms.
The distinction between creating the option and earning the promised return matters. If A offers B $100 for walking across a bridge and the offer genuinely calls for completed performance rather than a promise, B's beginning the crossing creates protection against revocation. It does not ordinarily entitle B to the full $100 merely for starting. Completion remains the condition of A's duty to pay. B does not, merely by obtaining the option, promise to finish the walk and become liable for stopping.
The offeree must begin the performance that the offer actually requests. Buying walking shoes or traveling toward the bridge ordinarily amounts to preparation. Preparation can be costly and foreseeable without constituting the invited act itself. It may support a reliance argument on appropriate facts, but § 45 does not turn every expense incurred after hearing an offer into the beginning of contractual performance. The offer's wording and context determine where preparation ends and the requested performance begins.
Section 45 also must be separated from an offer that allows acceptance by either a promise or performance.
Under the Restatement's related § 62, beginning the invited performance in that setting can constitute acceptance and carry a promise to complete. Under § 45, the performance-only offer creates an option at the beginning, while the offeror's ultimate exchange obligation remains conditional. The classification therefore affects both parties' duties, not just the moment when revocation becomes ineffective.
Section 90
Section 90(1) recognizes enforcement when a promise foreseeably induces action or forbearance by the promisee or a third person and enforcement is necessary to prevent injustice. Its second sentence permits relief to be tailored as justice requires. The rule supplies a possible basis for enforcement even when the promise lacks bargained-for consideration. A person must identify a promise, the reliance it should reasonably have induced, the actual resulting change of position, and the injustice that enforcement would prevent.
Foreseeability and actual reliance require separate proof. A promisor may reasonably expect a contractor to use a bid, but a contractor who never used that bid has not established the same causal reliance. Conversely, a person may actually change position for reasons that the promisor could not reasonably anticipate.
Reasonableness also matters: reliance on an obviously mistaken price, an expressly tentative estimate, or an assurance outside its stated limits is more difficult to justify. Expenditures made before the promise ordinarily cannot have been induced by it.
The Second Restatement removes the First Restatement's express requirement that the induced conduct be definite and substantial, but the seriousness and reasonableness of reliance remain relevant to injustice and relief. It also expressly includes reliance by a third person and recognizes remedial flexibility. A successful §
90 claim therefore does not mechanically produce either full expectation damages or reimbursement of every expenditure. The court connects the remedy to the promise, the reliance, and the injustice shown.
Section 90(2) separately addresses charitable subscriptions and marriage settlements: the Restatement's rule does not require proof of induced action or forbearance for those promises. That qualification should not be generalized to ordinary commercial promises. Nor does the Restatement's treatment establish that every jurisdiction has adopted the same rule for charitable pledges.
Relationship to Drennan
Drennan used § 90 to protect reliance on an offer before conventional acceptance. The subcontractor could foresee that the general contractor would commit to the owner using the quoted price. The court treated that reliance as supporting a subsidiary promise to keep the bid available long enough for acceptance after the award. Section 45 supplied an analogy: the law already protected an offeree who began an invited unilateral performance. But using Star's price in the general bid was not itself an acceptance of Star's offer or the bargained-for performance that Star requested.
The Second Restatement later addresses this situation more directly in § 87(2), which treats an offer inducing the requisite preacceptance reliance as binding as an option to the extent necessary to prevent injustice. This helps explain why reliance can preserve an opportunity to accept without automatically establishing that both parties already promised the entire exchange. In Drennan, the general contractor then promptly sought performance at the quoted price; the case did not authorize indefinite delay or renewed bargaining while retaining the original bid as a fallback.

== Application ==
Begin with the offer's invited method of acceptance. If a promise is invited and effectively given, ordinary formation may resolve the case. If only performance is invited, determine whether the offeree has begun that performance, bringing § 45 into play. If the conduct consists of preparation or another foreseeable change of position before acceptance, examine reliance under § 90 and, where relevant, § 87(2). Then identify the consequence actually justified: preservation of the offer, enforcement of a promise, or a remedy tailored to the reliance.
A signed merchant assurance under § 2-205 can be binding without any of this conduct. A qualifying New
York writing under General Obligations Law § 5-1109 likewise supplies its own basis for protection. These doctrines address overlapping practical problems through distinct elements; they should be applied independently before their consequences are compared.
