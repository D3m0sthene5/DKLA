Sun Printing & Publishing Ass'n v. Remington Paper & Power Co.
Sun Printing & Publishing Ass'n v. Remington Paper & Power Co., 235 N.Y. 338, 139 N.E. 470 (1923). New
York Court of Appeals, decided April 17, 1923. Judge Cardozo wrote the opinion for a five-judge majority that included Chief Judge Hiscock and Judges Pound, McLaughlin, and Andrews. Judge Crane dissented, joined by Judge Hogan. The case concerns whether a contract leaving price and the period for which each price would apply to future agreement was enforceable. Full judicial opinion.

== Parties ==
Sun Printing and Publishing Association, a newspaper publisher, was the buyer and the plaintiff. Remington
Paper and Power Company, a newsprint manufacturer, was the seller and the defendant. The buyer sought damages for the seller's refusal to deliver paper during 1920; the seller maintained that its obligation for those months had never been fixed.

== Material Facts ==
In September 1919 the seller agreed to sell and the buyer agreed to buy 1,000 tons of newsprint per month from September 1919 through December 1920, a total of 16,000 tons. The contract fixed the price for
September at $3.73¾ per hundred pounds and for October through December 1919 at $4.00 per hundred pounds. For the rest of the contract, it provided that "the price of the paper and length of terms for which such price shall apply shall be agreed upon by and between the parties hereto fifteen days prior to the expiration of each period for which the price and length of term thereof have been previously agreed upon," the price "in no event to be higher than the contract price for newsprint charged by the Canadian Export Paper Company to the large consumers." 235 N.Y. at 342-43.
The seller delivered and was paid for the paper due from September through December 1919. It then refused to agree on any price for 1920 and declined further performance, asserting that the contract was incomplete.
The buyer demanded delivery at the Canadian Export Paper Company's price, renewing the demand each month throughout 1920, and sued when the seller refused. Id. at 343.

== Procedural History ==
The case was decided on the pleadings. Special Term, the trial-level part of New York's Supreme Court, ruled for the seller. The Appellate Division reversed and certified a question to the Court of Appeals, which asked in substance whether the complaint stated a cause of action.

== Issue ==
When a sales contract leaves the price and the period for which each price will apply to future agreement, subject to a maximum price set by an external standard, is the seller bound to deliver at that maximum price if the parties fail to agree, or is the provision an unenforceable agreement to agree?

== Holding ==
An agreement that leaves essential terms to future agreement is not an enforceable contract as to those terms, and a court may not supply them by construction. Because the parties left open both the price and the length of the term during which each price would apply, and because agreement on time was as essential as agreement on price, the provision for 1920 was an agreement to agree. The seller was not bound to deliver at the Canadian price. The Court expressly did not decide whether the seller would have breached a duty by an arbitrary refusal to agree on a term after notice from the buyer, because the complaint did not rest on that theory. Id. at 344-47.

== Judgment ==
The Court of Appeals reversed the order of the Appellate Division, affirmed the judgment of Special Term for the seller, and answered the certified question in the negative, with costs.

== Reasoning ==
The buyer argued that because the price for 1920 could not exceed the Canadian Export Paper Company's price, the seller was bound to deliver at that price if the parties failed to agree on a lower one. Cardozo agreed that the Canadian price was a maximum but held that the argument overlooked the second open term. The parties were to agree not only on a price but on the length of the period for which that price would apply, and the price, once fixed, would remain fixed during that period even if the Canadian price changed. Agreement on a maximum payable this month, he wrote, is not the same as agreement that it shall continue to be payable next month. Id. at 344.
Without an agreed period, a court could not determine which Canadian price governed which deliveries. If the buyer could invoke the Canadian price month by month, it would hold not one option but a dozen, able to take advantage of whatever price prevailed each month, while the parties had contemplated stability and certainty over agreed periods. The complaint therefore left no escape from the conclusion that agreement on time was as essential to a completed contract as agreement on price. Id. at 344-46.
The buyer also argued that the seller had a duty, in default of agreement, to accept a term reasonable in view of the nature of the transaction and the practice of the business. Cardozo rejected that standard. The seller reserved the privilege of doing its business in its own way and did not undertake to conform to the practice and beliefs of others; to hold it to a standard of reasonableness would be to make the contract over, and courts are not at liberty to revise while professing to construe. Id. at 345-46.
Cardozo closed by limiting the holding. The question whether the seller would have failed in a duty by an arbitrary refusal to reach any agreement on time after notice from the buyer was not presented. No such notice appeared in the complaint, and the action was not based on a refusal to negotiate and attempt to reach agreement. Id. at 346-47.

== Separate Opinions ==
Judge Crane, joined by Judge Hogan, dissented. The parties were business people who used a formal contract, drafted by the seller, to bind themselves to buy and sell 16,000 tons of paper, and a court should presume they intended a binding agreement and give effect to it if possible. On December 15, 1919, a definite
Canadian Export price existed; if the buyer offered to pay that maximum, the seller was bound to deliver.
Crane offered several workable constructions: hold the seller to deliver the remaining paper at the Canadian price; apply the Canadian price in effect on the fifteenth of each month to that month's thousand tons; or fix a reasonable period in light of the circumstances and the practice of the paper trade. Letting the seller escape when any of these constructions would produce a practical and just result, he wrote, would give the sanction of law to a deliberate breach. Id. at 348-51 (Crane, J., dissenting).

== Notes ==
Sun Printing is a leading statement of the common-law rule that an agreement to agree on essential terms is unenforceable, and its facts show why the rule has costs: a seller facing a rising market could escape a sixteen-month supply contract by refusing to agree. The Uniform Commercial Code rejects the premise.
Under § 2-305(1)(b), when the parties intended to be bound but leave price to be agreed and fail to agree, the price is a reasonable price at the time for delivery, and § 2-204(3) saves contracts with open terms when the parties intended to contract and a court has a reasonably certain basis for a remedy. The case might come out differently today, although § 2-305(4) preserves the result if the parties intended not to be bound unless the price was agreed.
The reservation at the end of the opinion links the case to the precontractual-liability materials. Cardozo left open a claim that the seller arbitrarily refused to negotiate after notice, the kind of claim later recognized in
Tribune and Channel Home Centers when parties commit to negotiate. Crane's dissent anticipates Oglebay
Norton's approach of honoring the parties' intent to be bound by filling the gap with a reasonable term.
