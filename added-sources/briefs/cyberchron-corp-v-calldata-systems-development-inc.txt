Cyberchron Corp. v. Calldata Systems Development, Inc.
Cyberchron Corp. v. Calldata Systems Development, Inc., 47 F.3d 39 (2d Cir. 1995). United States Court of
Appeals for the Second Circuit, argued March 17, 1994, decided February 1, 1995. Judge Mahoney wrote the opinion for a panel that included Judge Walker and Judge Sprouse. The case concerns recovery by a supplier that produced goods at the buyer's urging while the parties failed to agree on essential terms. Full judicial opinion.
Source: added-sources/Contracts_Definiteness_and_Preliminary_Agreements_Briefs.pdf, pages 9–11

== Parties ==
Cyberchron Corporation, a manufacturer of rugged computer equipment, was the plaintiff. Calldata Systems
Development, Inc., a subsidiary of Grumman Data Systems, was the defendant; the opinion refers to the defendant's side of the negotiations as Grumman. Cyberchron sought payment for equipment it built; Calldata denied that any contract existed.

== Material Facts ==
Grumman held a Marine Corps contract for the Advanced Tactical Air Command Central, a combat command and control system. It negotiated with Cyberchron to supply rugged computer components: a video processor, a workstation, and a color monitor. The parties negotiated extensively but never agreed on the weight of the components or on penalties for exceeding weight limits. A May 15, 1990 purchase order specified a combined weight of 145 pounds and severe overweight penalties, terms Cyberchron never accepted. 47 F.3d at 40-42.
In July 1990 a Grumman representative told Cyberchron to proceed as if a new set of weights had been approved and said that the parties would work out the formal terms later; when Cyberchron asked for written confirmation, he declined but said Cyberchron should proceed. In August 1990, Grumman's director of material told Cyberchron that it had a responsibility and obligation to continue performing to maintain the schedule, and indicated that Cyberchron could assume it would be paid. Meanwhile Grumman was negotiating with another supplier, Codar Technology. On September 25, 1990, Calldata terminated the purchase order for default, no equipment having been delivered or paid for, and later bought heavier and inferior equipment from Codar. Between July 15 and September 25, 1990, Cyberchron spent $162,824.19 on labor and materials. Id. at 41-43.

== Procedural History ==
Cyberchron sued in the Eastern District of New York. After a bench trial, Judge Spatt held that no contract had been formed because the parties never agreed on two essential terms, the weights of the components and the weight penalties, and he dismissed Cyberchron's contract and quantum meruit claims and Calldata's counterclaim. He awarded Cyberchron $162,824.19 on a promissory estoppel theory, consisting of
$53,588.40 for materials and $109,235.79 for labor incurred between July 15 and September 25, 1990, but excluded overhead, lost profits, and shutdown costs. Both parties appealed. New York law governed. Id. at
43.

== Issue ==
Where negotiations never produced a contract because essential terms remained unresolved, could the supplier recover under promissory estoppel for costs incurred after the buyer insisted that it continue performing? If so, what measure of reliance damages applies, and does it include overhead and shutdown costs?

== Holding ==
No contract exists where the parties never reached a meeting of the minds on essential terms, and the district court's finding to that effect was not clearly erroneous. Under New York law, promissory estoppel requires a clear and unambiguous promise, reasonable and foreseeable reliance by the party to whom the promise is made, and injury sustained by reason of the reliance. Grumman's July and August 1990 assurances and demands were such a promise, Cyberchron's reliance was reasonable and foreseeable, and its injury was unconscionable, so injustice could be avoided only by enforcing the promise. Recovery is limited to reliance damages, which include reasonable overhead where the claimant has a demonstrable history of ongoing business operations, and shutdown costs incurred because of reliance on the promise. Id. at 43-46.

== Judgment ==
The Second Circuit affirmed the judgment insofar as it allowed recovery on promissory estoppel, but vacated the judgment and remanded for a redetermination of damages consistent with its opinion, leaving the district court free to take additional proof.

== Reasoning ==
The court first upheld the no-contract finding. The weights of the components and the penalties for exceeding them were essential terms, the parties never agreed on them, and the purchase order's termination-liability provision could not independently support recovery because it presupposed a valid purchase order. Id. at
43-44.
On promissory estoppel, the court applied the elements stated in Ripple's of Clearview, Inc. v. Le Havre
Associates, 88 A.D.2d 120 (2d Dep't 1982). The promise was clear and unambiguous: Grumman told
Cyberchron to proceed as if the new weights were approved, refused to put the assurance in writing but repeated that Cyberchron should proceed, and later insisted that Cyberchron was obligated to continue performing and could expect payment. Reliance was reasonable and foreseeable, because Grumman was demanding exactly the performance Cyberchron undertook. Some New York decisions required that the injury be unconscionable, and Calldata relied on Swerdloff v. Mobil Oil Corp. for a narrow view of the doctrine. The court concluded that Ripple's stated the New York rule and that, in any event, the injury here was unconscionable: Grumman pressed Cyberchron to produce at great expense while negotiating with a competitor, then abruptly terminated and bought heavier, inferior equipment elsewhere. Injustice could be avoided only by enforcing the promise. Id. at 44-45.
Recovery was limited to the period after the clear promise. Expenditures before July 15, 1990, when the parties were still negotiating without such assurances, were not recoverable. Id. at 45.
On damages, the court agreed that promissory estoppel recovery is limited as justice requires and here consisted of reliance damages rather than lost profits. It disagreed with the exclusion of overhead. Overhead is an actual cost of doing business, and nearly every business allocates it by standard accounting methods; the parties themselves had negotiated how overhead would be allocated. A claimant need not prove that an alternative project would have absorbed the overhead; a demonstrable history of ongoing operations suffices.
Shutdown costs incurred after termination were also recoverable to the extent they resulted from reliance on
Calldata's promises, but not to the extent they would have been incurred anyway. Id. at 45-46.

== Separate Opinions ==
The panel was unanimous. The reported decision contains no concurrence or dissent.

== Notes ==
Cyberchron shows how definiteness and reliance divide the work. The failure to agree on weights defeated the contract claim entirely, because a court could not identify what Cyberchron had promised to deliver. The narrower promise, to pay for work done at Grumman's insistence during negotiations, was clear enough to enforce under § 90. The measure of recovery reflects that difference: Cyberchron recovered what it spent, not the profit it expected from a contract that never existed.
The case is also a statement of New York promissory estoppel law. The court read Ripple's's three elements as the governing test and treated the unconscionable-injury requirement found in some New York decisions as satisfied here rather than rejecting it outright, so later courts have divided over whether New York requires unconscionable injury outside the statute-of-frauds context. The timing limit is important for application:
only reliance after the clear promise is protected, so costs a party incurs while it knows terms are unsettled and no assurance has been given remain its own risk.
