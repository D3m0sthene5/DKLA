Wyman v. Newhouse
Wyman v. Newhouse, 93 F.2d 313 (2d Cir. 1937), cert. denied, 303 U.S. 664 (1938). United States Court of
Appeals for the Second Circuit, decided December 6, 1937. Judge Manton wrote the opinion for a panel that included Judges Learned Hand and Swan. The case appears at casebook page 276 under the heading "Etiquette of Service" and concerns whether a judgment based on service obtained by luring the defendant into the forum through fraud may be enforced in another state. Full judicial opinion.
Source: added-sources/CivPro_Notice_Service_and_Hearing_Briefs.pdf, pages 17–19

== Parties ==
Wyman, the appellant, had obtained a default judgment against Newhouse in a Florida state court and sued in federal court in New York to enforce it. Newhouse, the appellee, was a New York resident who had never lived in Florida. Wyman sought enforcement of the Florida judgment; Newhouse contended that the Florida court never acquired jurisdiction over him because he was tricked into entering Florida to be served.

== Material Facts ==
Wyman and Newhouse were both married during their relationship, and Wyman's husband died before the suit. The two had known each other for some years and had engaged in what the court called meretricious relations, meaning a sexual relationship outside marriage. Newhouse's affidavits established the following facts, and Wyman did not deny making the representations they described. On October 25, 1935, while
Newhouse was in Salt Lake City, Utah, he received a telegram from Wyman: "Account illness home planning leaving. Please come on way back. Must see you." When he returned to New York, he received a letter stating that her mother was dying in Ireland, that she was leaving the United States for good to go to her mother, and that she could not go without seeing him once more and discussing her affairs. They then spoke by telephone, and she repeated the substance of the letter in a hysterical and distressed voice. He promised to come to Florida in a week or ten days. Before he left, she wrote him a letter expressing love and affection and her delight at his coming, and they exchanged telegrams arranging accommodations in Miami, one reaching him in Washington, D.C., en route. 93 F.2d at 314.
Newhouse arrived at the Miami airport at six in the morning. He saw Wyman standing with her sister about
75 feet away, and a deputy sheriff, after identifying him, served him with process in a suit for $500,000 for money loaned, money advanced, and seduction under promise of marriage. A photographer tried to take his picture. A stranger then offered to take him to the home of a lawyer who knew Wyman's attorney; the lawyer invited him to his office, where Newhouse found one of Wyman's lawyers waiting. Newhouse did not retain the Florida lawyer. He flew back to New York that evening, and his New York counsel advised him to ignore the Florida summons. He did so, and a default judgment was entered after a jury assessed damages. Id.

== Procedural History ==
Wyman sued Newhouse on the Florida judgment in federal district court in New York. Newhouse moved to dismiss the complaint before trial under Rule 113 of the New York Rules of Civil Practice, and the motion was decided on affidavits. The Federal Rules of Civil Procedure had not yet taken effect, and federal courts in actions at law followed state procedure under the Conformity Act. The district court granted the motion and dismissed the complaint. Wyman appealed, and after the Second Circuit affirmed, the Supreme Court denied certiorari. Id. at 313-14.

== Issue ==
Must a New York court give effect to a Florida default judgment when the Florida court obtained personal jurisdiction by serving a defendant whom the plaintiff fraudulently induced to enter Florida? May the defendant raise the fraud as a defense to enforcement in New York without having challenged service or defended on the merits in Florida?

== Holding ==
Fraud that induces a defendant to come within a court's territorial jurisdiction so that he may be served defeats the court's jurisdiction over his person. A fraud affecting jurisdiction is equivalent to a lack of jurisdiction, so a judgment procured in that way is void and is not binding when the plaintiff tries to enforce it in another state. The defendant need not attack the judgment in the rendering state or defend on the merits there; he may raise the fraud as an equitable defense when sued on the judgment, and the enforcing court may consider the jurisdictional question collaterally. Id. at 315.

== Judgment ==
The Second Circuit affirmed the dismissal of the complaint, so the Florida judgment could not be enforced against Newhouse in New York.

== Reasoning ==
The court first found the facts established. The affidavits and the reasonable inferences from them convincingly showed that Newhouse was induced to enter Florida by fraud: Wyman falsely represented her mother's illness, her intention to leave the United States, and her love and affection for him, when her sole purpose was to bring him within Florida's jurisdiction so that he could be served in an action for damages.
The timing of the service, the presence of a deputy sheriff and a photographer at the airport, and the waiting lawyers supported that conclusion, and Wyman did not deny making the representations. Id. at 314.
Service of process on a person physically present in a state ordinarily confers personal jurisdiction, the rule of
Pennoyer v. Neff. The court treated fraud as an exception to that rule. Newhouse's presence in Florida was the product of Wyman's deception, and a fraud affecting jurisdiction is the equivalent of a lack of jurisdiction. A judgment procured by that kind of fraud lacks jurisdiction and is null and void. Id. at 315.
Full faith and credit did not require a different result. The Constitution's Full Faith and Credit Clause, Article
IV, § 1, and its implementing statute (then 28 U.S.C. § 687, now § 1738) require each state to give sister-state judgments the effect they have where rendered, but a judgment rendered without jurisdiction over the defendant is not entitled to that effect. A judgment recovered in a sister state through the fraud of the party procuring the appearance of another is not binding on the latter when enforcement is sought in another state.
Id.
Newhouse was not required to go back to Florida. He had no obligation to proceed against the judgment there, and his equitable defense in answer to the suit on the judgment was sufficient. Nor did he have to make out a defense on the merits in Florida, because an error in entering judgment against a party over whom the court had no jurisdiction permits the jurisdictional question to be raised collaterally, that is, in a later proceeding brought to enforce the judgment. The complaint was therefore properly dismissed. Id.

== Separate Opinions ==
The panel was unanimous. The reported decision contains no concurrence or dissent.

== Notes ==
The casebook pairs Wyman with Tickle v. Barton, 95 S.E.2d 427 (W. Va. 1956). There the plaintiff's attorney, concealing his identity and purpose, telephoned a Virginia defendant and invited him to a high school football banquet in West Virginia, where a deputy sheriff served him. The Supreme Court of Appeals of West Virginia held that service procured by fraud, trickery, or artifice does not give a court jurisdiction over the person served, and it held sufficient the defendant's plea in abatement raising that defense. The cases differ in posture. Barton challenged service directly in the forum. Newhouse defaulted and waited to attack the judgment when Wyman tried to enforce it, a strategy that preserved his objection but would have left him with no defense on the merits if the New York court had rejected his fraud claim. The deception in Wyman also exploited a personal relationship and a fabricated family emergency, and its facts were effectively uncontested.
The casebook asks whether the result would change if Newhouse had been in Florida and the trickery had been used only to flush him out of hiding. Courts generally distinguish trickery that brings a defendant into the jurisdiction, which manufactures the basis for jurisdiction, from trickery that induces a defendant already present to come forward to be served, which only overcomes evasion; the second is usually tolerated.
Long-arm statutes and service outside the forum now make luring defendants less necessary. Burnham v.
Superior Court upheld jurisdiction based on in-state service on a defendant voluntarily present in the forum;
the Wyman and Tickle rule addresses presence that the plaintiff obtained by deception. The section of the casebook containing Wyman also includes related limits on service within the forum, such as immunity for nonresidents attending court proceedings and the United Nations Headquarters Agreement immunity considered in Kadic v. Karadzic, 70 F.3d 232 (2d Cir. 1995), where the court held that Karadzic was not immune when served in New York outside the Headquarters District.
