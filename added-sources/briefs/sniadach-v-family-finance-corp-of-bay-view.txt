Sniadach v. Family Finance Corp. of Bay View
Sniadach v. Family Finance Corp. of Bay View, 395 U.S. 337 (1969). Supreme Court of the United States, argued April 21, 1969, decided June 9, 1969. Justice Douglas wrote for the Court, Justice Harlan concurred, and Justice Black dissented. The casebook presents the case in Note 1 at page 279; the brief draws on the full opinion. Full judicial opinion.
Source: added-sources/CivPro_Notice_Service_and_Hearing_Briefs.pdf, pages 25–26

== Parties ==
Christine Sniadach, a wage earner, was the defendant in the garnishment action and the petitioner in the
Supreme Court. Family Finance Corporation of Bay View, the creditor, was the plaintiff and a respondent.
Her employer, Miller Harris Instrument Co., was named as garnishee, the third party holding money owed to the debtor. The creditor sought to freeze part of Sniadach's wages pending trial of its claim; Sniadach sought dismissal of the garnishment as a violation of due process.

== Material Facts ==
Family Finance began a garnishment action naming Sniadach as defendant and Miller Harris as garnishee.
The complaint alleged a claim of $420 on a promissory note. The employer answered that it held $63.18 in wages earned by Sniadach and not yet paid, and that it would pay half to her as a subsistence allowance and hold the other half, $31.59, subject to the court's order. 395 U.S. at 338.
Under Wisconsin's procedure, the clerk of court issued the garnishment summons at the request of the creditor's lawyer. Service on the garnishee froze the wages, and the creditor then had ten days to serve the summons and complaint on the debtor. The wages remained frozen until the main suit was tried, and the debtor could unfreeze them only by winning on the merits. The debtor had no opportunity to be heard before the wages were frozen. Id. at 338-39.

== Procedural History ==
Sniadach moved to dismiss the garnishment proceedings on the ground that the procedure violated the Due
Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment because it provided no notice or hearing before the wages were taken. The Wisconsin courts rejected the challenge, and the Wisconsin Supreme Court sustained the procedure, 37 Wis. 2d 163, 154 N.W.2d 259 (1967). The Supreme Court granted certiorari. Id. at 338.

== Issue ==
Does a state procedure that permits a creditor to freeze a debtor's wages through garnishment before trial, without notice to the debtor or an opportunity to be heard, violate the Due Process Clause?

== Holding ==
Yes. Wages are a specialized type of property presenting distinct problems in the economic system, and prejudgment garnishment of the Wisconsin type is a taking that may impose tremendous hardship on wage earners with families to support. Summary procedures may satisfy due process in extraordinary situations, but Wisconsin's statute was not narrowly drawn to meet any such situation, and none was present. Absent notice and a prior hearing, the procedure violated the fundamental principles of due process. Id. at 339-42.

== Judgment ==
The Supreme Court reversed the judgment of the Wisconsin Supreme Court.

== Reasoning ==
The Court acknowledged that Sniadach would eventually receive a hearing on the merits of the creditor's claim. In the meantime, however, she lost the use of wages she had already earned without any opportunity to be heard or to present any defense. Summary seizure had been upheld in extraordinary situations, such as the protection of the public from bank failures and misbranded drugs, and in cases involving attachment of a nonresident's property, but no situation requiring special protection of a state or creditor interest appeared here. Sniadach lived in the Wisconsin community, so personal jurisdiction over her was readily obtainable.
Id. at 339.
The Court then explained why wages deserve particular protection. Relying on congressional testimony gathered for the Consumer Credit Protection Act of 1968, it noted that until that recent act of Congress, which forbids discharging employees because their wages have been garnished for one debt, garnishment often cost the debtor a job, and it always drained family income. The creditor's leverage was enormous: the creditor could demand not only the original debt but also the collection fees its attorneys incurred in the garnishment. The Court also quoted congressional testimony that in a vast number of cases the debt was fraudulent, saddled on a poor and ignorant person trapped in an easy-credit nightmare. Prejudgment garnishment of this kind could, as a practical matter, drive a wage-earning family to the wall. Where the taking of property is so obvious, the Court concluded, little argument is needed to show that the procedure violates due process absent notice and a prior hearing. Id. at 340-42.

== Separate Opinions ==
Justice Harlan concurred. He responded to the argument that a temporary freeze does not deprive anyone of property: the property taken was the use of the garnished wages during the period between garnishment and the end of the main suit, and that deprivation was not de minimis. Apart from special situations, he wrote, due process is afforded only by the kinds of notice and hearing aimed at establishing the validity, or at least the probable validity, of the underlying claim before the debtor can be deprived of his property or its unrestricted use. Id. at 342-44 (Harlan, J., concurring).
Justice Black dissented. He accused the majority of invalidating a state law because it found the law unwise and of using a vague fundamental-fairness standard derived from natural law rather than the Constitution's text. In his view Wisconsin's procedure, which ultimately gave the debtor notice and a hearing, was consistent with the Court's earlier decisions upholding prejudgment attachment, such as McKay v. McInnes and Ownbey v. Morgan, and the policy choice belonged to the legislature. Id. at 344-51 (Black, J., dissenting).

== Notes ==
The opinion's emphasis on wages and family hardship left two questions open, as the casebook notes:
whether the rationale reached prejudgment seizures of other property when the hardship was less severe, and what extraordinary situations justify seizure without a prior hearing. Fuentes answered the first question broadly for household goods, and Doehr extended protection to real estate the owner continued to occupy.
Sniadach addressed prejudgment garnishment only. Garnishment after judgment, once the creditor's claim has been adjudicated, remains available, subject to the federal limits Title III of the Consumer Credit
Protection Act places on the portion of disposable earnings that may be garnished. The opinion's reference to readily available personal jurisdiction also connects to the casebook's question whether the Sniadach line should apply when attachment is used to obtain jurisdiction over a nonresident.
