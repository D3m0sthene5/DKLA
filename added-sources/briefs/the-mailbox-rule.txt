The mailbox rule
Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§ 40, 63-68; casebook printed pages 185-188, PDF pages 235-238.
Source: added-sources/Contracts_Offer_and_Acceptance_Briefs.pdf, pages 17–19

== Preamble ==
The mailbox rule ordinarily makes an acceptance effective when it is properly dispatched through a manner and medium invited by the offer. Under Restatement § 63(a), the acceptance can complete mutual assent when it leaves the offeree's possession, even if the offeror has not yet learned of it and even if it is later lost.
The rule is a default: the offer can specify another condition, such as actual receipt by a deadline. It also has exceptions for option contracts and for communications that do not satisfy the dispatch requirements. The essential question is when the relevant communication acquires legal effect. Restatement §§ 63, 66.
The rule gives an offeree a dependable point at which to commit. Once the offeree properly sends an acceptance of an ordinary offer, a subsequently received revocation ordinarily comes too late. The offeror bears the transmission risk associated with that invited method, subject to the offer's wording and the rule's qualifications. The casebook traces the doctrine to Adams v. Lindsell, 1 B. & Ald. 681, 106 Eng. Rep. 250 (K.B. 1818), involving a mailed offer for wool and a posted acceptance. It also explains that treating the postal service as the offeror's agent is an imperfect rationale; the more useful explanation concerns the offeree's ability to act on a reliable legal commitment. Casebook at 185-87.
The effective-time rules are asymmetric. An offer must ordinarily reach the offeree before it creates a power of acceptance. Revocation ordinarily takes effect upon receipt by the offeree. Rejection and counteroffer sent from a distance ordinarily terminate the power when received by the offeror, subject to § 40's rule for a later acceptance. A qualifying acceptance alone ordinarily receives the benefit of effectiveness on dispatch.
The fact that every communication travels by mail does not make their legal timing identical. Restatement
§§ 40, 42, 63.
Suppose a seller mails a revocation on Monday. The buyer has not received it when the buyer properly mails an invited acceptance on Tuesday, and the revocation arrives Wednesday. Under the ordinary mailbox rule, the contract forms Tuesday. The seller's Monday dispatch does not establish an effective revocation before acceptance. If the buyer receives the revocation on Monday and then mails an acceptance on Tuesday, the ordinary offer has already terminated. The sequence that matters compares legally effective events, which can differ from the chronological order in which the parties wrote or mailed their messages.
Proper dispatch requires an appropriate destination and the ordinary precautions for safe transmission, including correct addressing and sufficient postage when applicable. The manner and medium must also be invited by the offer, expressly or reasonably in context. An offer inviting an urgent response may not permit a slower method that defeats its purpose. Section 67 supplies a qualification for a seasonably but improperly dispatched acceptance: it is treated as operative on dispatch if it actually arrives within the time in which a properly dispatched acceptance normally would have arrived. An improperly sent message cannot receive the ordinary dispatch protection merely because the offeree intended to accept. Restatement §§ 63(a), 65-67.
The offeror can make receipt controlling by requiring that acceptance arrive by a specified time. If an offer requires a signed acceptance in the offeror's hands by 5 p.m. Friday, posting it Friday afternoon ordinarily does not satisfy that requirement. By contrast, a deadline stated without specifying receipt must be interpreted with the offer's other language, the authorized medium, and the default timing rules. A clear receipt clause shifts the risk of delay to the accepting party and gives both parties an observable deadline.
The offeree must satisfy that condition while the offer remains available. Restatement §§ 60, 63.
Acceptance under an option contract is effective upon receipt by the offeror under § 63(b), unless the offer provides otherwise. The holder already has a protected period to decide and does not need the ordinary mailbox rule's protection against intervening revocation. An option expiring Friday therefore ordinarily requires receipt of the exercise by Friday, even if a letter was properly mailed earlier. The option's terms may alter that default. The casebook's example, Pennsylvania Academy of the Fine Arts v. Grant, 590 A.2d 9 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1991), enforced an exercise mailed within the required period under a lease provision authorizing notices by delivery or specified forms of mail. That example makes the drafting relevant; it should not erase the Restatement's receipt default for options. Casebook at 187.
If the offeree dispatches an effective acceptance first and later sends a rejection, the contract ordinarily already exists. A faster rejection that arrives before the acceptance generally cannot unilaterally undo the contract. The offeror may choose to release the offeree or agree to rescission, but that requires a further basis. There is also a reliance qualification: if the offeror receives the overtaking rejection, reasonably relies on it, and materially changes position before learning of the earlier acceptance, the offeree may be estopped from enforcing the contract. Estoppel in this setting prevents the offeree from using the earlier acceptance to defeat reasonable reliance on the offeree's later misleading communication. Restatement § 63 cmt. c;
casebook at 186.
If the offeree sends a rejection or counteroffer first and then sends an acceptance, § 40 changes the treatment of the later message. The later acceptance is effective only if it reaches the offeror before the earlier rejection or counteroffer. If the rejection arrives first, the later message can operate only as a counteroffer requiring the original offeror's assent. For example, a buyer who mails a rejection Monday and an acceptance Tuesday does not obtain the ordinary dispatch rule for Tuesday's acceptance. The order of receipt controls under this special rule. This is why the two sequences, acceptance followed by rejection and rejection followed by acceptance, must be analyzed separately.
For receipt, § 68 looks to possession or authorized delivery rather than requiring proof that the recipient actually read the message. A written communication is received when it comes into the possession of the addressee, someone authorized to receive it, or an authorized place for such communications. The same principle explains why ignoring a properly delivered revocation cannot preserve an ordinary offer indefinitely. The meaning of authorized delivery depends on the setting, including the recipient's arrangements for receiving business communications. Restatement § 68.
The rule concerns formation and does not generally turn mailing into timely performance of an existing obligation. If an established contract requires payment by a specified date, whether mailing a check by that date constitutes payment depends on the contract and applicable payment rules. The mailbox rule for accepting offers does not answer that question by itself. The casebook uses a tailor's unpaid bill to expose the error of extending the dispatch rule to every communication or payment sent through the post. Casebook at 187.
Near-instantaneous communications require care. Section 64 treats acceptance by telephone or another medium of substantially instantaneous two-way communication under principles applicable to parties communicating in each other's presence. Email is not governed by a universal rule that every click on send creates a contract; its treatment depends on applicable law, the offer's terms, and the communication's characteristics. An email requiring a signed document to be received by a stated hour can make actual receipt decisive even when ordinary mailed acceptance would receive dispatch protection. The assigned materials raise electronic communication as a question about the continued scope of the postal rule.
Restatement § 64; casebook at 185, 187-88.
The casebook also contrasts the United Nations Convention on Contracts for the International Sale of
Goods. Under CISG Articles 18(2) and 23, acceptance ordinarily forms the contract when the indication of assent reaches the offeror. Article 16(1), however, ordinarily requires a revocation to reach the offeree before the offeree dispatches acceptance. This combination can protect an acceptance in transit against revocation even though formation awaits receipt. It demonstrates that the rules governing formation and the ability to revoke can be separated. Application of the CISG depends on the transaction and the parties' choice of governing rules; the ordinary domestic mailbox rule should not simply be assumed to control an international sale. Casebook at 187.
A complete answer identifies the communication, the authorized method, whether the offer requires receipt, whether an option exists, whether dispatch was proper, and the order of legally effective events. The connection to White is direct: White's example of a posted acceptance shows that an outward manifestation can become binding before actual knowledge. The connection to International Filter is the parties' ability to specify the act that completes acceptance. The Restatement's quoted acceptance and dispatch provisions are reproduced in Contracts Doctrine, Theory and Practice, Acceptance.
