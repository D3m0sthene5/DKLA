Federal Rule 4: Service of Process

Source: added-sources/CivPro_Notice_Service_and_Hearing_Briefs.pdf, pages 9–13

== Sources ==
Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 4 (text as last amended in 2016) and casebook pp. 251-272. Service refers to the act of delivering notice of a suit; process refers to the documents delivered, chiefly the summons, the court's formal notice directing the defendant to respond. Rule 4 translates Mullane's constitutional standard into specific authorized methods, so a method Rule 4 does not authorize can be defective even if it would satisfy due process. Courts often forgive technical defects when the defendant received actual notice; the casebook asks whether that tolerance extends to methods used without required court authorization, citing De
Gazelle Group, Inc. v. Tamaz Trading Establishment, 817 F.3d 747 (11th Cir. 2016). Rule 12(b)(4) and (5) supply the motions for challenging defective process and service. Rule 4.
Summons, Issuance, and Who Serves
Under Rule 3, a federal action is commenced by filing a complaint. Rule 4(a) requires the summons to name the court and the parties, be directed to the defendant, state the name and address of the plaintiff's attorney (or of an unrepresented plaintiff), state the time within which the defendant must appear and defend, warn that failure to do so will result in a default judgment, and bear the clerk's signature and the court's seal. Under
Rule 4(b), the plaintiff presents the summons to the clerk after filing the complaint, and the clerk issues a summons for each defendant. Rule 4(c) requires the summons to be served with a copy of the complaint and makes the plaintiff responsible for service within the Rule 4(m) period. Any person who is at least 18 years old and not a party may serve. The court may order service by a United States marshal on the plaintiff's request and must do so for a plaintiff proceeding in forma pauperis (permitted to litigate without paying court fees) or as a seaman under the statute that allows seamen to sue without prepaying fees.
Waiver of Service: Rule 4(d)
Rule 4(d) imposes on individuals, corporations, and associations subject to service under Rules 4(e), (f), or (h) a duty to avoid unnecessary expenses of serving the summons. The rule has a history the casebook traces.
In 1982 Congress rejected a proposed amendment that would have authorized service by registered or certified mail, amid concerns about illegible return receipts, unclaimed mail, and default judgments against people who never received the papers. In 1983 Congress adopted a system modeled on California's, under which the summons and complaint could be mailed by first-class mail with an acknowledgment form, and a defendant who failed without good cause to return it had to pay the cost of personal service. Because that system depended on the defendant's cooperation, the 1993 amendments replaced it with the current request to waive service.
The plaintiff's request must be in writing and addressed to the individual defendant or to an officer or authorized agent of an entity; name the court; include a copy of the complaint, two copies of the waiver form, and a prepaid means of returning one; inform the defendant of the consequences of waiving and not waiving;
state the date the request is sent; give the defendant a reasonable time of at least 30 days to return the waiver (60 days if the request is sent outside any judicial district of the United States); and be sent by first-class mail or other reliable means. The 1993 Advisory Committee Note to the 1993 amendment approved the use of electronic means. A defendant located in the United States who fails without good cause to sign and return a requested waiver must pay the expenses later incurred in making service and the reasonable expenses, including attorney's fees, of any motion required to collect them.
The incentive to waive is time. A defendant who timely returns a waiver need not answer until 60 days after the request was sent (90 days if sent outside the United States), compared with 21 days after formal service
under Rule 12(a). When the plaintiff files the waiver, no proof of service is required, and the rules apply as if the summons and complaint had been served when the waiver was filed. Waiving service does not waive objections to personal jurisdiction or venue. Because service is treated as occurring only when the waiver is filed, a plaintiff facing a statute of limitations that runs until service may be safer serving formally than waiting for a waiver; the casebook poses that question in its notes.
Individuals in the United States: Rule 4(e)
Rule 4(e)(1) permits service by any method allowed by the law of the state where the district court sits or where service is made. Rule 4(e)(2) adds three federal methods: (A) delivering a copy of the summons and complaint to the individual personally; (B) leaving a copy at the individual's dwelling or usual place of abode with someone of suitable age and discretion who resides there; and (C) delivering a copy to an agent authorized by appointment or by law to receive service.
Personal delivery rarely raises doctrinal questions, but evasive defendants test what delivery requires. The casebook's excerpt from McKelway's 1935 New Yorker profile of process server Harry Grossman illustrates the legal phrase "place and leave with": courts held that throwing papers at James Gordon Bennett and striking him in the chest was not proper service, but that laying papers on the shoulder of a defendant hiding under his wife's petticoats was sufficient. Courts generally treat a server who is in the defendant's presence, makes clear what the papers are, and leaves them near a defendant who refuses to take them as having accomplished personal delivery.
Abode service turns on facts. In National Development Co. v. Triad Holding Corp., 930 F.2d 253 (2d Cir.
1991), a Saudi citizen maintained twelve homes around the world. Papers were left with his housekeeper at his New York apartment, valued at $20 to $25 million, on a day he was actually living there. The Second
Circuit held that a person can have more than one dwelling house or usual place of abode and that service at the one where the defendant was living that day was, if not the most likely method, reasonably calculated to give actual notice. The casebook asks whether the result should change if he had been at another residence that day.
Service on an agent authorized by appointment normally requires an actual appointment for the specific purpose of receiving process. A person's acceptance of process, or claim of authority, does not bind the defendant without evidence that the defendant intended to confer that authority. National Equipment Rental,
Ltd. v. Szukhent, briefed separately, upheld an appointment made in a printed lease.
Corporations and Associations: Rule 4(h)
Rule 4(h)(1) permits service on a corporation, partnership, or unincorporated association in the United States either under state law, through Rule 4(e)(1), or by delivering the summons and complaint to an officer, a managing or general agent, or any other agent authorized by appointment or by law, with an additional mailing to the defendant if a statute appointing the agent so requires. Rule 4(h)(2) governs service abroad by reference to Rule 4(f), excluding personal delivery.
Courts read the categories functionally. In Insurance Co. of North America v. S/S "Hellenic Challenger", 88
F.R.D. 545 (S.D.N.Y. 1980), a marshal left papers in an admiralty cargo suit with a claims adjuster while the claims manager, one of the few employees expressly authorized to accept service, was out sick. The adjuster misplaced the papers, and the defendant learned of the suit only when its bank account was attached after a default judgment. The court upheld service because the adjuster was so integrated into the organization that he would know what to do with the papers: he had accepted service at least twice before and worked next to
the claims manager, separated from him only by the manager's glass-walled office. His loss of the papers was a mistake in the ordinary course of the defendant's business and did not justify relief. By contrast, the casebook cites American Institute of Certified Public Accountants v. Affinity Card, Inc., 8 F. Supp. 2d 372 (S.D.N.Y. 1998), which held service on the vice president of a different corporation sharing the defendant's office insufficient even though both corporations were owned by the same individual, who had received actual notice.
In Fashion Page, Ltd. v. Zurich Insurance Co., 50 N.Y.2d 265 (1980), a receptionist sent a process server to a vice president's executive secretary, who said "I can take it." Rather than decide whether she was a managing agent, the New York Court of Appeals held her an agent authorized by appointment, because she had regularly accepted summonses for at least five years. A corporation may assign the task of accepting process, but it cannot escape the consequences of the procedures it establishes, and a process server may rely on corporate employees to identify the proper person.
Service Abroad: Rule 4(f)
Rule 4(f) governs service on an individual in a foreign country, and Rule 4(h)(2) applies most of it to entities.
Rule 4(f)(1) authorizes any internationally agreed means reasonably calculated to give notice, principally the
Hague Service Convention, which requires each contracting state to designate a Central Authority to receive and execute requests for service from other contracting states; the casebook counted 79 contracting states as of June 2021. Where no agreement applies or the agreement allows other means, Rule 4(f)(2) permits methods reasonably calculated to give notice: the foreign country's own methods, a letter rogatory (a formal request from one court to a foreign court for assistance), or, unless foreign law prohibits it, personal delivery or mail requiring a signed receipt sent by the clerk. Rule 4(f)(3) permits other means not prohibited by international agreement, as the court orders.
Several decisions define the Convention's reach. Water Splash, Inc. v. Menon, 137 S. Ct. 1504 (2017), held that the Convention permits service by mail if the receiving state has not objected and mail service is authorized under otherwise-applicable law. Volkswagenwerk Aktiengesellschaft v. Schlunk, 486 U.S. 694 (1988), held that the Convention applies only when documents must be transmitted abroad, so service on a
German corporation's domestic subsidiary as its involuntary agent under state law did not implicate it. The
Convention does not govern service in noncontracting states, and a voluntary waiver of service may not implicate it at all. In Rio Properties, Inc. v. Rio International Interlink, 284 F.3d 1007 (9th Cir. 2002), the court upheld court-ordered email service on a foreign internet business under Rule 4(f)(3), describing that provision as one means among several rather than an extraordinary last resort; later courts have divided over email and social-media service depending on the evidence that the account is likely to reach the defendant.
For foreign states, Republic of Sudan v. Harrison, 139 S. Ct. 1048 (2019), held that the Foreign Sovereign
Immunities Act's mail provision requires the packet to be addressed and dispatched to the foreign minister at the ministry in the foreign state, not to its embassy in the United States.
Territorial Reach: Rule 4(k)
Serving a summons or filing a waiver establishes personal jurisdiction over a defendant who is subject to the jurisdiction of a court of general jurisdiction in the state where the district court sits; over a party joined under
Rule 14 or 19 and served within a judicial district of the United States not more than 100 miles from where the summons issued; and when a federal statute authorizes it. Rule 4(k)(2) adds jurisdiction over a defendant on a claim arising under federal law if the defendant is not subject to jurisdiction in any state's courts of general jurisdiction and exercising jurisdiction is consistent with the Constitution and laws of the United
States. Rule 4(k) ties the service rules to the personal-jurisdiction analysis studied earlier: proper service is necessary, but a federal court ordinarily reaches only as far as the forum state's courts could.
Proof of Service and Sewer Service: Rule 4(l)
Unless service is waived, the server must file proof of service, by affidavit unless the server is a marshal or deputy marshal; failure to prove service does not affect the validity of service. This filing is traditionally called the return. In Miedreich v. Lauenstein, 232 U.S. 236 (1914), the Court upheld a mortgage-foreclosure judgment against a state resident who alleged that the sheriff had falsely returned service, treating the return as conclusive because state law allowed recovery against the sheriff on his bond. Today most jurisdictions treat the return as strong evidence of service rather than conclusive proof; a defendant's own denial generally will not overcome a private process server's affidavit, and the required standard of proof varies.
Sewer service is the practice of discarding the papers and filing a false affidavit of service, so that the defendant never learns of the suit and a default judgment follows. In United States v. Brand Jewelers, Inc.,
318 F. Supp. 1293 (S.D.N.Y. 1970), the court held that the United States had standing to enjoin a seller of consumer goods on easy credit from systematically obtaining default judgments through sewer service; the
1972 consent decree vacated defaults obtained from 1969 to 1971 and made the seller's attorney responsible for fair service. New York responded with a series of reforms: its 1970 amendment to CPLR 308(2), which allowed service by leaving papers with a person of suitable age and discretion at the defendant's workplace or home and mailing a second copy, without first showing diligent efforts at personal delivery, a requirement viewed as the main cause of sewer service; requirements in 1973 for detailed statements about how process was served; amendments after a 1986 investigation found that about 48,000 of 140,000 annual default judgments in New York City were tainted; the 2009 Pfau v. Forster & Garbus proceeding against debt-collection firms; and a New York City requirement that licensed process servers keep GPS records of service attempts.
Time Limit and Statutes of Limitations: Rule 4(m)
If a defendant is not served within 90 days after the complaint is filed, the court, on motion or on its own after notice to the plaintiff, must dismiss the action without prejudice as to that defendant or order service within a specified time. If the plaintiff shows good cause for the failure, the court must extend the time for an appropriate period. The Supreme Court has observed that the rule permits an extension even without good cause, Henderson v. United States, 517 U.S. 654 (1996), a point that matters most when the limitations period has run and a dismissal without prejudice would operate as a dismissal with prejudice. The 90-day limit does not apply to service in a foreign country under Rule 4(f), 4(h)(2), or 4(j)(1); the casebook notes that circuits divide over what time limit, if any, then applies.
Timing of service interacts with statutes of limitations. For a claim based on federal law, the action is commenced for limitations purposes when the complaint is filed under Rule 3, West v. Conrail, 481 U.S. 35 (1987). For a state-law claim in federal court, state law usually determines when the action is commenced,
Walker v. Armco Steel Corp., 446 U.S. 740 (1980), and in states that require service to commence an action, a defect in service can be fatal because the limitations period may expire before it is corrected. A challenge to untimely service is generally raised under Rule 12(b)(5).

== Application ==
Confirm that the summons meets Rule 4(a) and was issued by the clerk. Decide whether to request a waiver, weighing the cost savings against the risk that the limitations period will run before the waiver is filed.
Identify the defendant's category (individual in the United States, individual abroad, entity, government) and the methods available for that category, including methods borrowed from state law. Check who served, where, and on whom: for abode service, whether the place was the defendant's dwelling on that day and the recipient resided there; for agents, whether an actual appointment exists; for entities, whether the recipient's role supports service. Confirm service within 90 days, file proof of service, and consider whether Rule 4(k) and the Constitution support personal jurisdiction. A defendant raises defects in the summons under Rule
12(b)(4), defects in delivery under Rule 12(b)(5), and must do so in its first Rule 12 motion or responsive pleading.
