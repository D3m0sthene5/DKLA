Garland v. Cargill
602 U.S. 406, 144 S. Ct. 1613 (2024). U.S. Supreme Court. Thomas, J. Argued February 28, 2024; decided June 14, 2024. Certiorari to 57 F.4th 447.
Source: added-sources/Legislation_Regulatory_State_Class3_Case_Briefs.pdf, pages 5–6

== Parties ==
Petitioners: Attorney General Merrick Garland and federal officials responsible for ATF, defending the agency's 2018 bump-stock rule. Respondent: Michael Cargill, who owned two bump stocks, surrendered them under protest, and challenged ATF's statutory authority under the Administrative Procedure Act.

== Procedural History ==
For years and across several administrations, ATF issued more than ten decisions concluding that a semiautomatic rifle equipped with a bump stock fell outside the federal definition of 'machinegun.' After the October 2017 Las Vegas mass shooting, ATF proposed and then adopted a 2018 rule classifying bump-stock devices as machineguns. The rule repudiated the agency's prior guidance and required owners to destroy or surrender the devices within ninety days to avoid criminal prosecution.
Cargill surrendered two bump stocks and sued under the APA, alleging that ATF exceeded its statutory authority because the devices fall outside 26 U.S.C. Section 5845(b). After a bench trial, the Western District of Texas entered judgment for ATF. 502 F. Supp. 3d 1163. A Fifth Circuit panel affirmed. 20 F.4th 1004. On rehearing en banc, the Fifth
Circuit reversed. A majority found at least ambiguity and applied the rule of lenity in Cargill's favor; an eight-judge plurality read the statute as unambiguously excluding bump-stock-equipped rifles. 57 F.4th 447. The Supreme Court granted review to resolve a circuit split and affirmed.

== Material Facts ==
The National Firearms Act defines a machinegun as a weapon that 'shoots, is designed to shoot, or can be readily restored to shoot, automatically more than one shot, without manual reloading, by a single function of the trigger.' 26
U.S.C. Section 5845(b). The definition also covers a part designed and intended to convert a weapon into a machinegun. Federal law sharply restricts civilian possession and transfer of machineguns.
A conventional machinegun can fire multiple rounds, often continuously, from one engagement of the trigger. A semiautomatic rifle fires one round from each engagement. After every shot, its trigger must release, move forward, reset the hammer and disconnector mechanism, and then be engaged again to fire the next round.
Bump firing uses recoil to speed that repeated trigger cycle. The shooter holds the trigger finger stationary while the firing hand permits the rifle to recoil backward, releasing and resetting the trigger. The other hand maintains calibrated forward pressure, moving the rifle and trigger back into the stationary finger to engage the trigger again. This repeats rapidly while the shooter preserves the balance between recoil and forward pressure. A bump stock replaces the rifle's ordinary rear stock with a casing that permits sliding motion and provides a ledge for the trigger finger. It makes the technique easier while preserving a release, reset, and reengagement for every shot.
In October 2017, a gunman using bump-stock-equipped rifles killed fifty-eight people and wounded more than five hundred at an outdoor Las Vegas music festival. Members of Congress introduced legislation directed at bump stocks and devices that accelerate semiautomatic fire; the proposals did not become law. ATF then reversed its interpretation through the 2018 rule and defined 'automatically' and 'single function of the trigger' to include bump-stock operation.

== Issue ==
Does a bump stock convert a semiautomatic rifle into a 'machinegun' under Section 5845(b) by enabling the weapon to shoot automatically more than one shot, without manual reloading, by a single function of the trigger?

== Holding ==
Substantive holding: A bump-stock-equipped semiautomatic rifle falls outside Section 5845(b). Each round follows a distinct function of the trigger, and continued bump fire depends on ongoing manual forward pressure beyond the trigger's function. ATF therefore exceeded its statutory authority by classifying bump stocks as machineguns.
Procedural disposition: The Supreme Court affirmed the en banc Fifth Circuit's judgment for Cargill.

== Reasoning ==
Source of law and competing positions: The Court independently interpreted Section 5845(b) in an APA challenge to agency authority. ATF treated a shooter's initial pull as the relevant single function and viewed the ensuing fire-recoil-bump cycle as automatic fire because the shooter's finger can remain stationary. Cargill treated each release, reset, and reengagement of the trigger as a separate function and the shooter's forward pressure as additional manual input.
'Single function of the trigger': Contemporary dictionaries defined function as the mode of action by which an object fulfills its purpose and trigger as the lever that activates a mechanism. The Court therefore identified the physical movement by which the trigger activates the firing mechanism. In a semiautomatic rifle, one engagement releases the hammer and fires one cartridge; the disconnector then captures the hammer until the trigger moves forward and resets.
A later shot requires a new engagement after that reset.
The bump stock accelerates those distinct cycles and leaves their number unchanged. For each additional round, recoil moves the rifle away from the stationary finger, the trigger releases and resets, and forward movement bumps the trigger into the finger again. The statute counts trigger functions by the firing mechanism's repeated engagements, regardless of whether the shooter flexes a finger or moves the rifle against a stationary finger. Rapid succession therefore remains a succession of separate functions.
ATF's own definition sharpened the problem. The rule defined a trigger function as a pull or an analogous motion, and
ATF conceded that sliding a rifle forward to bump the trigger is an analogous motion. Each forward bump consequently qualifies as its own function. ATF's initial-versus-continuing distinction depended on the beginning of a firing sequence, while the statute asks how many shots each trigger function produces. ATF's theory would also classify manual bump firing without a bump stock as a single-function sequence, even though the agency agreed that an unmodified semiautomatic rifle fires one shot per trigger function.
'Automatically': The syntax requires the single trigger function itself to cause more than one shot automatically.
Continued bump fire also requires the shooter's nontrigger hand to maintain the precise forward pressure that returns the rifle to the stationary finger. Excess pressure prevents adequate recoil and reset; insufficient pressure prevents the next engagement. The Court treated that continuing input as an action beyond the trigger's function.
ATF's treatment of the Ithaca Model 37 shotgun supported the same line. Holding the Model 37's trigger while manually pumping the action produces multiple shots, yet ATF classified the weapon outside the machinegun definition because the pump supplies additional manual input. Maintaining calibrated forward pressure for bump fire likewise supplies an additional action. A weapon may automate reloading or another operation and still fail the complete statutory phrase requiring more than one shot automatically from a single trigger function.
Presumption against ineffectiveness: ATF argued that exclusion would let accessories evade Congress's effort to restrict rapid-fire weapons. The majority held that its reading leaves the statute fully operative against traditional machineguns. Congress chose trigger function and automatic operation as the legal criteria; it could have chosen rate of fire or devices that accelerate firing. Statutory purpose could guide application of the enacted line, while the enacted words fixed where that line fell. ATF's own repeated pre-2018 conclusions also showed that this construction preserved a workable machinegun prohibition.
Justice Alito's concurrence: Alito found little practical difference between an M16 and a bump-stock-equipped semiautomatic rifle, yet agreed that the statutory text produced different treatment. He identified legislation amending the definition as the available remedy.
Justice Sotomayor's dissent: Joined by Justices Kagan and Jackson, Sotomayor read 'single function of the trigger' from the shooter's perspective as the single human action initiating a firing sequence. Dictionaries, 1934 hearing testimony from the NRA president, congressional reports equating the phrase with a single pull, and Staples v. United
States supported that usage. A bump-stock shooter initiates continuous fire once and then maintains forward pressure, just as an M16 shooter maintains backward pressure on the trigger. The dissent treated the bump stock's recoil-driven movement as external automation of repeated trigger manipulation. It also invoked the presumption against ineffectiveness because bump stocks permit roughly 400 to 800 rounds per minute and reproduce the rapid-fire capacity
Congress sought to control.
Narrow holding and significance: The holding concerns the statutory machinegun definition and ATF's authority to implement it. The case leaves Congress free to regulate bump stocks through amended legislation and leaves state restrictions untouched. The Court resolved the case through statutory text and firearm mechanics, without deciding a
Second Amendment question.
1L explanation: Read the definition as a sequence of required elements. The weapon must produce more than one shot; that result must occur automatically; and one trigger function must cause it. The majority located a new trigger function before every bump-fired round and identified calibrated forward pressure as an additional continuing action.
Once either proposition holds, the device falls outside the enacted definition even though its rate of fire can resemble a machinegun.
