Bayway Refining Co. v. Oxygenated Marketing & Trading A.G.
Bayway Refining Co. v. Oxygenated Marketing & Trading A.G., 215 F.3d 219 (2d Cir. 2000). United States
Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit, argued January 11, 2000, decided June 8, 2000. Judge Jacobs wrote the opinion for a panel that also included Chief Judge Winter and Judge Calabresi. The appeal concerns which party must prove that an additional term in a merchant's confirmation materially alters the contract under UCC § 2-207(2)(b), and what the "surprise or hardship" standard for material alteration requires. Full judicial opinion.

== Parties ==
Bayway Refining Company, a refiner that sold a gasoline blending component to the defendant, was the plaintiff and appellee. Its corporate parent, Tosco Corporation, joined as a plaintiff and counter-defendant on a related claim about a separate transaction; the district court ruled against Tosco on that claim and Tosco did not appeal, so it played no role in the Second Circuit's analysis. Oxygenated Marketing and Trading A.G.
(OMT), the buyer, was the defendant, counterclaimant, and appellant. Bayway sought reimbursement of
$464,035.12 in federal excise tax that it paid on the sale, relying on a tax-allocation clause in its own confirmation. OMT maintained that the clause never became part of the contract because it materially altered the bargain. Both parties were merchants dealing in petroleum products.

== Material Facts ==
In February 1998 Bayway and OMT agreed by telephone that Bayway would sell OMT 60,000 barrels of methyl tertiary butyl ether (MTBE), a gasoline blendstock. On February 12, 1998, OMT faxed Bayway a confirmation reciting the deal and stating that the document constituted the entire contract. On February 13,
1998, Bayway faxed its own confirmation. Bayway's document stated that, where not in conflict with its specific terms, "Bayway Refining Company's General Terms and Conditions dated March 01, 1994" and
Bayway's Marine Provisions were incorporated in full by reference. Paragraph 10 of those General Terms, which the court called the Tax Clause, provided that the buyer "shall pay seller the amount of any federal, state and local excise, gross receipts, import, motor fuel, superfund and spill taxes and all other federal, state and local taxes however designated, other than taxes on income, paid or incurred by seller directly or indirectly with respect to the oil or product sold hereunder."
OMT did not object to Bayway's confirmation or to the incorporated General Terms. OMT took delivery of the MTBE on March 22, 1998. Under 26 U.S.C. § 4081(a)(1)(A)(iv), a sale of taxable fuel to a person who is not registered with the Internal Revenue Service is a taxable event. OMT was not registered, so the sale generated federal excise tax of $464,035.12, which Bayway paid. Bayway invoked the Tax Clause and demanded that OMT reimburse the tax. OMT refused, contending that the Tax Clause was never part of the parties' agreement. OMT had no prior course of dealing with Bayway that allocated taxes differently. 215
F.3d at 221-22.

== Procedural History ==
Bayway and Tosco sued OMT in the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York under diversity jurisdiction. On Bayway's motion for summary judgment, Judge McKenna held that the Tax
Clause was properly incorporated into the contract under UCC § 2-207(2) and entered judgment for Bayway on the tax reimbursement claim. OMT's opposition papers were the first place the material-alteration argument appeared; Bayway answered it with affidavits about petroleum-industry custom submitted with its reply papers, and OMT neither sought leave to file a sur-reply (a further response to a reply brief) nor claimed to have contrary evidence.
OMT appealed. It argued that the Tax Clause materially altered the contract, so that it did not become part of the bargain under § 2-207(2)(b), and that the district court should not have considered custom evidence first offered in reply. OMT also argued, for the first time on appeal, that Bayway's actual acceptance had been a separate broker's confirmation containing no additional terms, and that the integration clause in OMT's own confirmation operated as a notification of objection under § 2-207(2)(c). The Second Circuit reviewed the grant of summary judgment de novo, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to OMT, and declined to reach the two arguments raised for the first time on appeal. Id. at 222-23, 227 & n.2.

== Issue ==
When a merchant's confirmation adds a term to a contract that the parties had already agreed to orally, and the other merchant contends that the term materially alters the contract under § 2-207(2)(b), which party bears the burden of proving material alteration? Did OMT carry that burden as to the Tax Clause, that is, would incorporating the clause without OMT's express awareness have produced surprise or hardship? Could the district court properly consider industry-custom evidence that Bayway introduced in its reply papers?

== Holding ==
Between merchants, an additional term in a definite expression of acceptance or a written confirmation becomes part of the contract unless one of the three exceptions in § 2-207(2) applies. Because the statute presumes inclusion, the party that opposes inclusion bears the burden of proving that the term materially alters the contract. A term materially alters the contract if its incorporation without the other party's express awareness would result in surprise or hardship. Surprise has a subjective element, what the objecting party actually knew, and an objective element, what a reasonable merchant in that party's position should have known; the objective element requires proof that a reasonable merchant would not have expected the term, and trade usage and prior course of dealing are the ordinary means of proof.
OMT did not carry that burden. Its executives' actual astonishment established subjective surprise only, and the unrebutted evidence showed that petroleum-industry custom places excise taxes on the buyer, so a reasonable merchant would not have been surprised. Any hardship was limited, routine, and self-inflicted, because OMT could have avoided the tax entirely by registering with the IRS. The Tax Clause therefore became part of the contract, and the district court acted within its discretion in considering evidence that responded to an argument OMT raised for the first time in opposition. Id. at 223-27.

== Judgment ==
The Second Circuit affirmed the judgment of the district court. Summary judgment for Bayway stood, leaving OMT liable to reimburse the $464,035.12 in federal excise tax under the Tax Clause.

== Reasoning ==
The court began with the structure of § 2-207. Bayway's confirmation was a written confirmation of an existing oral agreement, and the parties agreed that it was not expressly conditional on OMT's assent to the additional terms, so it operated as an acceptance under § 2-207(1) even though it added the Tax Clause. The
Tax Clause was therefore a proposal for addition to the contract under § 2-207(2). Because both parties were merchants, the proposal became part of the contract unless the offer expressly limited acceptance to its own terms, the term materially altered the contract, or OMT gave timely notice of objection. Only the material-alteration exception was properly before the court. Official Comment 4 supplies the definition: a material alteration is one that would "result in surprise or hardship if incorporated without express awareness by the other party." Id. at 223.
The burden question was one of first impression in the circuit. The court reasoned from the text: § 2-207(2) provides that additional terms "become part of the contract unless" an exception applies, so inclusion is the default and the party invoking an exception must prove it. That allocation matched the weight of authority, including the Tenth Circuit in Avedon Engineering, Inc. v. Seatex, the First Circuit in JOM, Inc. v. Adell
Plastics, Inc., the Seventh Circuit in Comark Merchandising, Inc. v. Highland Group, Inc., and state courts in
Alaska, Indiana, and Washington. A Texas intermediate appellate decision, Westech Engineering, Inc. v.
Clearwater Constructors, Inc., had placed the burden on the party seeking inclusion; the Second Circuit declined to follow it. Id. at 223-24.
On surprise, the court separated the two elements. OMT's executives described the incorporation of the Tax
Clause as a "contract by ambush" and a sleight-of-hand proposal, which sufficed to show subjective surprise.
Objective surprise required OMT to show that a reasonable merchant would not have consented to the term, and on that question the record ran the other way. Bayway submitted unchallenged testimony from two industry experts that buyers customarily pay all taxes resulting from a petroleum transaction; one stated that the practice was so universally understood that he could not recall an instance in which a buyer had refused to pay the seller for excise or sales taxes. OMT responded with the standard forms of five other sellers. The court found that the CITGO, Conoco, and Enron forms mirrored the Tax Clause, that Chevron's form differed only by folding the taxes into the contract price rather than itemizing them, and that Texaco's form was silent, which on an unrebutted record of universal custom supported no contrary inference. OMT had no prior course of dealing with Bayway to the contrary, and conclusory assertions of surprise could not defeat summary judgment. Id. at 224-25.
On hardship, OMT argued that it was a small business operating on precarious margins and could not absorb a loss of this size. The court found that the cry of hardship rang hollow. The Tax Clause placed on the buyer a responsibility tied to a specific sale of goods, one at least not uncommon in the industry, and one the buyer could have avoided by registering with the IRS; any loss the clause imposed was therefore limited, routine, and self-inflicted. Quoting Judge Posner in Union Carbide Corp. v. Oscar Mayer Foods Corp., 947 F.2d
1333 (7th Cir. 1991), the court noted the view that hardship is a consequence of a material alteration rather than an independent criterion, and that a party cannot walk away from a contract it fairly agreed to merely because performance turns out to be a hardship. The court did not decide whether hardship can ever establish material alteration without surprise, because OMT failed under either reading. Id. at 225-26.
Finally, the court rejected OMT's procedural objection. A district court has discretion to consider reply evidence that addresses new material issues raised in the opposition papers, so as to avoid giving the answering party an unfair advantage. OMT had introduced the material-alteration theory in opposition, had
itself asserted that Bayway offered no evidence of custom, and had never moved for leave to file a sur-reply or identified contrary evidence. Id. at 226-27.

== Separate Opinions ==
The panel was unanimous. The reported decision contains no concurrence or dissent.

== Notes ==
Bayway supplies the Second Circuit's rule on the burden of proof under § 2-207(2)(b) and a working definition of surprise as both subjective and objective. The objective element does the real work: a merchant who is astonished by a term still loses if trade usage shows that a reasonable merchant would have expected it. The case also shows how the material-alteration inquiry is proved. Bayway won on affidavits about industry custom; OMT lost because its competing forms confirmed the custom rather than undermining it.
Two limits deserve attention. The holding rests on an unrebutted record of universal trade custom; the court did not hold that tax-allocation clauses are immaterial as a matter of law. And the court never decided whether hardship alone can make a term material, so Bayway should not be cited for either answer to that question. The forfeited arguments are instructive as well. OMT's own confirmation contained an integration clause, and OMT's first-in-time confirmation might have been characterized as an offer that expressly limited acceptance to its own terms under § 2-207(2)(a) or as a notification of objection under § 2-207(2)(c); those routes were lost because OMT did not raise them in the district court. Compare Dorton, which treats material alteration as a factual question, and Northrop, which addresses different rather than additional terms.
