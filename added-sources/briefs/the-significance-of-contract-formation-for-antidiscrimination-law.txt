The significance of contract formation for antidiscrimination law
Casebook printed page 156, PDF page 206; 42 U.S.C. § 1981; Perry v. Command Performance and Hampton v. Dillard Department Stores.
Source: added-sources/Contracts_Offer_and_Acceptance_Briefs.pdf, pages 12–13

== Preamble ==
Contract formation can determine the existence and scope of a protected civil right. Section 1981 guarantees equal rights to make and enforce contracts and reaches racial discrimination in private contracting. Its current definition covers formation, performance, modification, termination, and enjoyment of the contractual relationship's benefits, privileges, terms, and conditions. A claimant may therefore challenge interference with an existing contract or discriminatory obstruction of a proposed contract. The analysis must identify the contractual activity or right affected and connect its impairment to intentional racial discrimination. 42 U.S.C. § 1981(a)-(c).
The casebook's example, Perry v. Command Performance, 913 F.2d 99 (3d Cir. 1990), arose under an earlier interpretation of § 1981. In Patterson v. McLean Credit Union, 491 U.S. 164 (1989), the Supreme
Court limited the statute's protection concerning the making of contracts and generally excluded discriminatory conduct occurring afterward during performance. That interpretation made the instant of formation decisive in some service transactions. Congress subsequently expanded the statute through the
Civil Rights Act of 1991. The chronological distinction is necessary to understand both the 1990 decision and the rule applicable today.
Edith Perry, a Black woman, had used the Command Performance salon in Pennsylvania's King of Prussia
Mall more than five times. On October 12, 1987, her husband telephoned the salon and arranged a noon appointment for her wash and set. The full opinion identifies her husband as the caller, although the casebook compresses that detail. When Perry arrived, Helene Kugler, who had previously styled her hair, explained that she had a bad cold and asked whether another hairdresser could do the work. Perry agreed.
According to Perry's complaint and deposition, the substitute, Beth Abbott, loudly refused because Perry was Black and made explicitly racial statements about whom she would serve. Perry became distressed, summoned mall security, and left with her husband. Perry, 913 F.2d at 100.
Perry sued the salon under § 1981 and Pennsylvania's law of intentional infliction of emotional distress. The district court granted summary judgment for the salon because it assumed the telephone appointment had already created a contract on racially neutral terms. Under its reading of Patterson, the subsequent refusal therefore constituted conduct outside the statute's contract-making protection. Perry appealed. The Third
Circuit reviewed summary judgment de novo, meaning that it independently applied the governing legal standard, with the evidence and reasonable inferences viewed favorably to Perry as the nonmoving party.
The appellate court vacated the order and remanded for further proceedings; it did not find the salon liable or award damages. 913 F.2d at 100-02.
The first problem was the absence of a sufficient factual basis for treating the telephone appointment as a completed contract. An appointment might reserve an opportunity to negotiate or obtain service, with contractual acceptance occurring when the service begins. Alternatively, the call might exchange present commitments to provide and pay for the service. The record did not establish which meaning applied. The
Third Circuit directed further consideration of industry practice and the parties' expectations. It did not announce that every appointment is merely preliminary or that no appointment can create a contract. It held that the district court could not assume formation at the telephone call on this record. 913 F.2d at 101-02.
The second problem remained even if the appointment formed a contract. The salon might have agreed to serve a Black customer only if a willing hairdresser happened to be available, while undertaking to serve a white customer without that racial condition. If evidence established such unequal terms at formation, the claim could fit § 1981 even under Patterson. The court therefore required an opportunity to develop evidence concerning the terms of the supposed agreement. It also left unresolved whether the conduct could be attributed to the salon and whether the required intentional discrimination occurred. A factual dispute over whether Kugler offered to serve Perry after Abbott's outburst likewise remained in the record. 913 F.2d at 102 & n.1.
Congress's 1991 amendment added § 1981(b), expressly extending the statute to performance and the enjoyment of contractual benefits. For a comparable transaction governed by that amended provision, an earlier contract no longer excludes a claim simply because the refusal occurs during performance. Formation continues to matter because the claimant must identify a relevant contractual relationship or attempted transaction and the particular right impaired. The amendment changes the consequences of the timing analysis without eliminating the need to examine the transaction. The full Perry opinion is available at 913
F.2d 99.
The casebook also cites Hampton v. Dillard Department Stores, Inc., 247 F.3d 1091 (10th Cir. 2001), which illustrates the importance of identifying the contract and its benefits after the amendment. Paula Hampton bought children's clothing at Dillard's. The sales clerk gave Hampton and her niece, Demetria Cooper, coupons for fragrance samples. As they attempted to redeem the coupons, a security officer interrupted them and examined Hampton's shopping bag on suspicion of shoplifting; the goods matched her receipt. Their litigation included § 1981 claims concerning discriminatory interference with obtaining the samples. The contractual question was whether receipt of a sample formed part of the purchase's benefits, even though no separate price was charged for it. 247 F.3d at 1099-1105.
Hampton's sample claim proceeded to a jury, which found that the sample was a benefit of her purchase and that the store intentionally interfered with her ability to obtain it on racial grounds. The Tenth Circuit upheld her judgment. Cooper's claim had a different result because she had neither made nor attempted a purchase and lacked an enforceable third-party-beneficiary interest under the court's analysis. Receiving a coupon and accompanying a purchaser did not, on that record, establish her own protected contractual interest. The contrast turns on the legal relationship each plaintiff established. The court's disposition concerning the two plaintiffs shows why identifying the contracting party and the promised benefit remains consequential. 247
F.3d at 1101-05, 1117-19.
For present-day use, the older opinions also require a causation qualification. Comcast Corp. v. National
Ass'n of African American-Owned Media, 589 U.S. 327 (2020), holds that a § 1981 plaintiff must plead and prove that race was a but-for cause of the injury. That requires a showing that the relevant injury would not have occurred in the absence of the racial discrimination; it does not require race to be the only cause. The earlier Hampton opinion's discussion of a motivating-factor instruction should therefore not be copied as the current general causation standard. The current qualification is supplemental to the assigned formation topic.
Comcast, 589 U.S. at 341.
The contracts-class inquiry begins with what the parties proposed and promised, what conduct expressed assent, when any agreement formed, and which benefits followed from that agreement. The civil-rights inquiry then asks whether intentional racial discrimination impaired a protected contractual activity under the statute applicable to the conduct. A refusal can prevent formation and still support a statutory claim concerning the right to contract. Conversely, a completed sale may retain unperformed benefits whose discriminatory denial remains actionable. Hampton, 247 F.3d 1091; 42 U.S.C. § 1981.
