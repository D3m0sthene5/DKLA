Reasonable Notice After Mullane

Source: added-sources/CivPro_Notice_Service_and_Hearing_Briefs.pdf, pages 6–8

== Sources ==
Casebook pp. 245-251, Notes and Questions 1 through 11 following Mullane. The notes trace how courts applied Mullane's reasonable-calculation standard to publication, posting, mail, statutory agents, the time allowed to respond, the content of the notice, and advance waivers of notice. Citations follow the casebook.
The recurring question is what the sender knew, or could reasonably have learned, when it chose its method of notice.
Publication When Names and Addresses Are Known
McDonald v. Mabee, 243 U.S. 90 (1917), which Mullane relied on, involved a suit on a promissory note against a Texas domiciliary who had left the state to take up a domicile elsewhere while his family remained in Texas. Service was attempted by publication in a local newspaper once a week for four weeks, Mabee never appeared, and the Supreme Court held the resulting judgment void: to dispense with personal service, the substitute most likely to reach the defendant is the least that ought to be required. Mullane's own formulation is somewhat more forgiving, because it accepts a method that is not substantially less likely to reach the party than feasible substitutes; the casebook asks whether Mullane permits constructive service only when it is the method most likely to succeed.
The Court then applied the known-address rule to government proceedings against property. In Walker v.
City of Hutchinson, 352 U.S. 112 (1956), notice of a condemnation proceeding (a government taking of private property for public use) by publication in a local newspaper was inadequate for a resident owner whose name was known from official records. In Schroeder v. City of New York, 371 U.S. 208 (1962), newspaper notice in two small communities far from vacant land, together with signs posted on trees near the property, was inadequate because the owner's name and address could be found in deed records and tax rolls.
Mennonite Board of Missions v. Adams, 462 U.S. 791 (1983), extended the rule to a mortgagee whose interest would be affected by a tax sale. Because the mortgagee was identified in a publicly recorded mortgage, publication and posting had to be supplemented by mail to its last known address or by personal service; notice by mail or other means as certain to ensure actual notice is a minimum constitutional precondition to a proceeding that will adversely affect any party whose name and address are reasonably ascertainable. Justice O'Connor, joined by Justices Powell and Rehnquist, dissented on the ground that the majority had adopted a bright-line rule at odds with Mullane's balancing. Five years later Justice O'Connor wrote for the Court in Tulsa Professional Collection Services, Inc. v. Pope, 485 U.S. 478 (1988), which held that Oklahoma's probate nonclaim statute, barring creditors' claims not filed within two months after published notice, could not bar a known or reasonably ascertainable creditor (there, a hospital owed for the decedent's care) without actual notice. The Court added that actual notice may be dispensed with for creditors whose claims are merely conjectural.
Posting and Mail
Greene v. Lindsey, 456 U.S. 444 (1982), held that posting eviction notices on apartment doors in a public housing project was inadequate where process servers knew that such notices were "not infrequently" removed by children or other tenants; in those circumstances due process required notice by mail. Justice
O'Connor, joined by Chief Justice Burger and Justice Rehnquist, dissented, questioning whether mail is more reliable than posting and noting that unattended mailboxes are subject to theft. The case shows that Mullane asks about the reliability of a method in the circumstances the sender knew, not in the abstract. The casebook also asks whether the tenants' interest in avoiding public posting of their financial troubles should count in the analysis.
Actual Receipt and Follow-Up
Dusenbery v. United States, 534 U.S. 161 (2002), involved the forfeiture of about $30,000 and a car belonging to a federal prisoner. The FBI published notice and sent certified letters to the prison, to his residence at the time of arrest, and to his stepmother's address, and the prison followed logged procedures for delivering certified mail to inmates. The Court held the notice adequate: due process does not require actual receipt, and the government need not make heroic efforts to ensure delivery. Justice Ginsburg, joined by
Justices Stevens, Souter, and Breyer, dissented on the ground that the notice was substantially less likely to succeed than a feasible alternative. No Justice treated the absence of actual notice as itself unconstitutional.
Jones v. Flowers, 547 U.S. 220 (2006), qualified that approach. When a mailed notice of a tax sale came back unclaimed, the State had to take reasonable additional steps to notify the owner if it was practicable to do so.
Chief Justice Roberts reasoned that no one who actually wanted to inform an owner would do nothing after learning that the letter had failed, and he identified reasonable steps: resending the notice by regular mail, posting it on the front door, or addressing mail to the "occupant." The State was not required to search phone books or tax rolls for a new address. Justice Thomas, joined by Justices Scalia and Kennedy, dissented, arguing that reasonableness must be judged when notice is sent and that a duty triggered by later information has no natural end point. Earlier, Covey v. Town of Somers, 351 U.S. 141 (1956), held mailed notice of a tax foreclosure inadequate when sent to a person the town knew had been adjudged insane and committed to a hospital without a guardian. Together, Covey and Jones show that knowledge that a method will fail, whether acquired before or after sending, affects what due process requires.
Statutory Agents
Wuchter v. Pizzutti, 276 U.S. 13 (1928), involved a nonresident-motorist statute like the one upheld in Hess v. Pawloski, which authorized service on the Secretary of State. The statute did not require that notice be sent to the nonresident, and a divided Court held it invalid even though the defendant in fact received notice. A statute of this kind must itself require the plaintiff or the official receiving service to communicate with the defendant. The case measures the statutory scheme rather than the result in a particular case. Szukhent later distinguished Wuchter as a limit on state statutory schemes rather than on private contractual appointments of an agent.
Time and Content of Notice
Notice must allow adequate time to respond. In Roller v. Holly, 176 U.S. 398 (1900), a defendant served in
Virginia was required to defend an action in Texas five days later, and the Court held the procedure violated due process. Federal Rule 12(a) now allows 21 days after service, or 60 days after a waiver request is sent.
Notice must also convey the information a party needs to respond. In Aguchak v. Montgomery Ward Co., 520
P.2d 1352 (Alaska 1974), a seller sued rural buyers of a snowmobile and a freezer in small-claims court and obtained a default judgment of $988.22 plus costs. The summons did not tell the buyers that they could appear by written pleading or that they could request a change of venue, and travel to court would have required an overnight stay costing $186. The Supreme Court of Alaska held that a small-claims summons must include that information and set aside the default judgment.
Advance Waiver of Notice
A cognovit note is a written instrument in which a debtor authorizes entry of judgment against it without notice or hearing if it does not pay when due; it waives prior notice, bars the assertion of defenses, and forecloses appeal from the resulting judgment. In D.H. Overmyer Co. v. Frick Co., 405 U.S. 174 (1972), the
Court held that enforcing a cognovit note does not per se violate due process and that validity depends on the circumstances, including consideration for the waiver and the parties' relative bargaining power. Fuentes v.
Shevin later distinguished Overmyer when it refused to find a waiver in a consumer's printed installment contract.

== Application ==
Start with the proceeding. Mullane applies to any proceeding that will be accorded finality and can deprive a person of property, including tax sales, probate claim bars, evictions, and forfeitures. Identify each class of interested parties and ask what the sender knew or could reasonably ascertain when it gave notice. A name and address available from the sender's own files or from public records call for mail or an equally reliable method; a party who cannot be located with due diligence may be notified by publication. Next, ask whether the method was reliable in the circumstances the sender actually knew, including known reasons it would fail, such as posted notices that disappear, an addressee known to be incompetent, or mail returned unclaimed. Finally, check the time allowed to respond, the information the notice conveyed, and whether the party validly waived notice in advance.
