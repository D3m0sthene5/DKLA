Scope
Article 2 generally applies to transactions in goods. Under § 2-105, goods are things movable when identified to the contract, including specially manufactured goods. Carpeting and steel coils therefore fit
Article 2 in Dorton and C. Itoh. Goods also include unborn animals and growing crops, subject to the Code's provisions concerning things attached to realty. Money used as the price, investment securities, and things in action fall outside the statutory definition. A sale of land or a contract solely for professional services ordinarily falls under other law.
A transaction need not involve two merchants to fall within Article 2. A consumer buying a refrigerator and a private owner selling a used refrigerator can enter sales contracts governed by Article 2. Merchant status matters only where a particular provision makes it matter. Section 2-205 requires the offeror to be a merchant; § 2-207(2)'s automatic incorporation of certain additional terms applies between merchants.
Sections 2-204 and 2-206 do not generally require merchant status.
Contracts combining goods and services require a further classification. Many courts ask whether the predominant purpose of the transaction is a sale of goods with incidental labor or a service with incidental goods. The contract's language, the supplier's business, and the relative significance of the goods and services help answer that question. A purchase of equipment with installation can be predominantly a sale; a painting service does not ordinarily become a sale of goods merely because the contractor supplies paint.
Jurisdictions differ in their treatment of mixed transactions. The uploaded casebook illustrates the predominant-factor approach through Bonebrake v. Cox, 499 F.2d 951, 960 (8th Cir. 1974), in its later discussion of Article 2's scope.
Merchant Status
Section 2-104 includes a person who deals in goods of the kind, a person whose occupation holds the person out as having knowledge or skill peculiar to the practices or goods involved, and a person to whom such knowledge or skill is attributed through an agent or intermediary. The relevant expertise depends on the provision being applied. A business may possess expertise in ordinary commercial practices even when it does not regularly sell the particular goods involved in a transaction.
For firm offers, knowledge of commercial practices can suffice. A manufacturer selling used factory equipment therefore should not automatically be treated as a nonmerchant simply because selling that equipment is not its regular business. The casebook contrasts this broader inquiry with the implied warranty of merchantability, which § 2-314 ties to a merchant dealing in goods of the kind. Merchant status must be connected to the specific statutory rule, rather than treated as a universal label with identical consequences throughout Article 2.
Firm Offers
Section 2-205 protects an offer by a merchant to buy or sell goods when a signed writing gives assurance that the offer will be held open. No consideration is required for that assurance. If the assurance states a period, statutory irrevocability lasts for that period, subject to a three-month maximum. If no period is stated, it lasts for a reasonable time, also subject to that maximum. If the offeree supplies the form containing the assurance, the offeror must separately sign the assurance; a general signature on the form is insufficient for this special requirement.
An ordinary offer and an assurance against revocation perform different functions. A quotation must first be sufficiently definite and committed to count as an offer. Its identifying an acceptance deadline does not automatically establish every element of a firm offer. The writing must also communicate that the offer will be held open. Conversely, a qualifying firm offer remains only an offer until accepted. Section 2-205 binds the merchant's assurance without requiring the offeree to accept the proposed sale.
The three-month ceiling limits irrevocability supplied by § 2-205 without consideration. It does not impose a three-month ceiling on a separately supported option contract. It also does not necessarily make the underlying offer lapse when statutory protection ends. An offer that remains outstanding may still be accepted after that point unless it has expired, been effectively revoked, or otherwise terminated. A stated four-month acceptance period and a three-month period of statutory irrevocability can therefore coexist.

== Application ==
Suppose a merchant signs an offer to sell 500 chairs for a specified price and promises to keep it open for sixty days. Section 2-205 can prevent revocation during those sixty days even if the buyer pays nothing for the assurance and never relies on it. If the buyer prepared a form containing that assurance, the seller must separately sign the assurance. If the same writing promises six months of irrevocability, § 2-205 alone supplies at most three months; additional legal grounds must support protection beyond that period.
The contrast with Drennan is central. Star Paving made an oral bid for construction work, and the court protected foreseeable reliance under common-law principles. Drennan does not depend on a merchant's signed assurance. Section 2-205 supplies an independent statutory route, with its own scope and formal requirements. Failure to qualify under that section is not itself proof that every possible consideration-based or reliance-based argument fails; those arguments require their own analysis under the governing law.
