Klocek v. Gateway, Inc.
Klocek v. Gateway, Inc., 104 F. Supp. 2d 1332 (D. Kan. 2000). United States District Court for the District of
Kansas, No. 99-2499-KHV, memorandum and order of June 15, 2000, by Judge Kathryn H. Vratil. The action was later dismissed for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction in a motion the plaintiff joined, Klocek v.
Gateway, Inc., 2000 WL 1372886 (D. Kan.), so the ruling was never reviewed on appeal. The decision addresses whether standard terms shipped in the box with a computer, including an arbitration clause, bound a consumer who kept the computer more than five days. Full judicial opinion.
Source: added-sources/Contracts_Battle_of_the_Forms_and_Rolling_Contracts_Briefs.pdf, pages 12–15

== Parties ==
William S. Klocek, an individual consumer who represented himself (pro se), was the plaintiff. Gateway,
Inc., which sold him a computer, and Hewlett-Packard, Inc., which made a scanner he bought to use with it, were the defendants. Gateway sought dismissal and an order compelling arbitration under paragraph 10 of its
Standard Terms and Conditions Agreement. Hewlett-Packard moved to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. Klocek sought damages, individually and for a class, for products that did not work as promised and for false promises of technical support.

== Material Facts ==
Klocek's complaint stated that he lived in Missouri. Gateway's affidavit said only that it shipped a computer to him on or about August 31, 1997. Klocek responded that he bought the computer in person at Gateway's store in Overland Park, Kansas, on August 27, 1997, and took it with him. The box contained the computer, a battery, power cables, instruction manuals, and Gateway's Standard Terms and Conditions Agreement, a four-page document with sixteen numbered paragraphs. At the top of the first page, in emphasized type inside a printed box, a notice stated: "This document contains Gateway 2000's Standard Terms and
Conditions. By keeping your Gateway 2000 computer system beyond five (5) days after the date of delivery, you accept these Terms and Conditions." Paragraph 10, headed Dispute Resolution, provided that any dispute or controversy arising out of or relating to the agreement or its interpretation would be settled exclusively and finally by arbitration under the Rules of Conciliation and Arbitration of the International Chamber of
Commerce, conducted in Chicago, Illinois, before a sole arbitrator, with any award final and binding.
Gateway offered no evidence that at the time of the sale it told Klocek about the Standard Terms, the five-day review period, or the arbitration clause; its evidence showed only that it enclosed the document in the box for him to read afterwards. Klocek alleged that Gateway induced him and other consumers to buy computers and support packages through false promises of technical support, and individually claimed breach of contract and breach of warranty on the ground that the computer was not compatible with standard peripherals and
Internet services as warranted. Against Hewlett-Packard he alleged a breach of the duty to warn that its scanner was incompatible with Gateway computers. 104 F. Supp. 2d at 1334-35, 1337.

== Procedural History ==
Klocek filed in the District of Kansas under diversity jurisdiction. Gateway moved on November 22, 1999, to dismiss and to compel arbitration under the Federal Arbitration Act, 9 U.S.C. §§ 3-4. Hewlett-Packard moved separately to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction because the damages alleged against it did not satisfy the $75,000 amount-in-controversy requirement. Klocek filed motions of his own, including for class certification and sanctions. The court applied a standard similar to summary judgment: Gateway bore an initial burden to show an enforceable agreement to arbitrate, and only if it met that burden would the burden shift to Klocek to show a genuine issue of fact for trial. The court could not tell from the record whether
Kansas, Missouri, or some other state's law governed formation, but found no material difference between
Kansas and Missouri law because both states had adopted the relevant provisions of UCC Article 2. Id. at
1335-37.

== Issue ==
Did Klocek agree to the arbitration clause in the Standard Terms? Answering that question required the court to decide whether the Seventh Circuit's approach in Hill and ProCD should be followed; whether § 2-207 applies to a transaction in which only one party's form exists; which party was the offeror; whether Gateway's acceptance was expressly conditional on assent to the Standard Terms; and whether a buyer who is not a merchant expressly assents to additional terms by keeping the goods beyond a stated period.

== Holding ==
Whether the parties agreed to arbitrate is governed by ordinary state contract law, and the party seeking arbitration must prove the agreement. In a typical consumer transaction the purchaser is the offeror and the vendor accepts by completing the sale or shipping the goods. Terms shipped with the goods are additional terms in an expression of acceptance or a written confirmation, and § 2-207 applies even though only one form exists. An acceptance is expressly conditional under § 2-207(1) only if it clearly tells the offeror that the offeree is unwilling to proceed unless the additional terms are included. Where the buyer is not a merchant, additional terms are proposals that become part of the contract only if the buyer expressly agrees to them, and express assent cannot be presumed from silence or a failure to object.
Gateway offered no evidence that it, rather than Klocek, made the offer; no evidence that it conditioned the sale on acceptance of the Standard Terms; and no evidence that Klocek expressly agreed to them. Keeping the computer beyond five days did not demonstrate express agreement on this record. Gateway therefore failed to carry its burden of proving an agreement to arbitrate. Id. at 1337-41.

== Judgment ==
The court overruled Gateway's motion to dismiss and compel arbitration. It sustained Hewlett-Packard's motion and dismissed the claims against it for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction, and it overruled Klocek's own motions. Gateway was given until June 30, 2000, to file a supplemental motion if it contended that some law other than that of Kansas or Missouri governed formation, with a response due July 24 and a reply due
August 7, 2000, and the court reserved the possibility of a jury trial on whether an arbitration agreement existed.

== Reasoning ==
The court started from the Federal Arbitration Act's framework. Federal policy favors arbitration and resolves doubts about the scope of arbitrable issues in its favor, but whether an agreement to arbitrate exists is decided under ordinary state-law principles of contract formation, and the party seeking arbitration bears a summary-judgment-like burden of proving that agreement. Gateway rested its motion on Hill and ProCD. Id.
at 1336-38.
Judge Vratil examined those cases and declined to follow them. In each, the Seventh Circuit had concluded without support that § 2-207 was irrelevant because only one written form was involved, and it had provided no explanation for its conclusion that the vendor is the master of the offer. Neither conclusion, the court found, is supported by the statute or by Kansas or Missouri law. By its terms § 2-207 applies to "an acceptance or written confirmation" and says nothing that requires a second form; Official Comment 1 states that the section applies where an agreement has been reached orally and is followed by one or both parties sending formal memoranda that add terms. Other courts had treated terms shipped with goods the same way:
Step-Saver Data Systems, Inc. v. Wyse Technology, 939 F.2d 91 (3d Cir. 1991), held that box-top license terms were proposals under § 2-207 that required the buyer's express assent because the contract had already been formed by the parties' conduct; Arizona Retail Systems, Inc. v. Software Link, Inc., 831 F. Supp. 759 (D.
Ariz. 1993), treated a license shipped with software as a proposed modification requiring express assent; and
U.S. Surgical Corp. v. Orris, Inc., 5 F. Supp. 2d 1201 (D. Kan. 1998), held that a single-use restriction on packaging that arrived after the sales contract was formed was a proposed modification needing the buyer's agreement. Id. at 1338-40.
The court then identified the offeror. In typical consumer transactions the purchaser is the offeror and the vendor the offeree, as in Brown Machine, Division of John Brown, Inc. v. Hercules, Inc., 770 S.W.2d 416 (Mo. Ct. App. 1989), and Rich Products Corp. v. Kemutec, Inc., 66 F. Supp. 2d 937 (E.D. Wis. 1999). A vendor can be the offeror in a particular transaction, but Gateway offered no factual evidence supporting that characterization here. The court therefore assumed that Klocek offered to buy the computer, in person or by order, and that Gateway accepted by completing the sale or shipping the goods. Under § 2-207(1) the
Standard Terms were either an expression of acceptance or a written confirmation containing additional terms. Gateway's acceptance was not expressly conditional on assent to those terms: a conditional acceptance must clearly express the offeree's unwillingness to proceed unless the additional terms are included, and shipping the goods with the terms attached did not communicate any unwillingness to proceed without
Klocek's agreement. Gateway also offered no evidence that at the time of the sale it told Klocek the transaction was conditioned on acceptance of the Standard Terms. Id. at 1340-41.
Because Klocek was not a merchant, § 2-207(2) treated the additional terms, including arbitration, as proposals that became part of the contract only if he expressly agreed to them. Gateway argued that the five-day notice made retention of the computer an acceptance of the terms. The court disagreed on this record. Express assent cannot be presumed from silence or a mere failure to object; Gateway's evidence showed only that it enclosed the Standard Terms for Klocek to read after the purchase, with no evidence that the parties discussed additional terms or that Klocek was told the five-day review period was a condition of the sale. Keeping the computer past five days was therefore insufficient to show that Klocek expressly agreed to the Standard Terms, and Gateway had not shown an enforceable agreement to arbitrate. Because Gateway had not addressed choice of law, the court allowed it to renew the motion if it contended that another state's law would produce a different result. Id. at 1341.
Hewlett-Packard's motion succeeded on a separate ground: the damages alleged against it fell short of the
$75,000 required for diversity jurisdiction, and the complaint did not support a punitive award large enough to close the gap. Id. at 1342.

== Separate Opinions ==
A single district judge decided the motions. The reported decision contains no separate opinion.

== Notes ==
Klocek and Hill involve the same seller, materially similar documents, and opposite results, and the divergence turns on two framing choices. First, who made the offer: Hill treats the vendor as master of the offer, while Klocek applies the default that the purchaser offers and the vendor accepts by shipping. Second, what body of law governs the terms that arrive with the goods: Hill excludes § 2-207 because there is one form, while Klocek applies it as written and reads Comment 1 to cover confirmations that follow an earlier agreement. Once Klocek's framing is accepted, the consumer's status as a non-merchant makes the box terms proposals requiring express assent, and retention alone does not supply it.
The holding is narrower than it is sometimes described. The court decided that Gateway failed to prove its case on this record; a vendor that shows it disclosed the terms and the review period at the point of sale, or that it was the offeror, could reach a different outcome under the same framework. The Restatement of the
Law, Consumer Contracts § 2(b) adopts a rule between the two cases: it permits terms delivered after assent to be adopted, as Hill does, but only if the business proves that it gave the consumer notice of those terms before assent and a reasonable opportunity to review and terminate afterward, a burden that resembles the one Gateway failed to carry in Klocek.
