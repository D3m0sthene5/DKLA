Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz
471 U.S. 462 (1985). Supreme Court of the United States. No. 83-2097; argued January 8, 1985; decided May 20, 1985. Brennan, J., for a six-Justice majority; Stevens, J., joined by White, J., dissenting; Powell, J., took no part. Review of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit.
Source: added-sources/CivPro_Asahi_Nicastro_Burger_King_Case_Briefs (1).pdf, pages 5–6

== Parties ==
Appellant / plaintiff: Burger King Corporation, a Florida corporation with principal offices in Miami that licensed and supervised restaurant franchises. Appellee / defendant: John Rudzewicz, a Michigan resident and senior partner in a
Detroit accounting firm who supplied investment capital and personally assumed a twenty-year franchise obligation.
Other defendant below: Brian MacShara, a Michigan resident who proposed the venture and managed the restaurant.
MacShara allowed his judgment to become final and did not participate in the Supreme Court appeal.

== Procedural History ==
In May 1981, Burger King sued Rudzewicz and MacShara in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of
Florida. Federal subject-matter jurisdiction rested on diversity, 28 U.S.C. § 1332(a), and the federal trademark grant, §
1338(a). Burger King alleged breach through failure to send required payments to Miami and trademark infringement through continued operation after termination. It sought damages, an injunction, possession, fees, and costs. The defendants were served in Michigan under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 4 and entered special appearances challenging personal jurisdiction.
After a hearing, the district court denied the jurisdictional motions. It applied Florida Statutes § 48.193(1)(g), which reaches a person who breaches a contract in Florida by failing to perform acts required there. The defendants answered, and they counterclaimed under the Michigan Franchise Investment Law. Following a three-day bench trial, the court reaffirmed jurisdiction, found breach and trademark infringement, rejected the counterclaim, entered $228,875 in joint and several contract damages, ordered closure or surrender of the restaurant, and awarded fees and costs.
MacShara took no appeal. Rudzewicz compromised with Burger King and waived appellate review of the trademark-infringement finding and injunction. A divided Eleventh Circuit panel reversed the remaining judgment.
Burger King Corp. v. MacShara, 724 F.2d 1505 (11th Cir. 1984). The parties had stipulated that Florida's long-arm statute reached Rudzewicz, so the panel decided federal due process and held Florida litigation fundamentally unfair.
Rehearing en banc was denied, 729 F.2d 1468.
Burger King sought a direct Supreme Court appeal under 28 U.S.C. § 1254(2), a former provision allowing appeal when a court of appeals held a state statute unconstitutional. The Supreme Court dismissed that route because the
Eleventh Circuit had accepted a statutory stipulation and had not clearly adjudged the Florida statute unconstitutional.
Using 28 U.S.C. § 2103, the Court treated the jurisdictional statement as a certiorari petition, granted it, reversed 6-2, and remanded.

== Material Facts ==
Burger King granted twenty-year trademark licenses, leased standardized facilities, and provided market research, advertising assistance, management training, accounting guidance, and operational systems. Franchisees paid a $40,000 initial fee, monthly royalties and advertising charges, and rent tied partly to gross sales. They accepted detailed standards governing food, service, premises, staffing, insurance, accounting, default, and termination.
Burger King's structure divided functions between Miami headquarters and ten district offices. The agreements established the franchise relationship in Miami, selected Florida law, and required fees and notices to be sent to Miami.
Headquarters set policy and controlled major disputes; the Michigan district office handled routine monitoring and relayed matters beyond its authority.
Rudzewicz and MacShara applied through the Birmingham, Michigan, office in autumn 1978. Miami approved a preliminary agreement in February 1979. MacShara attended management training in Miami, and the franchisees bought $165,000 in equipment from Burger King's Miami division. During five months of negotiation, disputes arose over development fees, building design, rent, and personal liability. The franchisees negotiated through both Michigan and Miami, obtained limited concessions from Miami, and began operating in Drayton Plains in June 1979. Rudzewicz personally assumed obligations exceeding $1 million over twenty years.
A late-1979 recession reduced patronage, and the franchisees fell behind on payments due in Miami. Headquarters sent default notices and conducted extended negotiations by telephone and mail, handled credit and refinancing discussions, imposed cost-cutting measures, terminated the franchise, and ordered surrender of the premises. Rudzewicz and
MacShara continued operating under Burger King's marks. Rudzewicz maintained his residence, restaurant, staff, and ordinary business operations in Michigan and personally never visited Florida.

== Issue ==
Does Florida's exercise of specific personal jurisdiction satisfy the Fourteenth Amendment when a Michigan franchisee formed a carefully structured twenty-year relationship with a Florida corporation, accepted continuing supervision and obligations centered in Miami, sent payments and communications there, and allegedly caused forum-related injury through breach, while conducting the restaurant's physical operations in Michigan?

== Holding ==
Yes. Rudzewicz deliberately created a substantial and continuing relationship with Burger King's Miami headquarters, and Burger King's claims arose from that relationship. The negotiations, contemplated future consequences, contract terms, and actual course of dealing established purposeful Florida contacts. Those contacts made jurisdiction presumptively reasonable, and Rudzewicz presented no compelling fairness showing sufficient to overcome that conclusion. The Court reversed the Eleventh Circuit and remanded.

== Reasoning ==
Constitutional function: Personal jurisdiction protects an individual liberty interest by ensuring fair warning about the sovereigns capable of entering binding judgments. That warning lets a potential defendant structure primary conduct, insure, price risk, choose contractual protections, and anticipate where suit may occur. The right is waivable through consent and other legal arrangements.
Stage one - purposeful minimum contacts and relatedness: Specific jurisdiction requires forum contacts created by the defendant's own purposeful conduct and a claim arising out of or relating to those contacts. Purposeful availment identifies deliberate affiliation that invokes the forum's benefits and protections. Mail, telephone, and continuing interstate obligations can create that affiliation because modern commercial activity routinely operates across state lines.
Contract analysis: The existence of an interstate contract begins the inquiry. Courts evaluate prior negotiations, contemplated future consequences, contract terms, and the parties' actual course of dealing. These features reveal the relationship the defendant chose to create. A contract often records earlier negotiations and organizes future performance, so its jurisdictional significance comes from the full commercial relationship.
Application to Rudzewicz: He reached from Michigan to obtain a long-term affiliation with a Florida enterprise, negotiated contested terms with Miami, accepted a twenty-year regulated relationship, promised payments and notices to Miami, communicated repeatedly with headquarters, and depended on Miami for major decisions. The breach claims arose directly from payments due there and continuing use of a relationship governed from there. These were substantial, deliberate contacts.
Choice of law: The Florida choice-of-law clause selected the governing substantive law and stated that the agreement became valid upon acceptance in Miami. It did not select an exclusive litigation forum and could not create jurisdiction by itself. Combined with the long-term relationship and course of dealing, it reinforced Rudzewicz's deliberate affiliation with Florida and notice that Florida law would protect and regulate the relationship.
Stage two - fair play and substantial justice: After purposeful contacts are established, courts consider the defendant's litigation burden, forum interest, plaintiff's convenient and effective relief, interstate efficiency, and shared substantive policies. Strong fairness considerations can make jurisdiction reasonable on a comparatively modest contact showing. A defendant with purposeful contacts must present a compelling case that the factors make jurisdiction unconstitutional.
Fairness application: Florida had a legitimate interest in claims concerning obligations deliberately centered there.
Michigan's franchise policy could be accommodated through choice-of-law rules. The record supplied no concrete witness or evidence burden of constitutional magnitude; ordinary inconvenience could be addressed through transfer under 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a). Disparity in resources alone did not defeat the consequences of Rudzewicz's purposeful commercial affiliation.
Trial findings and Rule 52(a): The district court found after trial that Burger King made no misrepresentations, that the franchisees were experienced and sophisticated, and that they acted free of economic duress imposed by Burger
King. Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 52(a)'s clear-error standard governed those findings. The appellate record supplied no basis for a definite and firm conviction that the trial judge had erred.
Justice Stevens's dissent: The dissent emphasized Rudzewicz's Michigan residence, restaurant, employees, taxes, customers, face-to-face dealings, and operational supervision. It viewed the Michigan office as the practical center of the relationship and found the distant Miami provisions insufficient to give a small franchisee fair warning of Florida litigation. The dissent would have affirmed the Eleventh Circuit's fairness assessment.
Exam rule: For a contract dispute, identify who initiated the interstate relationship; map negotiations, expected duration, future obligations, payment destination, governing law, decisionmaking authority, and course of dealing;
connect the claim to those contacts; then apply all five fairness factors. Once purposeful contacts exist, specify the compelling hardship or policy conflict the defendant offers to defeat jurisdiction.
