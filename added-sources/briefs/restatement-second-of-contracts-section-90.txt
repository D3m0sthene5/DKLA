Restatement (Second) of Contracts Section 90
Promise Reasonably Inducing Action or Forbearance. Restatement (Second) of Contracts Section 90 (1981), including Subsections (1)-(2) and Comments a-d. The operative language is organized and paraphrased.
Source: added-sources/Contracts_Promissory_Estoppel_Restitution_Big_Brief.pdf, pages 14–15

== Parties ==
Promisor: the person who makes a commitment and bears the objective risk of reliance the commitment should reasonably induce. Promisee: the addressee whose action or forbearance may make the commitment binding. Third person: another foreseeable actor whose induced reliance can also satisfy Subsection (1). Court: the decisionmaker that determines promise, foreseeability, inducement, injustice, and the form and scope of relief.

== Procedural History ==
The American Law Institute adopted the original Section 90 in the Restatement (First) of Contracts in 1932. That version required expected and actual action or forbearance of a definite and substantial character and made the promise binding when enforcement alone could avoid injustice. Courts used it to organize earlier lines involving charitable subscriptions, gratuitous promises, family support, pensions, and precontractual reliance.
The 1981 Restatement (Second) retained foreseeable and induced reliance plus the injustice requirement. It added promisee-or-third-person language, placed remedial limitation in the operative rule, and created a separate subsection for charitable subscriptions and marriage settlements. The comments explain how reliance quality, formality, bargain policy, unjust enrichment, and remedy interact.

== Material Facts ==
Element 1 - Promise: The promisor must manifest a commitment sufficiently definite for a court to identify the promised course and connect reliance to it. A prediction, opinion, negotiation position, expression of present intention, or statement of hope receives its legal effect from context and the justified understanding it creates.
Element 2 - Reasonable expectation of inducement: The question is objective. The promisor bears responsibility for action or forbearance that a reasonable person in the promisor's position should foresee from the promise's words, setting, relationship, and known purpose. Actual desire for reliance strengthens the case, while the text requires reasonable expectability.
Element 3 - Promisee or third person: Reliance can occur through the promisee or through another person whose response falls within the promise's reasonably expected reach. The third-person language allows enforcement when the commitment is designed to guide family members, lenders, employees, investors, beneficiaries, or other identified actors.
Element 4 - Actual inducement: The promise must cause the relevant action or forbearance. Timing, testimony, contemporaneous communications, alternative motivations, and conduct consistent with an independently made decision bear on causation. Reliance can consist of affirmative expenditure or performance, surrender of an opportunity, retirement, movement, disclosure, or refraining from protective action.
Element 5 - Injustice: The promise becomes binding when enforcement is required to prevent injustice. Comment b directs attention to the reasonableness of reliance, its definite and substantial character in relation to requested relief, the promise's formality, the evidentiary and cautionary functions served by the setting, bargain-enforcement policies, and prevention of unjust enrichment.
Element 6 - Remedy: Subsection (1) states that relief may be limited as justice requires. The court can protect expenditures, opportunity costs, benefits conferred, or the promised performance. The same factors that establish injustice guide the character and amount of relief.
Subsection (2): A charitable subscription or marriage settlement can become binding under Section 90 without proof that the promise actually induced action or forbearance. The subsection recognizes the form, social setting, and institutional policies surrounding these promises while preserving the Section 90 framework.

== Issue ==
When does reliance make a promise binding despite the absence of a bargained-for exchange, and how much relief does Section 90 authorize?

== Holding ==
A promise is binding when the promisor should reasonably expect it to induce action or forbearance by the promisee or a third person, it actually produces that response, and enforcement is necessary to prevent injustice. The court may limit the remedy to the extent justice requires. Charitable subscriptions and marriage settlements receive enforcement under
Subsection (2) without proof of actual induced reliance.

== Reasoning ==
Promise quality: Section 90 begins with a commitment. The promise must supply enough content to identify the reliance it foreseeably invites and the relief connected to breach. Courts frequently express this requirement as a clear and definite promise. Context can give operational meaning to terms that would appear open-ended in isolation.
Objective foresight: The rule allocates reliance risk according to what the promisor should reasonably expect. Known vulnerability, stated plans, repeated assurances, the seriousness of the setting, and the promisor's involvement in the decision can make action foreseeable. D & G Stout shows that the promisor may need to foresee the general kind of reliance rather than every detail of the opportunity surrendered.
Inducement and causation: The claimant must connect the promise to the change of position. Feinberg separated two periods: the pension did not cause her to continue working, while it did cause her later retirement. D & G Stout's remand record similarly separated the July 9 commitment that affected General's decision from a July 23 call occurring after Stout had decided.
Reasonableness: The promisee's knowledge, experience, access to information, warnings, conditional language, relationship history, customary practice, and available protective steps all bear on reliance. A sophisticated commercial actor can reasonably rely on an informal assurance when the industry and a long relationship give words practical force.
Definite and substantial character: The Second Restatement removed these words from the rule's first sentence while retaining them as injustice factors. Their present function relates the reliance to the remedy. A small or reversible change can support correspondingly narrow relief; retirement, relocation, disclosure of confidential information, or surrender of a priced business opportunity can support substantial relief.
Formality: Writing, deliberation, board action, repeated confirmation, signatures, professional setting, and internal authorization supply evidence of commitment and encourage caution before promising. Ricketts's signed note,
Pfeiffer's formal board resolution, and Bacardi's repeated business assurances each perform some of these functions.
Injustice as policy judgment: Courts consider the strength of proof, reasonableness and magnitude of reliance, availability of other remedies, promisor fault, promisee conduct, public policy, and enrichment. Cohen II classified this element as a legal question and considered journalistic custom, editorial need, constitutional values, and economic harm.
Remedial spectrum: Expectation relief gives the value of promised performance. Reliance relief restores losses caused by changing position. Restitution restores benefits conferred on the promisor. Specific relief orders promised conduct when money cannot adequately address the reliance. Section 90 allows the court to select and limit among these forms.
Ceiling and causation: Comment d cautions against placing the promisee in a better position than performance would have produced when the promisor received no unjust enrichment. The remedy also excludes losses that the promise did not cause. The D & G Stout remand court used both principles when it excluded Fort Wayne property costs General planned to incur under the promised course.
Full enforcement: Enforcing the promise according to its terms can be the proportionate remedy when reliance reorganizes a long period of life or when installment performance directly addresses the induced risk. Ricketts enforced the note, and Feinberg enforced the promised pension amount.
Reliance-limited enforcement: Opportunity costs and expenditures can define relief when the promise itself gives no determinate future expectancy. D & G Stout allowed the lost sale differential and excluded future Bacardi profits.
Cohen awarded economic losses caused by disclosure rather than a hypothetical price for permanent secrecy.
Interaction with bargain doctrine: Begin with Restatement Section 71. If the promisor sought the return act or promise in exchange, ordinary consideration supports the commitment. If the act followed as induced reliance rather than bargained price, Section 90 supplies the separate analysis. Ricketts's wish that Scothorn enjoy independence generated foreseeable reliance while requesting no return promise.
Interaction with restitution: Section 90 can consider prevention of unjust enrichment within injustice and remedy. A standalone restitution claim still requires a benefit conferred on the defendant. Theories should remain separately measured so a claimant recovers once for the same economic interest.
Integrated case map: Ricketts shows foreseeable resignation and full enforcement through equitable-estoppel language. Feinberg shows retirement induced by a formal pension resolution. Cohen shows confidentiality reliance, public-policy analysis, and a generally applicable law applied to the press. D & G Stout shows commercial reliance on an at-will assurance and careful limitation to a forgone opportunity.
Exam sequence: (1) Identify the exact promise and speaker authority. (2) State the reliance the promisor should reasonably foresee. (3) Prove what action or forbearance actually occurred because of the promise. (4) Evaluate reasonableness, magnitude, formality, alternative remedies, policy, and enrichment. (5) Explain why relief is required to prevent injustice. (6) measure expectation, reliance, and restitution separately. (7) Select the remedy that addresses the proven injustice without duplicate recovery.
