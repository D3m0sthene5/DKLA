Riggs v. Palmer
115 N.Y. 506, 22 N.E. 188 (1889). New York Court of Appeals. Earl, J. Submitted June 21, 1889; decided October 8, 1889. Review of judgments entered on a referee's report and affirmed at General Term.
Source: added-sources/Legislation_Regulatory_State_Class3_Case_Briefs.pdf, pages 1–2

== Parties ==
Plaintiffs / appellants: Philo Riggs, as guardian ad litem, and Francis B. Palmer's two daughters, Mrs. Riggs and Mrs.
Preston. They sought to prevent Elmer from receiving the estate and to have the property pass to them. Defendants / respondents: Elmer E. Palmer, Francis's grandson and residuary legatee, together with the estate administrator. Elmer claimed the property under Francis's formally valid and probated will.

== Procedural History ==
Elmer was tried and convicted of second-degree murder for poisoning Francis and was serving his sentence in a state reformatory when the civil action began. Francis's daughters sued to cancel and annul the provisions favoring Elmer. A referee found that Elmer intentionally murdered Francis to prevent revocation of the will and accelerate his inheritance, yet concluded that the will statutes required dismissal of the complaint. Judgment was entered on the referee's report, and the General Term affirmed.
The daughters appealed to the New York Court of Appeals. The court accepted the facts already found and reversed the
General Term and referee judgments. It entered judgment enjoining Elmer and the administrator from using the estate for Elmer's benefit, declaring the devise and bequest to Elmer ineffective, and declaring the daughters the owners of the estate subject to the support charges created for Elmer's mother and Francis's widow.

== Material Facts ==
On August 13, 1880, Francis executed a will that gave small legacies to his daughters and the residue of his farm and considerable personal property to Elmer, subject to support for Elmer's mother. The will provided a gift over to the daughters if Elmer survived Francis and then died under age, unmarried, and without issue. Francis later married Mrs.
Bresee and signed an ante-nuptial agreement charging her lifetime support against the farm.
Elmer lived with Francis as a family member. At age sixteen, Elmer knew the will favored him and knew that Francis had manifested an intention to revoke that benefit. Elmer willfully poisoned Francis to prevent revocation, cause the will to become operative, and obtain immediate possession of the property. The governing will and inheritance statutes contained no express provision disqualifying a beneficiary who murdered the testator. A literal application of those statutes would transfer the residue to Elmer.

== Issue ==
May a beneficiary acquire property under a formally valid and probated will after intentionally murdering the testator to prevent revocation and accelerate the inheritance, when the governing statutes contain no express slayer provision?

== Holding ==
Substantive holding: Elmer was barred from acquiring any interest in Francis's estate. Statutes governing wills and devolution operate together with the common-law maxim that no person may obtain property through his own crime.
Procedural disposition: The Court of Appeals reversed the judgments below and entered judgment for the daughters on the facts already found.

== Reasoning ==
Source of law and competing positions: The court interpreted New York's statutes governing the making, probate, effect, alteration, and revocation of wills against general common-law maxims and the statutes' purposes. Elmer relied on the enacted text: Francis was dead, the will had been made in the prescribed form and admitted to probate, and the statutes literally directed the property to him. The daughters relied on the will statutes' purpose and the settled principle that a claimant cannot found a property right on his own deliberate wrong.
The majority treated legislative intention as part of the statute's legal meaning. The will statutes aimed to carry out a testator's legally expressed wishes and secure the orderly, peaceable, and just devolution of property. A legislature pursuing those ends would exclude a beneficiary who killed the testator to activate the will. The general words therefore did not govern this unforeseen collateral consequence.
The court called this method rational or equitable interpretation. General language may be restrained when its application would produce a consequence contrary to the enactment's reason. The opinion's examples supplied the method: a statute authorizing a man to try causes in his manor excludes his own case, and a law punishing anyone who draws blood in the street excludes a barber performing a medical procedure. In each example, purpose determines the sensible reach of general words.
A second and independent source of law reinforced that construction. Common-law maxims forbid a person to profit by fraud, take advantage of his own wrong, base a claim on iniquity, or acquire property by crime. The majority regarded those maxims as background law that controls statutes and wills unless legislation supersedes them. Prior law had applied the same principle to deny life-insurance proceeds to a beneficiary who murdered the insured and to invalidate testamentary provisions produced by fraud, undue influence, immorality, or public-policy violations.
The causal connection between Elmer's crime and the claimed property made the maxim decisive. Elmer might have died first, and Francis might have revoked the gift. By killing Francis, Elmer made the will speak at the chosen time and converted a contingent expectation into an immediate claim. The judgment prevented that acquisition. The majority characterized the result as a rule governing title rather than an additional criminal punishment because Elmer never possessed the estate before the murder.
Justice Gray's dissent: Gray treated testamentary succession as a field comprehensively controlled by statute. The enacted provisions specified the exclusive methods for altering or revoking a will, and Francis completed none of them.
For Gray, equitable and natural-justice principles supplied reasons for the legislature to enact a slayer rule; they supplied no judicial power to alter the statutory disposition. He also viewed disinheritance as an added penalty after the criminal law had imposed the punishment authorized for murder and warned that the court was effectively making a different will for Francis.
Narrow holding and significance: A legatee who intentionally kills the testator to activate the gift cannot acquire the resulting property. The case presents a direct conflict between a textually specified outcome and the legal force of statutory purpose, absurd-consequence reasoning, and common-law principles that the legislature left intact.
1L explanation: The majority and dissent disagree about the law available to a court. Earl reads the statute together with its purpose and inherited common-law maxims; that combined law excludes Elmer's claim. Gray reads the detailed will statute as the complete decision rule; the valid, unrevoked will therefore controls until the legislature creates a slayer rule. Their dispute concerns judicial authority and statutory meaning, rather than disagreement over Elmer's conduct or moral desert.
