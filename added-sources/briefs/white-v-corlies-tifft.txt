White v. Corlies & Tifft
46 N.Y. 467 (1871). Court of Appeals of New York. Folger, J. Decided November 20, 1871. Also reported as White v. Corlies; the supplied casebook spells Tift with one final f.
Source: added-sources/Contracts_Offer_and_Acceptance_Briefs.pdf, pages 8–9

== Preamble ==
The New York Court of Appeals held that White's private decision to accept a proposed office-fitting job, followed by buying and working on general-purpose materials, did not sufficiently manifest acceptance to the merchants who proposed the job. The trial judge had incorrectly instructed the jury that White could bind the merchants by acting on their note and beginning the job without indicating assent to them. The
Court of Appeals reversed the judgment for White and ordered a new trial, with costs to depend on the eventual outcome. White was the builder, plaintiff, and respondent on appeal; Corlies and his associates were the merchants, defendants, and appellants. 46 N.Y. at 467-70.
White operated his building business on Fortieth Street in New York City. The merchants conducted business on Dey Street and wanted a suite of offices at 57 Broadway fitted up. In September 1865, they gave
White specifications and asked him to estimate the cost. He delivered his estimate on September 28, after which the merchants were to consider it and communicate their decision. They changed the specifications that day and sent the revised version to White to obtain his assent under the estimate. White signed his assent to the changes and returned the specifications. That preliminary agreement about specifications formed part of the negotiations; the Court of Appeals found no completed agreement before the later note.
The next day, the merchants' bookkeeper sent White a note dated September 29. It stated that, upon an agreement to finish fitting up the offices within two weeks from that date, he could begin at once. The writer also said he would probably call again between five and six that afternoon. White did not reply. Immediately after receiving the note, he bought lumber and began working on it. The merchants countermanded the proposal the following day. White sued for damages on the theory that his conduct had accepted the undertaking before the countermand arrived.
The supplied casebook adds details from White's testimony in the record on appeal. The merchants had initially wanted the offices finished in black walnut within twenty-one days; White said he could not do that hardwood work within that period. They then requested an estimate for white pine. White's September 28 estimate did not specify a completion time. The subsequent countermand requested another estimate for black walnut. Those record details help explain why agreement about specifications and price did not necessarily settle the entire undertaking. They supplement the published opinion and are identified here as casebook reporting of the appellate record. The opinion itself refers once to a September 30 communication;
the casebook notes that the court apparently meant the September 29 note.
At trial, the judge told the jury that White did not have to return to the merchants and state that he had begun, and that he could act on the note and commence the job. The defendants took an exception to that instruction, preserving the claimed legal error. The jury found for White. The General Term of the First
Judicial District affirmed the trial judgment, and the defendants appealed to the Court of Appeals. The issue on that appeal was whether the instruction treated White's decision and preparations as sufficient acceptance when his conduct had not communicated assent or been placed in an appropriate course of communication.
The disposition was a new trial, rather than a final appellate award to either party. 46 N.Y. at 467-70.
The court first treated the merchants' note as a proposal requiring White's acceptance. It found no earlier contract in the evidence or the jury's findings. The court then stated that an offer made when the parties are apart requires acceptance through an appropriate outward act. The act need not always have reached the offeror's actual knowledge before a contract forms. It must, however, be placed in a proper course of communication so that it will ordinarily reach the offeror within a reasonable time. A mailed acceptance illustrates the distinction: dispatch may complete the contract even before delivery because the offeree has taken an objectively communicative step. 46 N.Y. at 469.
White's conduct failed that test. The lumber he purchased was suitable for other comparable jobs, and the work he performed on it resembled the work he would do for those jobs. His acts therefore did not identify this proposed undertaking as the one he had accepted. His private intention could explain the acts to himself, but it did not give the merchants an outward indication of assent. The court found nothing in his conduct that communicated acceptance, set such communication in motion, or would necessarily result in communication to the merchants. His expenditures and labor thus established preparation without establishing the acceptance required on this record. 46 N.Y. at 469-70.
White's strongest argument arose from the note's permission to begin immediately: a builder could reasonably understand an instruction to start as inviting responsive conduct. The court nevertheless examined what White actually did and how those acts would manifest assent. It did not establish a rule that conduct can never accept an offer. Nor did it hold that actual receipt of notice is always essential, since its mailed-letter example expressly recognized formation before receipt. Its conclusion was that these general preparations, accompanied by an uncommunicated intention, were insufficient under this proposal.
The casebook identifies a genuine interpretive question concerning the note's opening phrase. It could require White to agree to the newly stated completion period before starting, or it could refer to the proposed work while authorizing commencement. The Court of Appeals treated the note as an offer but did not develop every possible reading of that phrase. Work at the specified offices, an unequivocal return message, or other conduct objectively directed to this job could present a different acceptance question. Those are analytical variations; the court did not decide them. The brief's holding should remain tied to the generic lumber and uncommunicated work in the actual record.
Under the later Restatement framework, the initial inquiry would be whether the offer invited a promise, performance, or either. Section 62 may make the beginning of invited performance an acceptance carrying a promise to complete; § 45 may protect an offeree who begins performance when only completed performance is invited as acceptance. Both inquiries require identifying the work actually requested and distinguishing its beginning from preparation. Sections 54 and 56 separately address notice. These later provisions help organize a modern analysis of similar facts, but the 1871 court did not apply them.
The decision supports an objective account of assent: an act must obtain its contractual meaning from the communication and circumstances, and a hidden intention cannot supply the missing outward meaning. The justices concurred in reversal, with Justice Allen not voting. The opinion is available at White v. Corlies, 46
