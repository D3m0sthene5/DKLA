Oglebay Norton Co. v. Armco, Inc.
Oglebay Norton Co. v. Armco, Inc., 52 Ohio St. 3d 232, 556 N.E.2d 515 (1990). Supreme Court of Ohio, decided July 11, 1990. The opinion was issued per curiam, meaning by the court as a whole without a named author, and Chief Justice Moyer and Justices Sweeney, Holmes, Douglas, Wright, H. Brown, and Resnick concurred. The case concerns whether a court may enforce a long-term contract after both of its pricing mechanisms fail, by setting a reasonable price and ordering the parties to negotiate and mediate future prices.

== Parties ==
Oglebay Norton Company, which operated a fleet of Great Lakes bulk vessels, was the carrier and the plaintiff. Armco, Inc., a steel producer, was the shipper and the defendant. Oglebay sought a declaration that the contract remained in force and a determination of the rate; Armco contended that the contract had become unenforceable because its pricing mechanisms had failed.

== Material Facts ==
Under a 1957 long-term contract, Oglebay agreed to maintain vessel capacity sufficient to carry up to 7.1 million gross tons of Armco's iron ore per year on the Great Lakes, and Armco agreed to use that capacity.
Later amendments extended the contract through 2010, and a 1962 amendment gave Armco a contractual right to seek specific performance of Oglebay's obligations. The parties had a long and close relationship that included joint ventures, interlocking directorates, and Armco's ownership of Oglebay stock. 52 Ohio St. 3d at
233-35.
The contract set the rate through two mechanisms. The primary mechanism looked to the rate recognized by leading iron ore shippers, which in practice was the published rate for a leading shipper in Skillings Mining
Review. If no such rate existed, a secondary mechanism required the parties to agree on a rate taking into consideration the rates charged by leading independent vessel operators. The mechanism worked for decades, but in the mid-1980s, as the steel industry declined, it failed. For 1985 the published rate was $7.41 per gross ton, and Armco paid only $5.00. After 1985 the journal stopped publishing the rate, and information about independent carriers' rates could not be obtained. The parties could not agree on a rate, and Armco took the position that the contract was no longer enforceable. Id. at 234-35.

== Procedural History ==
Oglebay sought declaratory relief in the Court of Common Pleas. The trial court found that the parties intended to be bound despite the failure of the pricing mechanisms. Relying on the parties' course of dealing, the detriment each would suffer, and market comparisons reflecting the depressed state of the steel industry, it set a reasonable rate of $6.25 per gross ton for the 1986 season. It also ordered the parties to negotiate a rate for each shipping season through 2010 and, if negotiations failed, to mediate, and it retained continuing jurisdiction. The court of appeals affirmed, and the Supreme Court of Ohio heard Armco's appeal. Id. at
235-36.

== Issue ==
When both pricing mechanisms in a long-term contract fail, did the parties still intend to be bound? If so, may the court determine a reasonable price, and may it exercise continuing equitable jurisdiction by ordering the parties to negotiate future rates and, failing agreement, to mediate?

== Holding ==
Whether the parties intended to be bound despite the failure of their pricing mechanisms is a question of fact, and competent, credible evidence supported the trial court's finding that they did. When the parties intended to conclude a contract but the price is not settled, a court may determine a reasonable price, as Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 33 and its comments and UCC § 2-305(1), adopted in Ohio as R.C. 1302.18(A), recognize. The $6.25 rate fell within the range of reasonable rates the evidence established. Because damages for breach of a contract of this length in a volatile market would be speculative, the trial court could order the equivalent of specific performance and require the parties to negotiate and, if necessary, mediate future rates.
Id. at 235-38.

== Judgment ==
The Supreme Court of Ohio affirmed the judgment of the court of appeals.

== Reasoning ==
On intent, the court deferred to the trial court's finding, which rested on the parties' long relationship, their course of dealing, and the importance of the contract to both. Armco needed dedicated capacity and Oglebay had maintained its fleet to supply it. Nothing in the record showed that the parties intended the entire arrangement to end if the published rate disappeared. Id. at 235.
On the rate, the court relied on Restatement § 33. Comment e recognizes that parties who intend to conclude a contract may leave the price unsettled, and a court may then supply a reasonable price; comment a notes that the parties' actions may show conclusively that they intended a binding agreement even though terms are missing. UCC § 2-305(1) states the same principle for sales of goods. Ohio precedent was consistent:
Normandy Place Associates v. Beyer enforced an agreement where the parties manifested intent to be bound and the terms were sufficiently definite, and Litsinger Sign Co. v. American Sign Co. held that a court should not frustrate the parties' intent to be bound if it is reasonably possible to fill gaps. Evidence of other carriers' rates, including $7.41 paid by another steel producer and $5.90 quoted by another carrier for 1986, placed the
$6.25 rate within a reasonable range. Id. at 236-37.
On the remedy, the court applied Restatement § 362, which permits specific performance when the terms are sufficiently certain to provide a basis for an appropriate order, allowing the court to use the usual aids, including usage and terms supplied by law. Damages were an inadequate remedy because the dramatic changes in Great Lakes shipping rates and the length of the contract would make it impossible to calculate
Oglebay's damages accurately. The parties' 1962 amendment had itself contemplated specific performance.
Ordering negotiation and, if it broke down, mediation neither added to nor detracted from the parties' obligations; it provided a practical means of carrying out a contract the parties had intended to last until 2010.
Id. at 237-38.

== Separate Opinions ==
The opinion was per curiam, and all seven justices concurred. There is no separate opinion.

== Notes ==
Oglebay Norton is the modern counterpart to Sun Printing. In both, the parties left price to an external standard and to future agreement, and the standard failed. Cardozo treated the open terms as proof that no contract existed for the unpriced period; the Ohio court treated the parties' intent to be bound as controlling and supplied the missing term. The difference reflects the Code and Restatement principles in § 2-305 and §
33, the length and importance of the relationship, and the parties' decades of performance, which made intent to be bound much easier to find.
Two points about scope are important. The contract was for transportation services, so the court applied §
2-305 by analogy rather than directly. And the mediation remedy is unusual: courts rarely supervise ongoing negotiations, and the court justified doing so by the parties' unique, long-lasting relationship, the inadequacy of damages, and the parties' own provision for specific performance. The case also illustrates why §
2-305(1)(c) addresses the failure of an agreed market standard: when the index disappears, the question becomes whether the parties would have wanted the contract to continue at a reasonable price, and a long relational contract makes that answer more likely to be yes.
