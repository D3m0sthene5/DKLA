The Bremen v. Zapata Off-Shore Co.
The Bremen v. Zapata Off-Shore Co., 407 U.S. 1 (1972). United States Supreme Court, decided June 12,
1972. Chief Justice Burger delivered the opinion of the Court. Justice White filed a short concurrence, and
Justice Douglas dissented. The dispute arose in federal admiralty and concerned enforcement of a contractual forum-selection clause.

== Parties ==
Zapata Off-Shore Company, a Houston-based American corporation, owned the drilling rig Chaparral. It was plaintiff in the American damages action and respondent in the Supreme Court. Unterweser Reederei, a
German corporation, agreed to tow the rig with its deep-sea tug Bremen. Unterweser and the vessel were defendants below and petitioners seeking enforcement of the London forum clause. The American complaint asserted personal liability against Unterweser and proceeded in rem against the tug itself.

== Material Facts ==
In November 1967, Zapata accepted Unterweser's bid to tow the Chaparral from Louisiana to a location off
Ravenna, Italy. Zapata reviewed the proposed contract, made several changes, and signed it without altering the forum clause or two provisions purporting to relieve Unterweser of responsibility for certain damage to the rig. The forum clause required that any dispute arising be treated before the London Court of Justice, understood as the English High Court of Justice.
The Bremen departed Louisiana on January 5, 1968. Four days later, a storm in international waters in the
Gulf of Mexico caused the rig's raised legs to break off, seriously damaging it. At Zapata's direction, the tug took the rig to Tampa, the nearest port of refuge. The location of the emergency landing therefore supplied the immediate American connection to the ensuing litigation. The parties had contemplated a voyage through many jurisdictions and had selected London as a neutral, experienced maritime forum before the accident. 407 U.S. at 2-4.

== Procedural History ==
On January 12, Zapata filed an admiralty action in the federal district court in Tampa seeking $3.5 million for negligent towage and breach of contract. The vessel was arrested and later released after security was furnished. Unterweser moved to dismiss for lack of jurisdiction or forum non conveniens, or alternatively to stay the action pending litigation in London. In February it brought a contract action against Zapata in the
English High Court. Zapata specially appeared there to challenge jurisdiction, but the English courts enforced the forum clause.
The American court had not ruled on Unterweser's motion when the six-month deadline for seeking limitation of maritime liability approached. To preserve that protection, Unterweser filed a limitation action in Tampa on July 2, 1968. The court issued the customary injunction against proceedings elsewhere, and
Zapata filed its damages claim in the limitation proceeding. Later that month, the district court denied the original motion to dismiss or stay. Relying on circuit precedent hostile to forum clauses, it applied ordinary forum non conveniens balancing and placed the burden on Unterweser to show that the balance strongly favored another forum.
In January 1969, the district court also denied a stay of the limitation proceeding and restrained Unterweser from continuing the English action. It reasoned that Unterweser had invoked the American court's equitable authority by filing for limitation and should litigate the entire controversy there. A divided Fifth Circuit panel affirmed. On rehearing en banc, the court adopted the panel decision, with six of fourteen judges dissenting, 428 F.2d 888 and 446 F.2d 907. The Supreme Court granted certiorari amid disagreement over the force of forum-selection clauses. The challenged orders concerned where the dispute would be decided, rather than a completed determination of negligent towage. 407 U.S. at 4-8.

== Issue ==
Should a federal admiralty court enforce a freely negotiated international agreement requiring litigation in
London, or may it disregard the clause because the American forum is convenient and foreign law might enforce liability limitations disfavored domestically? Did Unterweser surrender the clause by filing a protective limitation action in the United States?

== Holding ==
A forum-selection clause in this setting is prima facie valid and should be enforced unless the resisting party clearly shows that enforcement would be unreasonable and unjust, that the clause resulted from fraud or overreaching, or that enforcement would contravene a strong public policy of the forum. Foreseeable inconvenience is insufficient; the resisting party must show that litigation in the chosen forum would be so gravely difficult that it would effectively lose its day in court. Zapata bore that heavy burden. Unterweser's protective limitation filing did not waive reliance on the clause. 407 U.S. at 10-20.

== Judgment ==
The Court vacated the Fifth Circuit's judgment and remanded for reconsideration under the proper standard and burden. It strongly indicated that the existing record did not justify refusing enforcement, but allowed
Zapata an opportunity to make the required showing. It did not decide the negligence claim, enforce the exculpatory clauses on the merits, or itself enter a damages judgment.

== Reasoning ==
The Court rejected the traditional idea that enforcing a forum clause improperly ousts a court of jurisdiction.
The parties' agreement did not eliminate the American court's legal power. It shaped whether the court should exercise that power beyond giving effect to the agreed forum. Jurisdiction to hear the controversy and enforcement of a contractual choice of forum were therefore distinct questions.
The commercial setting made the agreement especially important. Two sophisticated businesses from different countries had arranged a costly international tow. An accident could occur along a long route, and the vessel might seek refuge in many ports. An agreed forum reduced the uncertainty of litigation in whichever place happened to become available after an accident. London was neutral between the parties and possessed substantial maritime expertise. The Court regarded the clause as part of the allocation of risk and cost embodied in the bargain.
The lower courts' ordinary convenience analysis put the burden on the wrong party. A plaintiff who agreed beforehand to litigate in London could not defeat that promise simply by showing that Tampa was now preferable. Zapata had to establish a strong reason for setting the clause aside. The district court's conclusions about Gulf Coast witnesses and convenience did not establish effective deprivation of a meaningful opportunity to litigate, especially given the availability of deposition evidence.
The possibility that English law would enforce exculpatory provisions did not establish a controlling
American public-policy objection. The Court distinguished Bisso's policy concerning towage in American waters from this negotiated international undertaking. It preserved the general principle that a strong policy declared by statute or judicial decision could defeat a forum clause. It did not hold every contractual choice valid whenever parties are sophisticated, nor did it decide that every possible liability waiver in the contract was enforceable.
Finally, Unterweser's limitation action was defensive. The statutory deadline was about to expire while its pending request to enforce the clause remained undecided. Filing preserved a protection that might otherwise be lost if the American litigation continued. Treating that necessary precaution as abandonment of the
London clause would allow Zapata's own refusal to honor the agreement to force the loss of Unterweser's rights. The clause's mandatory and comprehensive wording also covered the in rem claim against the tug.
407 U.S. at 12-20.

== Separate Opinions ==
Justice White joined the opinion and judgment except for comments assessing matters left for remand; he preferred that the district court consider those matters first. Justice Douglas dissented. He believed
Unterweser's invocation of the limitation court subjected it to that court's full equitable authority, including an injunction against the English action. He also regarded the forum clause as a means of obtaining enforcement of exculpatory terms contrary to American maritime policy and emphasized the practical advantages of the Tampa forum. He would have affirmed. 407 U.S. at 20-24.

== Notes ==
The holding arose in federal admiralty and a negotiated international commercial agreement. Carnival
Cruise later considers how the rationale applies to a nonnegotiated passenger ticket. A forum-selection clause identifies where litigation must occur; it is analytically distinct from a clause selecting governing law or limiting liability, although those provisions can interact. The remand here followed use of the wrong enforcement standard, rather than a finding that the American court lacked subject-matter jurisdiction.
