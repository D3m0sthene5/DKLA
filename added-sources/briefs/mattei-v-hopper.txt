Mattei v. Hopper
51 Cal. 2d 119, 330 P.2d 625 (1958). Supreme Court of California, in bank. Spence, J. S.F. No. 19806; decided October 24, 1958. Appeal from a judgment entered after a bench trial.
Source: added-sources/Contracts_Consideration_Interpretation_Big_Brief.pdf, pages 1–2

== Parties ==
Plaintiff / appellant: Peter O. Mattei, a real-estate developer assembling land for a shopping center. He signed the buyer's side of the deposit receipt, paid the deposit, pursued leases, and sought damages after the conveyance failed.
Defendant / respondent: Amelia F. Hopper, owner of the adjacent parcel. She offered to sell the property, later repudiated through counsel, and obtained judgment at trial. Relevant third party: Coldwell Banker & Company, the real-estate intermediary charged with obtaining leases satisfactory to Mattei.

== Procedural History ==
Mattei filed an action for damages alleging that Hopper breached the parties' written land-sale agreement by failing to convey the parcel. The case was tried to the court without a jury. The trial court treated Mattei's satisfaction clause as leaving performance within his unrestricted choice, characterized the agreement as illusory and lacking mutuality, and entered judgment for Hopper.
Mattei appealed. The California Supreme Court reviewed the written instrument's legal effect and the consideration question. It held that the deposit receipt created a contract, that good faith governed Mattei's judgment about the leases, and that this enforceable obligation supplied consideration. The court reversed the judgment. Chief Justice Gibson and
Justices Shenk, Carter, Traynor, and Schauer joined Justice Spence. Justice McComb dissented without an opinion.

== Material Facts ==
Mattei planned a shopping center on a tract beside Hopper's land. A real-estate agent negotiated with Hopper for several months. Hopper rejected several proposals because the offered price was inadequate. She then submitted an offer, and Mattei accepted it the same day. Their writing used the agent's deposit-receipt form.
The stated purchase price was $57,500. Mattei had to deposit $1,000 with the agent. The agreement gave him 120 days to examine title and consummate the transaction. At the end of that period, the balance became payable when Hopper tendered a good and sufficient deed. The concluding provision stated that the transaction was subject to Coldwell
Banker obtaining leases satisfactory to the purchaser.
The lease provision served the shopping-center project. Mattei wanted time to secure tenant commitments before paying the balance and taking title. The commercial value of the assembled site depended on lease features that included term length, renewal rights, covenants, restrictions, rent, tenant creditworthiness, and the nature of each tenant's business.
Mattei paid the $1,000 deposit and worked to obtain leases. Before the 120-day period expired, Hopper's attorney informed him that Hopper would decline to sell on the deposit receipt's terms. Hopper later received notice that satisfactory leases had been obtained and that Mattei offered the unpaid purchase price. Hopper withheld the deed required by the writing.

== Issue ==
Two connected questions: (1) Did the signed deposit receipt create an immediate contract whose performance was conditioned on Mattei's obtaining satisfactory leases? (2) Did the satisfaction provision impose a legally enforceable standard on Mattei's judgment and thereby preserve consideration for Hopper's conveyance promise?

== Holding ==
Contract formation: The deposit receipt created a binding contract when the parties signed it. The lease clause made
Mattei's duty to complete the purchase conditional on obtaining leases satisfactory to him and gave him a contractual power to terminate if that condition failed. Consideration: Mattei had to evaluate the leases through honest, good-faith judgment. That legal duty restricted his discretion and made his promise sufficient consideration. Disposition:
Judgment for Hopper reversed.

== Reasoning ==
Interpretation of 'subject to': The court read the entire deposit receipt as one transaction. The writing stated the property, price, deposit, performance period, deed tender, and lease condition. This structure formed a present agreement and located the satisfaction event within performance. The clause therefore allocated leasing risk to Mattei during the 120-day period.
Consideration and mutuality: In a bilateral contract, each exchanged promise must impose a legal obligation. A promise becomes illusory when its maker retains unrestricted freedom to perform or withdraw. The terms 'illusory promise' and 'lack of mutuality' identify the same formation inquiry: whether the exchange contains consideration.
Modern doctrine states the point through Restatement (Second) Section 79: once consideration exists, mutuality supplies no additional requirement.
Two satisfaction standards: A satisfaction condition concerning commercial value, quality, operative fitness, or mechanical utility ordinarily uses an objective reasonable-person standard. A condition concerning taste, fancy, or judgment uses the promisor's actual judgment, governed by honesty and good faith. The selected standard comes from the subject matter the parties assigned to the promisor.
Classification of the leases: Shopping-center leases required evaluation of many interacting factors: duration, renewal options, covenants, restrictions, rent, financial responsibility, and business mix. Those considerations called for business judgment. Mattei therefore possessed subjective evaluative authority coupled with a duty to exercise that authority honestly and in good faith. Genuine dissatisfaction with the proposed leases could excuse his purchase performance; a strategic or fabricated claim of dissatisfaction would breach the agreement.
Why the good-faith duty supplies consideration: Mattei promised to accept leases that satisfied his honest judgment and to complete the purchase when that condition occurred. Hopper could challenge a refusal by proving that Mattei's stated dissatisfaction lacked genuineness. That exposure to judicial review created a real legal detriment and supported the reciprocal conveyance promise.
Third-party performance: The leases came from prospective shopping-center tenants; Hopper's promised performance was conveyance of the land. The court applied the same good-faith standard because the contract made those leases the condition by which Mattei's performance would be measured. Mattei's evaluative duty retained the same content when applied to third-party leases.
Doctrinal history: Earlier mutuality formulations often asked whether a court could compel each side at formation.
Satisfaction cases developed enforceable standards for apparently broad discretion. The First Restatement Section 265 and the Williston and Corbin treatises treated a satisfaction promise as an undertaking to exercise honest judgment.
Mattei used that established approach to connect interpretation, implied good faith, and consideration.
Prior California dicta: Lawrence Block Co. v. Palston described buyer-approval clauses as unrestricted because judgment lacked a reasonable-person measure. Its actual result rested on an unaccepted counteroffer. Pruitt v. Fontana repeated that language, while its result concerned a later oral agreement and estoppel against a statute-of-frauds defense. Mattei disapproved the dicta to the extent they treated objective reasonableness as the only enforceable standard. Honest good faith supplies the standard for judgment-based satisfaction.
Rule and application sequence: Identify the promised performance; decide whether the disputed language creates a formation condition or a performance condition; classify the subject of satisfaction; select objective reasonableness or subjective good faith; then ask whether that standard meaningfully restricts discretion. Here, the signed receipt formed the contract, lease acceptability involved judgment, and good faith bound Mattei.
