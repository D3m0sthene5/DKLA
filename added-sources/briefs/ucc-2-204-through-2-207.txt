UCC §§ 2-204 through 2-207

Source: added-sources/Contracts_Formation_Reliance_and_UCC_Briefs.pdf, pages 8–10

== Sources ==
The provisions work in sequence. Section 2-204 asks whether the parties formed a sales contract. Section
2-205 protects certain offers against revocation. Section 2-206 identifies permissible methods of acceptance.
Section 2-207 addresses formation and terms when an acceptance or confirmation does not match the offer or earlier agreement. The section numbers should remain distinct: an irrevocable offer, an accepted offer, and an incorporated additional term are three different legal conclusions. § 2-204; § 2-205; § 2-206; § 2-207.
Section 2-204: Formation in General
A sales contract can arise in any manner sufficient to show agreement, including conduct by both parties recognizing a contract. The precise instant of formation need not be identified. Open terms do not defeat formation if the parties intended a contract and there is a reasonably certain basis for an appropriate remedy.
Commercial conduct and objective intention therefore matter even when the parties' paperwork is incomplete.
This flexibility does not dispense with agreement. Negotiations that reserve commitment for later approval may remain preliminary. Nor does the Code supply every missing essential automatically. An open price can often be supplied under § 2-305, but the absence of an ascertainable quantity is a more serious problem unless a recognized arrangement, such as a requirements or output contract, supplies the quantity measure.
Formation also should be separated from enforceability under the statute of frauds in § 2-201, an independent inquiry where applicable.
For example, if a seller repeatedly ships identified goods and the buyer accepts and pays for them, the parties' conduct may demonstrate a sales contract despite uncertainty about which conversation completed the agreement. If their exchanged forms conflict, § 2-207 supplies the more specific analysis of which terms govern. Section 2-204 establishes flexibility; it does not let a court simply select whichever party's boilerplate seems preferable.
Section 2-205: Firm Offers
A merchant's offer to buy or sell goods is protected against revocation without consideration when a signed writing assures that the offer will be held open. Protection lasts for the stated time, or a reasonable time if none is stated, with a maximum of three months under this statutory mechanism. An assurance appearing on the offeree's form must be separately signed by the offeror. Only the offeror must qualify as a merchant for this provision; the offeree may be a consumer.
The section removes the need for consideration supporting the assurance, not the need for acceptance of the proposed sale. It also does not require the reliance that matters in Drennan. The detailed scope and examples appear in the opening notes. Its three-month limit concerns the particular statutory protection, rather than every option or every outstanding offer involving goods.
Section 2-206: Offer and Acceptance
Unless the offer's language or circumstances unambiguously indicate otherwise, an offer invites acceptance in any reasonable manner and by any reasonable medium. An order for prompt or current shipment can ordinarily be accepted through a prompt promise to ship or through prompt or current shipment. The offeror can specify a narrower method, but an exclusive requirement must be established from the actual language and circumstances.
Shipment of nonconforming goods ordinarily both accepts the order and breaches the resulting contract. The seller cannot automatically avoid a contract by sending the wrong goods. The exception is a seasonable notice that the nonconforming shipment is offered only as an accommodation. With that notice, the shipment functions as a proposed substitute transaction rather than acceptance of the original offer. The buyer can decide whether to accept the proposed accommodation.
Suppose a buyer orders 100 blue chairs for immediate shipment. A prompt promise to send them accepts the offer. A prompt shipment of 100 blue chairs also accepts. A shipment of 100 red chairs without a seasonable accommodation notice ordinarily accepts and breaches. If the seller seasonably explains that the red chairs are offered only as a substitute accommodation, the shipment does not accept the original order. These are formation consequences; the buyer's separate rights concerning rejection, cure, or acceptance of goods depend on other Article 2 provisions.
Where beginning the requested performance is a reasonable method of acceptance, § 2-206(2) protects an offeror who is not notified within a reasonable time: the offeror may treat the offer as having lapsed before acceptance. An offeree therefore should not assume that undisclosed commencement always binds an unaware offeror indefinitely. Also distinguish acceptance of an offer, which forms a contract, from acceptance of delivered goods, which concerns performance under a sales transaction.
Section 2-207: Additional or Different Terms
Subsection (1) first asks whether there is a definite and seasonable expression of acceptance, or a written confirmation sent within a reasonable time. Such a response can operate despite additional or different terms.
This displaces the traditional insistence that the acceptance mirror the offer. The response must still objectively express agreement; a fundamental disagreement over the transaction can show that no definite acceptance occurred.
The proviso in subsection (1) makes an exception where the response expressly conditions acceptance on the offeror's assent to the additional or different terms. Dorton interprets that language strictly: the response must clearly communicate unwillingness to proceed without the offeror's assent. Merely saying the response is subject to the seller's terms did not satisfy that standard there. A material alteration and an expressly conditional acceptance are distinct concepts. The importance of a term does not by itself prove that the seller expressly conditioned acceptance on assent to it.
If a contract arises through subsection (1), subsection (2) addresses additional terms. They begin as proposals. Where the transaction is not between merchants, inclusion ordinarily requires agreement to the proposed addition; the special rule for merchants does not automatically incorporate it. Between merchants, an additional term becomes part of the contract unless the offer expressly limits acceptance to its own terms, the addition materially alters the contract, or timely notice of objection has been given. An objection may already have been communicated or may be made within a reasonable time after notice of the proposed term.
Material alteration generally concerns a change that would produce surprise or hardship if incorporated without express awareness. The parties' practices, communications, and commercial setting can matter.
Dorton required factual findings about the arbitration clause rather than announcing that every arbitration term necessarily is, or is not, material. Failure to object is not enough to incorporate a material alteration under the merchant rule. Conversely, a term excluded from automatic incorporation can still become binding through actual agreement.
If the writings do not form a contract, subsection (3) asks whether both parties nevertheless act as though a contract exists. Shipment and the buyer's acceptance and payment can establish that conduct. The resulting terms are those on which the writings agree, together with supplementary terms supplied under the Code. A seller's unaccepted additional arbitration clause does not enter this contract merely because it appeared in the last form sent. C. Itoh applies this route after a genuinely conditional acknowledgment and subsequent performance without assent to the added clause.
The statute expressly mentions additional and different terms in subsection (1), but its model subsection (2) expressly discusses additional terms. Courts differ in their treatment of different terms where a contract forms under subsection (1). A common approach removes conflicting terms and supplies the applicable
Code rule; other approaches favor the offer's term or analyze differences through subsection (2). Do not present one approach as universal. This interpretive dispute is separate from subsection (3)'s express instruction to retain agreed terms and add the Code's supplementary provisions.

== Application ==
Reconstruct the transaction before reading the boilerplate in isolation. Determine whether an oral agreement already existed, identify the offer and response, and locate the disputed term in each communication. If the response is an effective acceptance under subsection (1), apply subsection (2) to additional terms. If the response is genuinely conditional and the offeror does not assent, ask whether later mutual performance establishes a contract under subsection (3). If neither assent nor qualifying conduct exists, formation has not been established through either route.
This sequence explains the different outcomes of Dorton and C. Itoh. Dorton's acknowledgment was not expressly conditional in the required sense, so the court needed findings about earlier agreement and material alteration. C. Itoh's acknowledgment was expressly conditional, the writings did not form a contract, and subsequent performance supplied one under subsection (3). Receipt and payment proved commitment to the sale without necessarily proving assent to every printed term.
