Restitution as an Alternative Basis for Recovery
Restatement (Second) of Contracts Sections 344(c), 370-376; Restatement (Third) of Restitution and Unjust Enrichment Sections 1 and 31-39. Doctrinal source brief.
Source: added-sources/Contracts_Promissory_Estoppel_Restitution_Big_Brief.pdf, pages 12–13

== Parties ==
Claimant: the person who transferred money, property, services, or another measurable benefit. Recipient: the person whose assets, expenses, or legally protected interests improved through the transfer. Court: the institution that identifies the enrichment, determines why retention would be unjust, respects any valid allocation made by contract, and orders return or payment measured by the recipient's gain.

== Procedural History ==
Restitution developed from legal and equitable actions including money had and received, quantum meruit for the reasonable value of services, quantum valebant for goods, accounting, rescission, and constructive trust. The 1937
Restatement of Restitution organized unjust enrichment as a coherent source of private-law obligation. The 2011
Restatement (Third) of Restitution and Unjust Enrichment updated that body of law and addresses both independent unjust-enrichment claims and restitution connected to contracts.
The Restatement (Second) of Contracts identifies restitution as one of three protected remedial interests. Sections 370 and 371 require a benefit conferred on the other party and measure money relief by the reasonable value of what the recipient obtained or by the increase in the recipient's property or interests. Sections 373 through 376 address restitution after breach, unenforceability, avoidance, and similar contract settings.

== Material Facts ==
Core unjust-enrichment principle: A person who receives enrichment at another's expense under circumstances that make retention unjust bears liability in restitution. The analysis identifies the benefit, traces it to the claimant, and asks whether a legal basis supports the recipient's retention.
Benefit: Enrichment can take the form of money received, property acquired, services accepted, an expense saved, a liability discharged, or an increase in property value. Restatement (Second) Section 370 limits contract restitution to the extent the claimant conferred a benefit on the other party.
Why retention becomes unjust: Common grounds include mistake, fraud, duress, failed basis, avoidance, ineffective agreement, rescission, or receipt of requested performance followed by material breach. A benefit intended as a gift supplies the recipient a legal basis to retain it. A valid contract can also define the parties' rights and displace an inconsistent unjust-enrichment claim.
Measure: Section 371 permits the court to use the reasonable value of what the recipient obtained from a person in the claimant's position or the extent to which the recipient's property and other interests increased. The selected measure follows justice and the type of benefit. Restitution can exceed the claimant's cost when the recipient received greater value, and it can fall below that cost when the recipient's gain was smaller.

== Issue ==
When a claimant cannot establish a bargain or the elements of promissory estoppel, can recovery rest on a benefit the claimant conferred and the defendant unjustly retained?

== Holding ==
Yes, when the defendant received a measurable benefit at the claimant's expense and the circumstances supply no adequate basis for retention. Restitution enforces the duty to restore that enrichment. Recovery tracks the defendant's gain and therefore may differ sharply from the value of the promised performance or the claimant's reliance loss.

== Reasoning ==
Independent basis of obligation: A bargain grounds liability in exchanged assent. Promissory estoppel grounds liability in a commitment that foreseeably induced reliance. Unjust enrichment grounds liability in the recipient's acquisition or retention of a benefit. The claimant should plead and prove each available basis through its own elements.
Contract-connected restitution: A party who performs and then faces the other party's material breach may choose restitution for the value conferred, subject to governing limits. A party who paid or performed under an agreement that becomes voidable or unenforceable may seek restoration when retention lacks a valid basis. The governing agreement continues to control risks and benefits that its terms validly allocate.
Requested services and quantum meruit: When a recipient requests services and knowingly accepts their benefit in circumstances indicating payment, restitution commonly values the services at a reasonable market rate. This route protects the value received even when an attempted contract lacks an enforceable price or fails on formation.
Volunteers and gifts: Restitution respects a recipient's ability to choose whether to purchase a benefit. A person who officiously imposes services ordinarily creates no payment duty. A donor who intentionally completes a gift creates a valid basis for the recipient's enrichment. Mistake, emergency, incapacity, and other recognized circumstances can justify intervention without prior request.
Restitution and reliance measure different things: Reliance reimburses expenditures and opportunity costs caused by the promise. Restitution removes a gain received from the claimant. A single act can create both: requested construction may cost the builder money and increase the owner's property value. The claimant's records should separate cost incurred from value received.
Remedial forms: Personal restitution produces a money judgment. Proprietary restitution can identify a particular asset, fund, or traceable substitute through remedies such as constructive trust or equitable lien when governing rules permit. Rescission unwinds an exchange and restores transferred performance. Disgorgement addresses gains from qualifying wrongful or opportunistic conduct.
Application to Ricketts: Scothorn gave up wages and employment opportunity, yet her grandfather's estate received no corresponding asset or saved expense from her resignation. Her claim therefore fit induced reliance and enforcement of the note more closely than restitution.
Application to Feinberg: Pfeiffer received nearly forty years of compensated service before the pension promise, and
Feinberg testified that the resolution did not induce her next eighteen months of work. Restitution would require identification of an unpaid post-promise benefit attributable to Pfeiffer. Promissory estoppel instead addressed the employment opportunity Feinberg surrendered by retiring.
Application to Cohen: The newspapers received records and a source for a political story. Restitution could ask for the value of that information or the expense saved in obtaining it. Cohen's $200,000 judgment measured job loss and reduced earning capacity caused by breach, which are reliance consequences rather than the publishers' enrichment.
Application to D & G Stout: General surrendered a favorable sale opportunity and lost bargaining leverage. Those losses did not transfer an equivalent gain to Bacardi. The courts therefore measured the difference attributable to the forgone opportunity as reliance damages and excluded costs General would have incurred under the promised course.
Exam sequence: Identify the transfer; identify the recipient and the enrichment; determine whether the claimant intended a gift or acted officiously; locate any contract allocating the benefit or risk; identify the reason retention would be unjust; choose the proper measure and remedy; and prevent duplicative recovery when bargain, reliance, and restitution theories overlap.
Core synthesis: Restitution offers an alternative when the facts show benefit received. Promissory estoppel offers an alternative when the facts show a promise-induced change of position. The first restores unjust gain; the second supplies the relief required by induced reliance.
