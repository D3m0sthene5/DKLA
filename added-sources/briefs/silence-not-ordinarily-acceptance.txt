Silence not ordinarily acceptance
Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 69; casebook printed pages 166-167, PDF pages 216-217.
Source: added-sources/Contracts_Offer_and_Acceptance_Briefs.pdf, pages 10–11

== Preamble ==
Silence acquires contractual significance when the circumstances reasonably give it the meaning of assent.
An unsolicited offer, standing alone, ordinarily leaves the recipient free to do nothing. The offeror cannot impose a duty to reject merely by announcing that silence will count as acceptance. This rule protects the recipient's control over whether to enter a bargain and avoids making every uninvited proposal a demand for action. The assigned discussion uses an offer declaring acceptance automatic after forty-eight hours of silence to illustrate that limit. Restatement § 69; casebook at 166.
Section 69(1)(a) addresses the knowing acceptance of services. An offeree who takes the benefit of offered services, has a reasonable opportunity to reject them, and has reason to know that the provider expects compensation may accept through that conduct. For example, a person who knowingly permits requested paid work to continue for that person's benefit can communicate assent without a spoken promise. The opportunity to reject and the reason to understand that payment is expected are essential. If someone provides an unavoidable benefit without giving the recipient a meaningful choice, those facts require a different analysis; liability cannot be inferred simply from the existence of a benefit.
Section 69(1)(b) concerns an offeror who gives the offeree reason to understand that assent may be expressed through silence or inaction. In this situation, silence operates as acceptance only when the offeree intends to accept. The offer can authorize silence as a convenient way of accepting, but it cannot force an unwilling recipient into a bargain. The offeree's intention therefore has a specified role within this exception.
The requirement differs from a general rule under which an offeror could impose contractual consequences by setting an arbitrary deadline for rejection.
Section 69(1)(c) concerns previous dealings and other circumstances that make it reasonable for the offeree to notify the offeror if the offeree intends to decline. A repeated practice can establish what an unspoken response means between these parties. If a supplier regularly sends goods of an agreed kind and the buyer regularly accepts and pays, the supplier may reasonably understand the same pattern to continue unless the buyer objects. The relevant question is the meaning created by the parties' relationship and current conduct.
A party's undisclosed intention to reject may fail to overcome conduct that objectively communicates acceptance under that established practice.
The casebook's principal illustration is Hobbs v. Massasoit Whip Co., 158 Mass. 194, 33 N.E. 495 (1893).
Hobbs sought $108.50 for 2,350 eel skins sent to a whip manufacturer. He had sent skins in the same manner four or five times before, and the manufacturer had accepted and paid for them. In the disputed transaction, the company retained the skins for months without giving Hobbs notice that it declined them, and the skins were eventually destroyed. Justice Holmes explained that the established dealings and retention could support a jury finding of acceptance. The prior transactions gave the shipment an understood commercial context, and prolonged retention without objection reinforced that meaning. The court overruled the defendant's exceptions, leaving the plaintiff's recovery intact. 158 Mass. at 196-97, 33 N.E. at 495.
The precise significance of Hobbs is evidentiary: those circumstances permitted the jury to infer assent. The decision does not establish that delivery of any unrequested goods automatically produces a contract. The defendant's established relationship with Hobbs and its handling of the shipment supplied the additional facts. The opinion's objective approach also explains why a recipient cannot always avoid liability by asserting that it silently meant to refuse. When conduct reasonably indicates assent in the relationship the parties have created, an undisclosed reservation may carry little weight. The opinion is available at Hobbs,
158 Mass. 194.
The casebook gives a second illustration involving a seller's response to orders. In American Bronze Corp. v.
Streamway Products, 8 Ohio App. 3d 223, 456 N.E.2d 1295, 1300 (1982), the parties had used a practice for more than twenty years under which the buyer telephoned orders, followed with written purchase orders, and the seller began production. When the seller refused three orders, the appellate court reversed and remanded after the buyer's claim had been denied. The established method of filling orders supported the buyer's belief that production had begun absent notice of rejection. The court relied on UCC § 2-204(1), which recognizes formation through conduct demonstrating agreement. This account follows the assigned casebook note; it illustrates how prior practice can supply meaning to a seller's silence as well as a buyer's.
Casebook at 167.
Section 69(2) separately addresses the exercise of ownership over offered property. An offeree who acts inconsistently with the offeror's ownership can become bound on the offered terms unless those terms are manifestly unreasonable. If the act is wrongful against the offeror, it operates as acceptance only if the offeror ratifies it. Using or disposing of offered property may thus carry a contractual consequence, while simple possession requires attention to the circumstances. A court must also consider any statute that changes the common-law result.
For merchandise mailed without the recipient's prior expressed request or consent, federal law provides a specific protection: qualifying unordered merchandise may be retained, used, discarded, or otherwise treated as a gift without an obligation to the sender. That statute prevents a sender from turning an unsolicited mailing into a bill merely because the recipient keeps or uses it. A subscription arrangement the recipient has already accepted presents a different consent question, because a later delivery may occur under that existing agreement. Whether a particular renewal arrangement is lawful also depends on applicable consumer-protection rules. 39 U.S.C. § 3009(a)-(d).
In applying this topic, specify which facts make silence meaningful: knowingly taking paid services, intending to use a permitted silent method of acceptance, an established course of dealings, or exercising ownership over offered goods. Then identify what the recipient could reasonably have understood and whether a governing statute changes the result. That method ties the conclusion to consent and conduct rather than treating silence as having the same meaning in every transaction.
