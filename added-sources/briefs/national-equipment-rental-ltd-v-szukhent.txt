National Equipment Rental, Ltd. v. Szukhent
National Equipment Rental, Ltd. v. Szukhent, 375 U.S. 311 (1964). Supreme Court of the United States, argued November 20, 1963, decided January 6, 1964. Justice Stewart wrote for a five-Justice majority.
Justice Black dissented, and Justice Brennan, joined by Chief Justice Warren and Justice Goldberg, dissented separately. The case appears at casebook page 257 and concerns whether a person named in a printed lease to receive process for the lessees was "an agent authorized by appointment" under the Federal Rules. Full judicial opinion.
Source: added-sources/CivPro_Notice_Service_and_Hearing_Briefs.pdf, pages 14–16

== Parties ==
National Equipment Rental, Ltd., a corporation with its principal place of business in New York, leased farm equipment to the respondents. It was the plaintiff in the district court and the petitioner in the Supreme Court.
The Szukhents, Michigan farmers who had leased the equipment, were the defendants and respondents.
Florence Weinberg, the person the lease designated to receive process for them in New York, was not a party.
The lessor sought to sue the Szukhents in New York for unpaid rent; the Szukhents sought to quash service on the ground that Weinberg was not their agent.

== Material Facts ==
In 1961 the Szukhents leased farm equipment from the petitioner on a printed form less than a page and a half long, containing eighteen numbered paragraphs. The last paragraph, printed just above the respondents' signatures in the same type as the rest of the form, provided that the agreement would be deemed made in
Nassau County, New York, and governed by New York law, and that the lessee designated Florence
Weinberg, of 47-21 Forty-first Street, Long Island City, New York, as agent for the purpose of accepting service of any process within New York. The Szukhents did not know Weinberg. The dissent later noted that she was chosen by the company, had never met the Szukhents, received nothing from them, undertook no obligation to them, and was revealed after the suit began to be the wife of one of the company's officers. 375
U.S. at 312-14; id. at 319 (Black, J., dissenting).
In 1962 the petitioner sued in federal district court in New York, alleging that the Szukhents had failed to make the periodic payments the lease required. A United States marshal delivered two copies of the summons and complaint to Weinberg. The same day she mailed them to the Szukhents with a letter stating that the documents had been served on her as their agent under the lease. The petitioner also notified the
Szukhents by certified mail that process had been served on Weinberg. Id. at 313-14.

== Procedural History ==
On the Szukhents' motion, the United States District Court for the Eastern District of New York quashed service. It reasoned that although Weinberg had promptly notified the Szukhents and sent them the papers, the lease did not explicitly require her to do so, so there was a "failure of the agency arrangement to achieve intrinsic and continuing reality." The Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit affirmed, 311 F.2d 79 (2d Cir.), and the Supreme Court granted certiorari. Id. at 314.

== Issue ==
Was a person designated in a printed lease to receive process for the lessees, who was a stranger to them and whose appointment did not expressly require her to forward process, "an agent authorized by appointment" to receive service within Federal Rule 4(d)(1) (now Rule 4(e)(2)(C)), where she in fact promptly transmitted the summons and complaint to them?

== Holding ==
Parties to a contract may agree in advance to submit to the jurisdiction of a given court, to permit notice to be served by the opposing party, or even to waive notice altogether. Under general principles of agency law, an appointed agent's prompt acceptance of process and transmittal of it to the principal validates the agency even without an explicit promise to transmit. Weinberg was therefore an agent authorized by appointment within the Rule, and service on her was valid. The Rule is a uniform federal standard, and even if state law were relevant, neither Michigan nor New York law requires an appointed agent to promise expressly to forward process. The Court limited its holding to a case in which prompt notice was actually given: a different case would be presented if Weinberg had not promptly notified the Szukhents. Id. at 315-18.

== Judgment ==
The Supreme Court reversed the judgment of the Court of Appeals and remanded, leaving the Szukhents subject to suit in the Eastern District of New York.

== Reasoning ==
The Court first set aside Wuchter v. Pizzutti, on which the Szukhents relied. Wuchter concerned the limits the
Fourteenth Amendment places on a state statute that subjects nonresidents to its courts' jurisdiction by designating a public official to receive process. The question here was whether a party to a private contract may appoint an agent to receive service within the meaning of the Federal Rule, where the agent is not personally known to the party and has not expressly undertaken to transmit notice. Id. at 315.
The purpose of the clause was clear: the petitioner inserted it, and the Szukhents agreed to it, to ensure that any litigation under the lease would take place in New York. It was settled, as the lower courts had recognized, that contracting parties may agree in advance to submit to a court's jurisdiction, to permit notice to be served by the opposing party, or to waive notice altogether. The Court framed the only question before it as whether Weinberg was an agent authorized by appointment; no question of subject-matter jurisdiction or venue was presented. Id. at 312, 315-16.
On that question the Court applied general principles of agency law. Weinberg's prompt acceptance of the papers and her transmittal of them to the Szukhents, under the authority the lease conferred, was itself sufficient to validate the agency, even though she had not promised in advance to forward process. The Court emphasized that it was applying a Federal Rule governing federal courts in all fifty states. Even assuming that a uniform federal standard should give way to contrary state policy, no relevant state rule would invalidate the agency: Michigan's statute validating service under similar circumstances did not require an express undertaking to notify the principal, and New York law, which the lease selected, did not require one either. Id. at 316-17.
The Court rejected the argument that the agency was invalid because Weinberg had a conflict of interest, based on the facts that she did not know the Szukhents and might be related to an officer of the company. The argument ignored the narrow nature of the agency. Weinberg was appointed for the single purpose of receiving service, and both the lessor and the lessees had an equal interest in ensuring that, if litigation arose, the lessees received the adequate and timely notice that is a prerequisite to a valid judgment. A footnote added that there was no allegation that Weinberg had any pecuniary interest in the litigation. Id. at 317-18 & n.9.
The Court confined its holding. It held only that, prompt notice having been given, Weinberg was an agent authorized by appointment. If she had not promptly notified the Szukhents, a claim might well be made that
her failure had invalidated the agency, and the Court expressly did not reach a case in which the defendant received no personal notice. Id. at 315, 318.

== Separate Opinions ==
Justice Black dissented. He stressed that the Szukhents had never dealt with, met, or heard of Weinberg, that she was handpicked by the company, and that only after suit was it revealed that she was the wife of one of its officers. He would have held that New York law governed and that, as the lower courts found, the appointment was invalid under it; alternatively, a federal definition of agency should exclude her; and upholding service raised serious due process questions. He predicted that such clauses would spread into the boilerplate of equipment leases and conditional sales contracts, giving large companies a green light to force individuals to defend in distant commercial centers or suffer default, and he called the notion that the
Szukhents knowingly consented to suit in New York "a fiction—not even an amiable one." Id. at 318-33 (Black, J., dissenting).
Justice Brennan, joined by Chief Justice Warren and Justice Goldberg, dissented on the ground that federal standards should define an agent authorized by appointment and that those standards should impose three requirements. The Rule should deny validity to the appointment of an agent whose interests conflict with those of the supposed principal. The appointment should include an explicit condition that the agent transmit process to the principal forthwith, drawing on Wuchter's reasoning. And because the corporate plaintiff prepared the printed form, an individual purchaser should not be bound by the appointment without proof, beyond a signature, that he understandingly consented to be sued in a state other than his residence. Id. at
333-34 (Brennan, J., dissenting).

== Notes ==
The majority resolved the case under the Federal Rule and general agency law, and it did not decide any due process question. The casebook asks why the Court looked to agency principles rather than the Due Process
Clause and whether the Constitution has any role when a party contractually waives service. The limit stated in the opinion is central to the holding: service was upheld because prompt notice was actually given, which gives the Szukhents the same practical notice Mullane requires. The opinion leaves open whether a contractual appointment of an agent who never forwards the papers would support a judgment.
Justice Black's objection concerns consent more than notice. Weinberg's prompt mailing gave the Szukhents actual notice; the burden he identified was the cost of defending far from home under a clause buried in a printed form. The same question returns in the enforcement of forum-selection clauses in consumer form contracts and, as the casebook's second note asks, in "shrinkwrap," "clickwrap," and "browsewrap" agreements assented to with a click or a tap. The Contracts materials on rolling contracts in Hill v. Gateway
2000 address the parallel question of when a consumer is bound by terms that appear only in a standard form.
