Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co.
Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co., 339 U.S. 306 (1950). Supreme Court of the United States, argued February 8, 1950, decided April 24, 1950. Justice Jackson wrote the opinion for the Court; Justice
Burton dissented, and Justice Douglas took no part. The case begins at casebook page 237 and concerns what notice due process requires before a court may enter a judgment that binds people who were not personally served. Full judicial opinion.
Source: added-sources/CivPro_Notice_Service_and_Hearing_Briefs.pdf, pages 1–5

== Parties ==
Central Hanover Bank and Trust Company, a New York trust company, was the trustee of a common trust fund and the petitioner in the state accounting proceeding; in the Supreme Court it was an appellee. Mullane was the appellant. The Surrogate's Court had appointed him special guardian and attorney for all persons, known or unknown and not otherwise appearing, who had or might later have an interest in the income of the fund. A special guardian is a representative the court appoints to protect people who are absent or unable to act for themselves. Vaughan, also an appellee, had been appointed to represent those interested in the principal. The beneficiaries themselves never appeared. The trust company sought a decree approving its account that would bind every beneficiary; Mullane sought to defeat the decree on the ground that the beneficiaries had not received constitutionally adequate notice.

== Material Facts ==
New York Banking Law § 100-c allowed a trust company, with the approval of the State Banking Board, to pool the assets of an unlimited number of trusts for which it was trustee into a single fund for investment.
Each participating trust shared ratably in the fund's income, gains, losses, and expenses, but no trust or beneficiary owned any particular asset, and exclusive management rested with the trust company. Pooling gave small trusts the diversification and economy of management that only large trusts could otherwise obtain; the District of Columbia and some thirty other states had similar statutes. The trustee had to account to the court twelve to fifteen months after establishing a fund and every three years after that. A decree settling an account was binding and conclusive, as to any matter set forth in the account, on everyone with an interest in the common fund or in any participating trust. 339 U.S. at 307-09.
Central Hanover established a common fund in January 1946 and, in March 1947, petitioned the Surrogate's
Court, New York's court for estates and trusts, to settle its first account. During the accounting period 113 trusts participated, about half inter vivos (created by a living settlor) and half testamentary (created by a will), with gross capital of nearly three million dollars. The record did not show the number or residence of the beneficiaries, but they were many, and some lived outside New York. The only notice of the accounting was the notice the statute required under § 100-c(12): publication in a local newspaper for four successive weeks, stating the trust company's name and address, the name and date of establishment of the fund, and a list of the participating trusts. The notice named no beneficiary.
Earlier, when each trust first entered the fund, the trust company had mailed notice under § 100-c(9) to every beneficiary of full age and sound mind whose name and address were then known and who was entitled to share in income or, if the relevant event had already occurred, in principal. That notice enclosed the statutory provisions on notice and judicial settlement. As the Supreme Court read the New York decision, the decree would settle all questions respecting the management of the fund and would seal and terminate every claim the beneficiaries might have against the trust company for improper management during the accounting
period. The beneficiaries' interests also bore the fees and expenses of the guardians appointed to represent them. Id. at 309-11.

== Procedural History ==
Mullane appeared specially, meaning that he appeared only to contest the court's authority without submitting to it. He objected that the statutory notice was inadequate under the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth
Amendment, so the Surrogate's Court lacked jurisdiction to enter a final and binding decree. The Surrogate overruled the objections and entered a final decree accepting the accounts. The Appellate Division affirmed, and the New York Court of Appeals affirmed, holding the notice constitutionally sufficient. The case reached the Supreme Court on appeal, the route then available when a state's highest court upheld a state statute against a federal constitutional challenge. Id. at 309-11.

== Issue ==
Did New York have power to bind beneficiaries who lived outside the state and were not personally served, when the decree adjudicated personal claims against the trustee rather than title to specific property, and
Pennoyer v. Neff limited a state's power over nonresidents in actions in personam? If so, was notice by newspaper publication constitutionally adequate for each category of beneficiary: those whose interests or whereabouts could not be ascertained with due diligence, those whose interests were conjectural or future, and known present beneficiaries whose addresses the trustee had on its books?

== Holding ==
The State's power to settle the account did not depend on whether the proceeding was labeled in rem, quasi in rem, or in personam. New York's interest in closing trusts that exist by the grace of its laws and are administered under the supervision of its courts established the right of its courts to determine the interests of all claimants, resident or nonresident, provided the procedure gave them full opportunity to appear and be heard.
Due process requires, before any proceeding that is to be accorded finality, notice reasonably calculated, under all the circumstances, to apprise interested parties of the pendency of the action and afford them an opportunity to present their objections. The notice must reasonably convey the required information and allow a reasonable time to appear. The means chosen must be such as one desirous of actually informing the absentee might reasonably adopt. A method is valid if it is reasonably certain to inform those affected or, where conditions do not reasonably permit such notice, if it is not substantially less likely to bring home notice than other feasible and customary substitutes.
Publication was sufficient for beneficiaries whose interests or whereabouts could not be ascertained with due diligence and for those whose interests were conjectural or future. It was insufficient for known present beneficiaries of known residence, who were entitled at least to notice by ordinary mail to the addresses in the trustee's records. Personal service was not required. The Court held the notice prescribed by § 100-c(12) incompatible with the Fourteenth Amendment as a basis for an adjudication depriving known persons whose whereabouts are also known of substantial property rights. Id. at 311-20.

== Judgment ==
The Supreme Court reversed the judgment of the New York Court of Appeals and remanded for further proceedings not inconsistent with its opinion. The Court expressly overruled the constitutional objections insofar as they were made on behalf of beneficiaries whose interests or addresses were unknown to the trustee; the defect lay in the notice to known present beneficiaries.

== Reasoning ==
Mullane's first argument drew on Pennoyer. An action in personam determines a person's own rights and obligations, and under Pennoyer it required personal service within the state to bind a nonresident. An action in rem determines rights in a thing and binds everyone. Because the decree affected neither title to nor possession of any thing and adjudicated only the beneficiaries' personal rights to surcharge their trustee,
Mullane argued, it was in personam and could not bind nonresidents served only by publication. The Court declined to decide the case by classification. Proceedings to settle fiduciary accounts had been called in rem, quasi in rem, and in the nature of a proceeding in rem, and this one had features of both categories. The distinctions had originated in the property law of a very different legal system, and the rise of intangible property had blurred them. The requirements of the Fourteenth Amendment, Justice Jackson wrote, do not depend on a classification so elusive and so variable from state to state. The State's interest in closing trusts created under its law and supervised by its courts justified adjudicating the claims of all beneficiaries, wherever they lived, if the procedure gave them full opportunity to be heard. Id. at 311-13.
The question of power therefore gave way to the question of opportunity. Whatever else the Due Process
Clause requires, a deprivation of life, liberty, or property by adjudication must be preceded by notice and an opportunity for hearing appropriate to the nature of the case. The accounting could deprive beneficiaries of property in two ways: it could cut off their claims against the trustee for negligent or illegal impairment of their interests, and it could diminish their interests by allowing fees and expenses to a guardian who conducted a possibly fruitless contest in their names but without their knowledge. Against the State's vital interest in a final settlement stood the individual's interest, which Grannis v. Ordean defined as the opportunity to be heard. That right has little reality or worth unless a person is informed that the matter is pending and can choose whether to appear or default, acquiesce or contest. Id. at 313-14.
Personal service of written notice within the jurisdiction is the classic form of notice and is always adequate, but the Court had never held it indispensable in every case, and a reading of the Due Process Clause that placed impossible or impractical obstacles in the way of settling trusts could not be justified. The Court therefore stated a functional standard: notice reasonably calculated, under all the circumstances, to apprise interested parties and afford them an opportunity to object, conveying the required information and allowing a reasonable time to respond. It added that "when notice is a person's due, process which is a mere gesture is not due process." The means employed must be those that a person who actually wanted to inform the absentee might reasonably use. Id. at 313-15.
Publication failed that test as a way of informing known people. It would be idle, the Court wrote, to pretend that publication alone is a reliable means of telling interested parties that their rights are before the courts.
Chance alone brings a small-type advertisement in the back pages of a newspaper to the attention of even a local resident, and the odds are large that it will never reach someone who lives outside the paper's circulation area. This notice did not even name the beneficiaries whose attention it was supposed to attract, so acquaintances could not call it to their attention. Measured against actual notice, the Court regarded it as no more than a feint. Id. at 315-16.
Publication has traditionally been acceptable as a supplement to action that itself is likely to warn an owner.
Libel of a ship (an admiralty proceeding against a vessel), attachment of a chattel, or entry upon real estate in the name of the law may reasonably be expected to come promptly to the owner's attention, because owners of tangible property usually arrange to learn of direct attacks on it. A state may assume that someone who leaves property within its borders has either abandoned it or left a caretaker who will warn of danger. Neither assumption held here. The beneficiaries had not abandoned their interests, and their caretaker, the trustee, became their adversary in the accounting. The trustee was released from any duty to warn them, and not even the special guardian was required or apparently expected to communicate with them. Id. at 316.
Publication remained acceptable where nothing better was reasonably possible. For beneficiaries whose interests or whereabouts could not be ascertained with due diligence, an indirect and even probably futile means of notice was all the situation permitted, and in the typical case it was not much more likely to fail than any alternative a legislature could prescribe. The State could also dispense with more certain notice to beneficiaries whose interests were conjectural or future, or who could be discovered only by investigation and had not come to the trustee's knowledge in the ordinary course of business. Keeping track of changes among income beneficiaries, presumptive remaindermen (those expected to take the principal when the income interests end), and the larger number of contingent beneficiaries would impose a severe burden on the plan and would likely dissipate its advantages. Due process did not require those searches. Id. at 317-18.
Known present beneficiaries of known residence stood on a different footing. Exceptions made in the name of necessity do not sweep away the rule that, within the limits of practicability, notice must be reasonably calculated to reach interested parties; where names and addresses are at hand, the reasons for resorting to less reliable means disappear. The trustee had the income beneficiaries' names and addresses on its books and periodically sent them their income. Mailing them a copy of the statute months or years before the accounting did not satisfy the requirement. Id. at 318.
The Court did not require personal service, even on known beneficiaries. The trust presupposed a large number of small interests, and each beneficiary's interest in the integrity of the fund and the fidelity of the trustee was identical to that of the class. Objections sustained on behalf of any beneficiary would benefit all, so notice reasonably certain to reach most of those interested in objecting would safeguard the interests of everyone, and reasonable risks that notice might not reach every beneficiary were justified. The Court also observed that personal service outside the issuing state would serve the end of actual notice, whatever power of compulsion it lacked, a remark that separates the function of notice from the question of a court's power.
Id. at 318-19.
The statutory notice to known beneficiaries was inadequate because it was not reasonably calculated to reach people who could easily be informed by other means at hand; its failure to reach everyone was not the constitutional defect. The mails were an efficient and inexpensive means of communication, and the trust company's own mailing when the fund was established showed that mailed notice at the time of accounting would not seriously burden the plan. The Court asked whether publication would satisfy a prudent businessman, counting his pennies but finding it in his interest to convey information to many people whose names and addresses were in his files, and concluded that it would not. Quoting McDonald v. Mabee, it warned that great caution should be used not to let fiction deny the fair play that can be secured only by a pretty close adhesion to fact. Id. at 319-20.

== Separate Opinions ==
Justice Burton dissented. He emphasized that common trusts are available only when the instruments creating the participating trusts permit participation in a common fund, and he regarded the question whether further notice should supplement the notice and representation New York provided as one properly within the State's discretion; in his view the Federal Constitution did not require more. Justice Douglas took no part in the consideration or decision of the case. The casebook omits the dissent. Id. at 320 (Burton, J., dissenting).

== Notes ==
Mullane separates two requirements that Pennoyer had combined: a court's power over the parties or the property, and the notice that must precede a binding judgment. The Court treated New York's power as established by the State's interest in trusts created under its law and then tested the notice independently. A court with power over a defendant can still violate due process by proceeding on inadequate notice, and personal service outside the forum can give excellent notice while conferring no power. The case also reduced the significance of the in rem and in personam labels for notice purposes; Shaffer v. Heitner, which subjected all assertions of state-court jurisdiction to the minimum-contacts standard, later made a similar move for jurisdiction.
The standard evaluates the method chosen, judged by what the sender knew or could reasonably learn, rather than whether the party actually received notice. It has two branches: a method reasonably certain to inform, or, where that is not reasonably possible, a method not substantially less likely to reach the party than feasible and customary substitutes. The analysis proceeds by sorting interested parties into categories, as the Court did: known parties with known addresses (mail at least), parties who cannot be identified or located with due diligence (publication can suffice), and parties with conjectural or remote interests (publication can suffice because investigation costs would defeat the proceeding). The Court's weighing of the individual interest against the cost of better notice anticipates the balancing the Court later formalized in Mathews v. Eldridge.
Two limits deserve attention. The Court did not require personal service, and its willingness to accept some risk that individual beneficiaries would not receive notice rested on the identity of interests within the class, reasoning that fits representative proceedings better than ordinary two-party suits. In Eisen v. Carlisle &
Jacquelin, 417 U.S. 156 (1974), the Court relied on Mullane in requiring individual notice to all members of a Rule 23(b)(3) class who can be identified through reasonable effort. The notes that follow Mullane in the casebook, summarized in the next section of this packet, apply the known-address rule to tax sales, condemnation, probate claims, eviction, and forfeiture.
