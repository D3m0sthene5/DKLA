Restatement of the Law, Consumer Contracts § 2(b) and (c)

Source: added-sources/Contracts_Battle_of_the_Forms_and_Rolling_Contracts_Briefs.pdf, pages 16–17

== Sources ==
Restatement of the Law, Consumer Contracts § 2 (Am. L. Inst. 2024). The American Law Institute's membership approved the Restatement at its May 2022 Annual Meeting, subject to minor revisions, and the
Institute published it in 2024. The abbreviation RLCC (sometimes RCC) is a citation convention rather than an official title. Like other Restatements, it describes common-law rules and is persuasive authority unless a court adopts it. Section 2 governs when standard contract terms are adopted as part of a consumer contract:
subsection (a) covers terms made available before the consumer assents, subsection (b) covers terms first made available afterward, and subsection (c) states the consequence when some terms are not adopted. The text summarized below is the published black-letter as reproduced in secondary sources; the assigned casebook excerpt governs for class. ALI publication notice; ALI Adviser summary of § 2.

== Text ==
Section 1 supplies the vocabulary. In substance, a consumer contract is a contract between a business and a consumer, a consumer is an individual who transacts primarily for personal, family, or household purposes, and a standard contract term is a term drafted in advance for use in multiple transactions rather than negotiated with the particular consumer. Reasonableness under the Restatement is judged on the totality of the circumstances. These definitions mark the boundary of the section: the merchant-to-merchant exchanges in Bayway, Northrop, Dorton, and C. Itoh fall outside it, while the consumer purchases in Hill and Klocek fall within it.
Subsection (a) states the baseline. A standard term is adopted if the business demonstrates that the consumer manifested assent to the transaction after receiving reasonable notice of the term and of the intent to include it in the contract, together with a reasonable opportunity to review it. Two features carry through the rest of the section. The business bears the burden of proof, and adoption of a term is analyzed separately from assent to the transaction as a whole.
Subsection (b): Terms Made Available After Assent
Subsection (b) addresses the transaction in Hill and Klocek: the consumer pays or otherwise assents first and sees the standard terms only later. A term that is available for review only after the consumer manifests assent is adopted if the business demonstrates three things. First, before manifesting assent, the consumer received reasonable notice that a standard term would be provided later and would be part of the contract, informing the consumer of the opportunity to review it and to terminate the contract, and explaining that a failure to terminate would result in adoption of the term. Second, after manifesting assent, the consumer received a reasonable opportunity to review the term. Third, after the term was made available, the consumer received a reasonable opportunity to terminate the transaction without unreasonable cost, loss of value, or personal burden, and did not exercise that power.
Each element responds to a criticism of Hill. The first element requires pre-assent notice of the existence of later terms and of the consequence of keeping the goods; Hill required no such notice and treated advertisements mentioning a limited warranty and the availability of terms on request as sufficient. The third element makes the exit route substantive: a return window that is too short, or that imposes restocking fees, return shipping, or loss of value, does not satisfy it. Under this test Gateway would have failed on the Klocek record, because it offered no evidence of any notice at the point of sale, and would have faced contested questions on the Hill record about the adequacy of its pre-sale notice and the cost of returning a computer within 30 days.
The subsection also departs from Klocek. That decision required a non-merchant buyer's express assent to terms arriving with the goods and refused to infer assent from retention. Subsection (b) permits adoption through retention, provided the business proves the notice and termination conditions. The Restatement thus accepts the accept-or-return structure in principle and regulates its conditions, rather than rejecting it as
Klocek did or accepting it without conditions as Hill did.
Subsection (c): Contract Without the Unadopted Terms
Subsection (c) answers the question that § 2-207(3) answers for merchants: what happens when a contract exists but some proposed terms did not make it in. If the consumer manifests assent to the transaction, the contract is not rendered unenforceable because some standard terms were not adopted under subsections (a) and (b). The contract's terms are those adopted under (a) and (b); any unadopted standard terms that the consumer elects to invoke; any terms, standard or not, to which both parties actually agreed; and terms supplied or incorporated by law.
Three consequences follow. The business cannot escape the bargain by arguing that its own terms failed; the failure of a standard term never voids the transaction. The consumer's election is one-directional: a consumer may choose to rely on an unadopted term that is favorable, such as a warranty or service promise, while the business cannot invoke an unadopted term against the consumer. And the gap left by an unadopted term is filled by law, so an arbitration clause that fails under (b) leaves the parties in court, and a failed warranty disclaimer leaves the implied warranties of Article 2 in place.

== Application ==
Begin by confirming that the contract is a consumer contract and that the disputed term is a standard term.
Then locate the moment of assent and ask whether the term was available before it. If it was, apply subsection (a): reasonable notice of the term and of the intent to include it, and a reasonable opportunity to review. If it was not, apply subsection (b) element by element, keeping the burden on the business: pre-assent notice of later terms and of the review-and-terminate mechanism; a post-assent opportunity to review; and an opportunity to terminate without unreasonable cost, loss of value, or personal burden that the consumer did not use. If any element fails, subsection (c) governs: the contract survives, the term drops out unless the consumer elects it, and the law supplies whatever the term would have covered.
Suppose a consumer orders a laptop by telephone, is told nothing about terms, and finds a five-day return notice and an arbitration clause in the box. Under subsection (b) the business cannot show pre-assent notice, so the arbitration clause is not adopted, and under subsection (c) the sale stands with disputes resolved in court. Suppose instead that the order confirmation states that terms will be enclosed, that the consumer may return the laptop within 30 days at the business's expense for a full refund, and that keeping it adopts the terms. The first element is met, and the case turns on whether the review and termination opportunities were reasonable in the circumstances, including any cost or burden of the return.
