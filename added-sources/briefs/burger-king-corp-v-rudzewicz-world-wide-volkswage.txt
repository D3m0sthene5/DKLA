Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz
471 U.S. 462 (1985). U.S. Supreme Court. Brennan, J. No. 83-2097; argued January 8, 1985; decided May 20, 1985. Review of Burger King Corp. v. MacShara, 724 F.2d 1505 (11th Cir. 1984), rehearing en banc denied, 729 F.2d 1468.
Source: added-sources/Civil_Procedure_World_Wide_Volkswagen_Burger_King_Asahi_Big_Brief.pdf, pages 4–6

== Parties ==
Petitioner / plaintiff: Burger King Corporation, a Florida corporation headquartered in Miami. It franchised restaurants, leased standardized facilities, supervised the national system, sued in Florida federal court, and obtained the trial judgment. Respondent / defendant-appellant below: John Rudzewicz, a Michigan resident and senior partner in a Detroit accounting firm. He supplied capital, signed the franchise documents personally, challenged personal jurisdiction, and pursued the appeal. Original codefendant: Brian MacShara, a Michigan resident with restaurant and construction experience who managed daily operations and attended training in Miami. He did not appeal the judgment.

== Procedural History ==
Federal complaint and subject-matter jurisdiction: Burger King filed in the U.S. District Court for the Southern
District of Florida in May 1981. The complaint invoked diversity jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. Section 1332(a) for the contract dispute and federal-question jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. Section 1338(a) for trademark claims. Burger King alleged that the franchisees breached obligations requiring payment at its Miami headquarters and continued using
Burger King marks and confidential operating information after termination. It sought contract damages, injunctive relief, costs, and attorney's fees.
State long-arm authority in federal court: The district court relied on Florida Statutes Section 48.193(1)(g), which extended personal jurisdiction over a person who breached a contract in Florida by failing to perform acts the contract required there, for claims arising from that breach. The federal court therefore asked whether the Florida statute reached the franchisees and whether the Fourteenth Amendment allowed Florida to bind them. Rudzewicz later conceded that his conduct fell within the statute, leaving constitutional due process as the contested limit.
Special appearance and denial: Rudzewicz and MacShara entered special appearances and argued that their Michigan residence, Michigan restaurant, and local operations placed the dispute outside Florida's personal jurisdiction. After a hearing, the district court denied their motions. It held that a nonresident Burger King franchisee was subject to jurisdiction in an action arising from franchise obligations requiring performance in Florida. The defendants then answered and asserted a counterclaim under the Michigan Franchise Investment Law. Their participation after the adverse ruling preserved the previously raised objection.
Bench trial and final judgment: Judge Kehoe conducted a three-day bench trial. The court reaffirmed jurisdiction, found breach of the franchise agreements and infringement of Burger King's trademarks and service marks, rejected the
Michigan statutory counterclaim, and entered joint and several contract damages of $228,875.40. It awarded $2,151.06 in costs and $30,000 in attorney's fees. It also ordered the franchisees to close Restaurant No. 775 immediately or surrender possession and the keys to Burger King. The final judgment made the preserved jurisdictional issue reviewable together with the merits.
Scope of Rudzewicz's appeal: MacShara accepted the judgment. Rudzewicz appealed to the Eleventh Circuit and challenged personal jurisdiction, the contract judgment, and the rejection of his counterclaim. He reached a compromise that waived appellate review of the trademark-infringement finding and the associated injunction. The surviving personal-jurisdiction issue concerned Florida's contract long-arm provision and Burger King's claim for performance due in Miami.
Eleventh Circuit: A divided panel reversed. 724 F.2d 1505 (11th Cir. 1984). Judge Vance's majority treated the minimum-contacts inquiry as a balance of commercial responsibility and fair preparation for suit. It emphasized that
Burger King's Michigan office conducted the face-to-face negotiations, supervised the restaurant, and supplied the contractual services; Rudzewicz lived and operated exclusively in Michigan; the restaurant's sales occurred there; and the printed Florida terms arrived through standardized documents. The majority found inadequate warning and substantial litigation disadvantage, held Florida jurisdiction fundamentally unfair, and stopped before reaching the substantive appellate claims. Judge Johnson dissented. He emphasized Rudzewicz's sophistication, five months of negotiations, personal undertaking exceeding $1 million, Florida choice-of-law clauses, payment obligations to Miami, and Florida's interest. Rehearing en banc was denied. 729 F.2d 1468.
Route into the Supreme Court: Burger King attempted a direct appeal under then-28 U.S.C. Section 1254(2), a route available when a federal court of appeals held a state statute unconstitutional as applied. The Supreme Court found the
Eleventh Circuit's basis unclear because Florida courts had construed Section 48.193(1)(g) to extend through the due-process boundary. A statute defined by that boundary may yield an unconstitutional exercise without itself being invalidated. The Court dismissed the statutory appeal, treated Burger King's jurisdictional statement as a petition for certiorari under 28 U.S.C. Section 2103, granted certiorari, and reached the federal question.
Supreme Court disposition and standard of review: By a 6-2 vote, the Court reversed the Eleventh Circuit and remanded for further proceedings. Justice Powell took no part. The constitutional sufficiency of the forum relationship presented a legal question. The district court's trial findings concerning sophistication, misrepresentation, fraud, adhesion, and economic duress remained governed by Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 52(a), which permitted reversal only for clear error. The record supported those findings, and the Court used them in the fairness analysis.

== Material Facts ==
Burger King operated a nationwide franchise system from Miami. Franchisees received a twenty-year license to use its trademarks and operating system in standardized restaurants. Miami headquarters set policy and controlled major decisions; district offices monitored daily operations and reported to Miami. The agreements required franchise fees, rent, royalties, tax refunds, and other payments to Miami, directed relevant notices there, stated that final acceptance occurred there, and selected Florida law. The choice-of-law clause expressly stated that it did not require every suit to be filed in Florida.
In autumn 1978, Rudzewicz and MacShara jointly applied through Burger King's Birmingham, Michigan district office. Rudzewicz supplied capital and MacShara planned to manage the restaurant. The Michigan office evaluated the application, recommended an existing restaurant in Drayton Plains, Michigan, negotiated local matters, and attended the closing. Miami headquarters approved the application, mailed documents for execution and completion, supplied the final agreements, and retained authority over site-development fees, building design, rent calculation, personal liability, and other disputed terms.
MacShara attended Burger King University in Miami. The franchisees bought and installed approximately $165,000 of equipment before the final rent was calculated. Rudzewicz sought a lower rent after learning the final amount, and
Miami granted a limited concession. The parties also disputed whether liabilities could be assigned to their corporation;
Burger King required continued personal responsibility. Rudzewicz and MacShara signed the lease and franchise agreements in Michigan on June 4, 1979. Rudzewicz personally undertook payments exceeding $1 million over the twenty-year term.
The store initially performed steadily, then patronage fell during the 1979 recession. The franchisees quickly became delinquent. Miami sent default notices. The franchisees, the Michigan office, and Miami headquarters conducted extended negotiations by telephone and mail. The Michigan office acted as a channel while Miami made the decisive concessions and enforcement decisions. Miami eventually terminated the franchise and ordered the franchisees to vacate. They remained in the premises and continued operating under Burger King's marks.

== Issue ==
When a Michigan businessman deliberately establishes a negotiated, twenty-year, interdependent franchise relationship with a corporation headquartered in Florida; accepts continuing payment, notice, regulatory, and choice-of-law obligations tied to Florida; communicates with Miami to resolve major disputes; and allegedly breaches the
Florida-linked obligations, does specific personal jurisdiction in Florida satisfy the Fourteenth Amendment even though he operates the restaurant and resides outside Florida?

== Holding ==
Yes. Rudzewicz purposefully created a substantial and continuing relationship with Burger King's Miami headquarters, and the litigation arose directly from that relationship. Physical entry into Florida supplied one possible form of contact and was unnecessary because the defendant deliberately directed interstate contractual activity toward a Florida resident. The course of dealing and documents gave fair warning of Florida-linked obligations. After those contacts were established, Rudzewicz failed to present the compelling showing of unreasonableness required to defeat jurisdiction. The Court reversed and remanded.

== Reasoning ==
The two-stage due-process structure: A court first identifies defendant-created minimum contacts connecting the defendant, the forum, and the litigation. For specific jurisdiction, the defendant purposefully directs activity toward the forum and the claim arises out of or relates to that activity. The court then evaluates whether exercising the resulting authority accords with fair play and substantial justice. This sequence gives the defendant fair warning and then measures the practical fairness of the particular forum.
Purposeful availment: The requirement selects deliberate conduct carrying a substantial connection with the forum. It excludes contacts generated by a plaintiff, customer, or third person and contacts that arise by chance. A defendant who creates continuing obligations with a forum resident obtains commercial benefits supported by the forum's law and can anticipate accountability there for disputes produced by those obligations. World-Wide Volkswagen supplies the contrast: the Robinsons moved the product to Oklahoma after a completed New York sale, while Rudzewicz himself established and maintained the Florida-centered relationship.
Physical presence: Interstate commerce occurs through mail, telephone, payments, licenses, and continuing administration. A defendant's deliberate direction of those activities toward a forum resident supplies a meaningful affiliation even when the defendant remains physically elsewhere. Rudzewicz's own travel history therefore carried limited weight. MacShara's Miami training reinforced the interstate relationship, while the contract, negotiations, future obligations, and course of dealing carried the analysis.
Contract analysis: The existence of a contract with a forum resident supplies the starting fact. Courts examine the prior negotiations, contemplated future consequences, contractual terms, and actual course of dealing. This highly realistic inquiry treats the written agreement as an intermediate step connecting negotiations with the future relationship the parties sought to create. It prevents party residence or the formal place of signature from controlling a multistate transaction.
Application of the contract factors: Rudzewicz pursued affiliation with a national enterprise headquartered in
Florida, negotiated issues that the Michigan office lacked authority to resolve, accepted twenty years of detailed regulation and payment obligations administered from Miami, and communicated continuously with Miami during default. The claims arose from missed Miami payments and post-termination use of Florida-controlled marks and information. The quality, duration, and claim relationship of these contacts gave Rudzewicz advance notice that Florida could adjudicate the resulting dispute.
Choice of law and forum selection: The clause selected Florida's substantive law and left the litigation forum open. It reinforced purposeful affiliation because the parties chose Florida's legal protections for a long-term Florida-centered relationship. The Court treated the clause as one component of the full course of dealing.
Reasonableness factors and allocation of proof: After purposeful contacts exist, the defendant may defeat jurisdiction by presenting a compelling case that the forum would be unreasonable. Courts assess the burden on the defendant, the forum's adjudicatory interest, the plaintiff's interest in effective relief, interstate efficiency, and shared substantive policies. Strong forum and plaintiff interests may support jurisdiction on a smaller set of purposeful contacts. An extreme litigation burden can defeat jurisdiction despite purposeful activity. Ordinary inconvenience is commonly handled through venue transfer, choice-of-law rules, and other procedural devices.
Application of fairness: Florida had a legitimate interest in adjudicating a dispute arising from obligations owed to a
Florida corporation and governed by Florida law. Michigan's franchise-regulation interest could be addressed through choice-of-law analysis. The record did not substantiate the Eleventh Circuit's concern that Florida litigation impaired access to essential Michigan witnesses. Rudzewicz was an experienced accountant represented by counsel, negotiated concessions, and accepted personal obligations exceeding $1 million. The district court found informed action, commercial sophistication, and an absence of misrepresentation or imposed economic duress. Those Rule 52(a) findings supported a fair opportunity to anticipate and defend the suit.
Case-specific reach: The Court adopted a transaction-specific inquiry. A franchise's decision structure, bargaining history, contractual allocation, duration, sophistication, and course of performance determine the forum relationship.
Burger King's proposed franchise-wide presumption therefore gave way to this individualized framework, which also governs modest consumer transactions.
Stevens dissent, joined by White: Justice Stevens focused on the economic and operational center of the franchise.
Rudzewicz's restaurant, employees, sales, property, taxes, and day-to-day supervision were located in Michigan;
Burger King's Michigan office conducted the direct negotiations and supplied contractual services. Stevens viewed the printed Florida provisions as insufficient fair warning when Burger King had structured the human relationship around its Michigan office. He would have affirmed the Eleventh Circuit's fairness judgment.
Procedural and doctrinal significance: Burger King makes the specific-jurisdiction inquiry sequential and assigns an evidentiary consequence to purposeful contacts: the defendant then carries the burden of showing a compelling constitutional unfairness. The case also demonstrates three separate review questions - state long-arm authorization, constitutional adjudicatory power, and trial-fact deference under Rule 52(a). Its reasonableness framework supplies the analysis that produced the controlling holding in Asahi.
