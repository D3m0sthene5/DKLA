Channel Home Centers, Division of Grace Retail Corp. v. Grossman
Channel Home Centers, Division of Grace Retail Corp. v. Grossman, 795 F.2d 291 (3d Cir. 1986). United
States Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit, argued January 7, 1986, decided June 30, 1986. Judge Becker wrote the opinion for a panel that included Judges Weis and Higginbotham. The case concerns whether a letter of intent's promise to negotiate exclusively and to take property off the market was an enforceable contract. Full judicial opinion.
Source: added-sources/Contracts_Definiteness_and_Preliminary_Agreements_Briefs.pdf, pages 12–14

== Parties ==
Channel Home Centers, a home improvement retail chain operated as a division of Grace Retail Corporation, was the prospective tenant and the plaintiff. Frank Grossman, a real estate developer, and the entities through which he was acquiring a shopping mall were the defendants. Channel sought to enforce the letter of intent and to stop Grossman from leasing the space to a competitor; Grossman maintained that the letter was a nonbinding preliminary document.

== Material Facts ==
Between November 1984 and February 1985, Grossman was acquiring the Cedarbrook Mall in Cheltenham
Township, Pennsylvania, and needed to show prospective tenants to lenders to obtain financing. Channel became interested in leasing space. On December 11, 1984, Channel sent Grossman a detailed letter of intent describing a 25-year lease of about 70,400 square feet, with a rent schedule and percentage rent. The letter stated: "To induce the Tenant to proceed with the leasing of this Store, you will withdraw the Store from the rental market, and only negotiate the above described leasing transaction to completion." Grossman had requested such a letter so that he could show it to "other people, banks or whatever," and he promptly signed and returned it. 795 F.2d at 292-93.
Both sides then worked toward a lease. Channel spent about $25,000 on measurements, plans, marketing, and equipment, and its parent approved the lease in February 1985. Grossman applied for zoning permits for
Channel's signs and exchanged draft lease documents. In late January 1985, Mr. Good Buys, a competitor of
Channel, contacted Grossman and toured the space. On February 6, 1985, Grossman terminated negotiations with Channel, asserting a 30-day deadline that did not appear in the letter, and on February 7 he leased the space to Mr. Good Buys at a substantially higher rent. Id. at 293-96.

== Procedural History ==
Channel sued in the Eastern District of Pennsylvania under diversity jurisdiction and sought a preliminary injunction. The district court consolidated the injunction hearing with a trial on the merits under Federal Rule
65(a)(2) without clear advance notice, and it held the letter of intent unenforceable because it did not bind the parties, lacked consideration, and failed to satisfy Pennsylvania's statute of frauds for leases. It also rejected
Channel's alternative unilateral-contract and promissory estoppel theories. Channel appealed. Id. at 297-98.

== Issue ==
Under Pennsylvania law, is a landlord's promise in a signed letter of intent to take the premises off the market and negotiate the lease only with the prospective tenant an enforceable contract, even though the lease itself remained to be negotiated? Specifically, did the parties intend to be bound by that promise, was it supported by consideration, and was it sufficiently definite?

== Holding ==
An agreement to negotiate in good faith, if it otherwise meets the requirements of a contract, is an enforceable contract. Under Pennsylvania law, enforceability depends on whether the parties manifested an intention to be bound, whether the terms are sufficiently definite to be enforced, and whether there was consideration. The evidence would support a finding that the parties intended to be bound by Grossman's promise to withdraw the store from the market and negotiate exclusively with Channel; the promise was definite enough to enforce; and Channel's execution and delivery of the letter, which Grossman wanted in order to obtain financing, was consideration for it. The statute of frauds did not apply because Channel did not claim that the letter was a lease. Id. at 298-301.

== Judgment ==
The Third Circuit reversed the judgment of the district court and remanded for further proceedings, including a trial on whether the parties intended to be bound and on the duration of the obligation to negotiate.

== Reasoning ==
The court first addressed procedure. Rule 65(a)(2) permits a court to consolidate a preliminary injunction hearing with trial on the merits, but parties must receive clear and unambiguous notice. The district court failed to give it, but because the court later offered a further hearing that Channel declined, the error did not by itself require reversal. The statute of frauds was irrelevant because Channel conceded that the letter was not a final lease and sought to enforce only the promise to negotiate. Id. at 297-98.
On intent, the court looked to the entire letter and the surrounding circumstances. Most of the letter described proposed lease terms that the parties plainly expected to negotiate further, but the exclusive-negotiation clause was unequivocal: Grossman would withdraw the store from the market and negotiate only with
Channel. The parties' conduct after signing, including Channel's planning expenditures, Grossman's zoning applications for Channel's signs, and the exchange of draft leases, was consistent with a binding commitment.
That evidence was sufficient to support a finding of intent to be bound, though it did not compel that finding as a matter of law. Id. at 299.
On definiteness, the court distinguished an unenforceable agreement to agree on the lease itself from
Grossman's promise to take the property off the market and negotiate exclusively with Channel. That promise was a specific, discrete undertaking whose breach a court could identify. Relying on decisions from other jurisdictions, the court held that an agreement to negotiate in good faith, if it otherwise meets the requirements of a contract, is enforceable. Id. at 299-300.
On consideration, the court rejected the argument that no money or thing of value supported the promise.
Grossman requested the letter so that he could show it to lenders, and Channel's signature, backed by its corporate parent, strengthened his financing position. Channel's execution and tender of the letter was therefore of substantial value to Grossman and constituted bargained-for consideration for his promise. Id. at
300.
On remand, the court identified at least two issues for trial: whether the parties in fact intended to be bound by the exclusive-negotiation promise, and how long the duty to negotiate lasted, including whether
Grossman's claimed 30-day limit had been agreed and, if not, what period was reasonable. Because it reversed on the contract theory, the court did not reach Channel's promissory estoppel and unilateral-contract arguments. Id. at 301.

== Separate Opinions ==
The panel was unanimous. The reported decision contains no concurrence or dissent.

== Notes ==
Channel Home Centers enforces a narrow promise inside a letter of intent that was otherwise nonbinding.
The lease terms were not enforceable, and Channel could not obtain the lease; what Grossman promised was exclusivity and good-faith negotiation. That distinction matters for remedies. Damages for breach of a duty to negotiate are difficult to measure, because it is uncertain whether good-faith negotiation would have produced a lease, and courts have divided over whether the injured party may recover lost expectation or only reliance.
The case parallels the second category in Tribune: both enforce a commitment to negotiate while leaving the final transaction unenforced. The consideration analysis is distinctive. Channel's promise was not needed; its act of signing a letter Grossman could show to lenders supplied the benefit Grossman bargained for. Drafters now commonly include explicit statements that a letter of intent is nonbinding except for identified provisions, such as exclusivity or confidentiality, precisely to control the question this case sent to trial.
