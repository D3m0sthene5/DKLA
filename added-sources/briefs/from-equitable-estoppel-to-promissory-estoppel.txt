From Equitable Estoppel to Promissory Estoppel
Doctrinal development reflected in Ricketts v. Scothorn; Samuel Williston, The Law of Contracts Section 139 (1920); Restatement (First) of Contracts Section 90 (1932); Restatement (Second) of Contracts Section 90 (1981).
Source: added-sources/Contracts_Promissory_Estoppel_Restitution_Big_Brief.pdf, pages 10–11

== Parties ==
Representor: a person whose statement, concealment, or conduct gives another a factual basis for action. Promisor: a person who manifests a commitment about future conduct. Reliant party: the person who reasonably changes position because of the representation or promise. Court: the institution that precludes an inconsistent factual position under equitable estoppel or grants calibrated enforcement under promissory estoppel.

== Procedural History ==
Traditional estoppel in pais developed as a doctrine of preclusion. A party who induced reasonable reliance on a representation of existing or past fact became unable to assert an inconsistent factual position in later litigation. The doctrine protected transactional good faith by holding the representor to the factual premise the representor created.
Nineteenth-century American courts confronted promises of future gifts, charitable subscriptions, pensions, land interests, and family support that produced serious reliance without a bargained exchange. Courts used several doctrinal explanations: they called the induced action consideration, found an implied return promise, or used equitable estoppel to prevent the promisor from pleading want of consideration.
Ricketts v. Scothorn illustrates the transitional form. Ricketts made a future payment promise and accurately intended to pay when he made it. Scothorn relied on the commitment itself by leaving work. The Nebraska Supreme Court nevertheless used equitable-estoppel terminology and precluded the estate from asserting lack of consideration.
Samuel Williston popularized the term promissory estoppel in his 1920 contracts treatise to identify reliance on a promise rather than reliance on a statement of fact. The American Law Institute gave the doctrine a general formulation in Restatement (First) of Contracts Section 90 in 1932. Courts including Feinberg then applied that rule directly as a basis for enforcing gratuitous promises that induced retirement or other substantial change.
Restatement (Second) of Contracts Section 90, published in 1981, broadened the text to include action or forbearance by a third person, removed the First Restatement's express phrase requiring reliance of a definite and substantial character from the operative sentence, and stated expressly that relief may be limited as justice requires. Its comments retain the definiteness and magnitude of reliance as factors relating reliance to the remedy sought.

== Material Facts ==
Equitable estoppel: A representation or conduct concerning fact, reasonable and good-faith reliance, a change of position, and resulting unfairness support preclusion of the representor's inconsistent position. The remedy operates on the later assertion and preserves the factual premise created by the earlier conduct.
Promissory estoppel: A commitment concerning future action, objectively foreseeable reliance, actual induced action or forbearance, and an injustice requiring enforcement support legal responsibility for the promise. The remedy responds to the reliance and may range from protection of reliance to enforcement of the promised performance.

== Issue ==
Why did courts and commentators create a distinct promissory-estoppel doctrine when equitable estoppel already protected reliance?

== Holding ==
The doctrines address different reliance objects and supply different forms of relief. Equitable estoppel holds a party to a factual position the party induced another to accept. Promissory estoppel recognizes a future commitment as binding to the extent justice requires after the promisor foreseeably induces action or forbearance. This distinction gives reliance on promises a direct home within contract law.

== Reasoning ==
Representation and promise: A factual representation asserts a state of the world that can be treated as true in litigation. A promise manifests an intention to act or refrain from acting and justifies an understanding that a commitment has been made. The promisee organizes future conduct around anticipated performance.
Why ordinary equitable estoppel fit awkwardly: A sincere promisor generally makes no false statement about present intention. The later breach does not retroactively transform the original intention into a false fact. The reliance injury flows from unperformed future conduct. Promissory estoppel addresses that causal structure directly.
From defense to affirmative enforcement: Equitable estoppel typically removes an inconsistent claim or defense.
Reliance cases such as Ricketts required an affirmative payment judgment. Section 90 states when the promise itself becomes binding and authorizes a remedy, giving courts a direct route from induced reliance to relief.
Relationship to consideration: Bargain doctrine enforces an exchange because the promisor sought the return performance or promise as the price of commitment. Promissory estoppel enforces a commitment because the promisor reasonably should have expected reliance, reliance occurred, and justice requires relief. The two grounds can arise from the same transaction and should be analyzed separately.
Reliance quality: The doctrine uses objective foresight to define risks attributable to the promisor. Actual inducement supplies causation. Reasonableness, definiteness, magnitude, and the relationship between the change of position and requested relief guide the injustice inquiry. Formality and commercial context strengthen the evidentiary and cautionary reasons for enforcement.
Remedial development: Early opinions often enforced the promise according to its terms. The Second Restatement makes remedial calibration explicit. Courts may award expenditures, opportunity costs, restitution, specific relief, or full promised performance as required by the particular injustice.
Case progression: Ricketts uses equitable preclusion to enforce a gift note after resignation. Feinberg uses First
Restatement Section 90 to enforce a pension after retirement. Cohen treats injustice as an explicit policy question and awards employment losses. D & G Stout applies Second Restatement remedial flexibility to a commercial opportunity cost.
Cold-call formulation: Ask what the claimant relied on. Reliance on an asserted fact points toward equitable estoppel.
Reliance on a commitment about future conduct points toward promissory estoppel. Then identify the induced change, the injustice created by withdrawal, and the relief proportionate to that injustice.
