Carnival Cruise Lines, Inc. v. Shute
Carnival Cruise Lines, Inc. v. Shute, 499 U.S. 585 (1991). United States Supreme Court, decided April 17,
1991. Justice Blackmun wrote for seven Justices. Justice Stevens dissented, joined by Justice Marshall. The decision applied federal admiralty law to a passenger ticket's forum-selection clause.
Full judicial opinion.

== Parties ==
Eulala and Russel Shute, a married couple from Washington, were plaintiffs below and respondents in the
Supreme Court. Carnival Cruise Lines, Inc., a cruise operator with its principal place of business in Florida, was defendant and petitioner. Mrs. Shute alleged personal injuries caused by negligence of Carnival and its employees; both spouses sought to litigate in Washington.

== Material Facts ==
The Shutes bought passage on a seven-day cruise aboard the Tropicale through a travel agent in Arlington,
Washington. Their payment was forwarded to Carnival's Miami headquarters, after which Carnival prepared and sent the tickets. A notice on the ticket's face directed passengers to the contract conditions on its final pages. One provision treated acceptance of the ticket as acceptance of its terms. Another required all disputes arising under, connected with, or incident to the contract to be litigated exclusively in a court located in
Florida.
The Shutes boarded in Los Angeles. The ship traveled to Puerto Vallarta, Mexico, and returned to Los
Angeles. Mrs. Shute allegedly slipped on a deck mat while touring the galley when the ship was in international waters off Mexico. The ticket clause was a standardized term that the Shutes had not individually negotiated. Whether the term had been adequately communicated was potentially important, but the Shutes essentially conceded notice in the litigation before the Supreme Court. The Court expressly limited its decision in light of that concession. 499 U.S. at 587-90.

== Procedural History ==
The Shutes sued in the United States District Court for the Western District of Washington, invoking admiralty. Carnival moved for summary judgment on two grounds: the ticket required suit in Florida, and
Carnival lacked sufficient Washington contacts for personal jurisdiction. The district court granted the motion on the constitutional personal-jurisdiction ground. It did not make findings that litigating in Florida would be physically or financially impossible for the Shutes.
The Ninth Circuit first reversed in 1988, then withdrew that opinion to certify to the Washington Supreme
Court whether the state's long-arm statute reached Carnival on these facts. The Washington Supreme Court answered yes, reasoning that the claim arose from Carnival's advertising and promotion in Washington. The
Ninth Circuit then reissued its modified opinion, 897 F.2d 377 (1990). It found sufficient contacts under a but-for theory linking solicitation to purchase of the cruise and the eventual injury. It separately rejected the forum clause because it had not been freely bargained for and because the record, in its view, showed the
Shutes could not afford to pursue the case in Florida.
The Supreme Court granted certiorari and reversed based on the contractual forum clause. Because that ground disposed of the question whether the Washington district court should hear the case, the Court expressly declined to decide Carnival's constitutional objection to personal jurisdiction. The change in rationale is crucial: the Supreme Court sustained Carnival's effort to end the Washington litigation through enforcement of the contract, rather than approving the district court's minimum-contacts analysis. 499 U.S. at
588-90, 597 n.*.

== Issue ==
Is a reasonable forum-selection clause in a standard passenger ticket unenforceable merely because the passengers did not negotiate it? Did alleged inconvenience or the federal statute protecting injured passengers require that this Florida clause be set aside?

== Holding ==
A nonnegotiated forum-selection clause in a form passage contract is not invalid merely because it was not individually bargained for. It remains subject to review for fundamental fairness, fraud, overreaching, and sufficiently grave inconvenience. The Shutes had not carried the heavy burden required to avoid this clause.
The clause also did not violate the version of the federal passenger-protection statute then codified at 46
U.S.C. App. §183c, because it preserved adjudication by a competent court and did not limit Carnival's substantive liability. 499 U.S. at 590-97.

== Judgment ==
The Supreme Court reversed the Ninth Circuit. The Florida forum clause was enforceable, and the Shutes could not maintain this action in Washington contrary to that agreement. The Court did not decide whether
Carnival had sufficient Washington contacts, whether Carnival was negligent, or whether the Shutes were entitled to damages. It also did not itself transfer the suit to a particular Florida court.

== Reasoning ==
The Court read The Bremen's emphasis on free negotiation in context. It was realistic to expect bargaining between sophisticated corporations arranging an international tow. It was unrealistic to expect an individual passenger to negotiate every term of an ordinary cruise ticket. That difference did not make a reasonable forum clause categorically unenforceable. The inquiry had to account for standardized passenger contracts while preserving scrutiny for unfair terms.
Carnival had a legitimate interest in limiting the number of forums in which it might be sued, because its passengers came from many locations. An advance choice reduced uncertainty, avoided litigation over where to sue, and conserved judicial resources. The majority also reasoned that savings from predictable litigation costs were likely reflected in lower fares. That was the Court's economic rationale; the opinion did not identify a factual finding that these particular passengers had received a quantified discount in exchange for the clause.
Florida was connected to Carnival's business rather than selected as an arbitrary obstacle. Carnival maintained its principal place of business there and operated many cruises from Florida ports. The record contained no showing that the company chose Florida to discourage legitimate claims or secured the Shutes' agreement through fraud or overreaching. Because notice had been conceded, the majority presumed they retained the opportunity to reject the contract. The dissent disputed the practical force of that presumption.
The asserted inconvenience did not justify nonenforcement. The district court had made no finding about physical or financial inability to litigate in Florida, and the Ninth Circuit's conclusory reference to the record did not supply a sufficient basis for the Supreme Court to accept such a finding. Florida was not a remote foreign forum, and an accident off Mexico on a cruise departing California was not an essentially local
Washington dispute. The Shutes therefore failed to meet the substantial burden described in The Bremen.
The statute prohibited ticket provisions relieving a carrier from negligence liability or weakening the right to a trial before a competent court. The majority distinguished selecting among competent courts from eliminating judicial recourse or capping recovery. A Florida court could adjudicate the same liability claim, so the clause did not violate the statute as construed by the Court. 499 U.S. at 593-97.

== Separate Opinions ==
Justice Stevens, joined by Justice Marshall, dissented. He emphasized that passengers received the ticket conditions after paying and that a separate nonrefund provision made rejection costly or uncertain. Fine print and unequal bargaining power undermined meaningful assent. He also read the passenger-protection statute more broadly: a distant forum could weaken the practical right to recover even without expressly eliminating liability. In his view, lower litigation expenses for Carnival did not justify shifting an unreasonable burden to an injured passenger, and a negotiated commercial clause in The Bremen did not resolve a consumer ticket's enforceability. 499 U.S. at 597-605.

== Notes ==
The case turns on an enforceable forum agreement and the plaintiffs' notice concession. It does not hold that every fine-print clause binds a passenger regardless of communication, fairness, or the circumstances of assent. The Ninth Circuit's but-for minimum-contacts analysis was left unreviewed by the Supreme Court; it should not be cited as the Supreme Court's holding. The choice of Florida also differs from a choice of
Florida substantive law, which the clause did not itself decide.
