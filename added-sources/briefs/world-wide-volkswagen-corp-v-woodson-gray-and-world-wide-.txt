World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson
444 U.S. 286 (1980). U.S. Supreme Court. White, J. Argued October 3, 1979; decided January 21, 1980. No. 78-1078. Certiorari to World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson, 1978 OK 131, 585 P.2d 351 (Okla. 1978).
Source: added-sources/Civil_Procedure_Gray_World_Wide_Volkswagen_Case_Briefs.pdf, pages 3–5

== Parties ==
Petitioners and jurisdictional objectors: World-Wide Volkswagen Corporation, a New York corporation serving as the regional distributor for Volkswagen products sold to dealers in New York, New Jersey, and Connecticut; and Seaway Volkswagen, Inc., the
New York retail dealer in Massena that sold the Robinsons' Audi. Underlying plaintiffs and respondents: Kay Robinson, suing for her own injuries; Harry Robinson, individually and as father and next friend for the injured children; and the children, including Eva
May Robinson and George Samuel Robinson. Named judicial respondent: Charles S. Woodson, District Judge of Creek County, whose continued exercise of in-personam jurisdiction the petitioners sought to prohibit.
Other underlying defendants: Audi NSU Auto Union Aktiengesellschaft, the manufacturer, and Volkswagen of America, Inc., the
U.S. importer. Volkswagen also entered a special appearance in the trial court, then declined to seek Oklahoma Supreme Court review.
Audi and Volkswagen therefore remained defendants in the Oklahoma action and were outside the jurisdictional judgment reviewed by the U.S. Supreme Court.

== Procedural History ==
Products-liability filing: After the Oklahoma accident, the Robinsons filed a manufacturers' products-liability action in the District
Court for Creek County, Oklahoma. They alleged defective design and placement of the Audi's gasoline tank and fuel system and joined the manufacturer, importer, regional distributor, and retail dealer. Oklahoma's Uniform Interstate and International Procedure
Act, 12 Okla. Stat. 1971 sections 1701.01 et seq., supplied the asserted long-arm authority over the out-of-state companies.
Special appearances and trial-court rulings: World-Wide and Seaway entered special appearances to contest personal jurisdiction under the Fourteenth Amendment. Their papers also used the phrases 'venue of the subject matter' and 'venue over the subject matter.'
The Creek County court treated the appearances as special, rejected their constitutional objection, and denied reconsideration. Those rulings were unreported. The merits action remained pending.
Preservation and waiver issue: The Robinsons later argued that petitioners had waived their objection by combining personal-jurisdiction language with a challenge concerning subject matter and by taking merits depositions in Oklahoma. The trial court retained the special-appearance characterization, and the Oklahoma Supreme Court decided the jurisdictional questions. The
U.S. Supreme Court accordingly treated the objection as preserved and reached the merits. 444 U.S. at 295 n.10.
Original proceeding for prohibition: World-Wide and Seaway invoked the Oklahoma Supreme Court's original jurisdiction and petitioned for a writ of prohibition directing Judge Woodson to stop exercising personal jurisdiction. Prohibition served as the pretrial vehicle for obtaining appellate review of adjudicatory authority before the petitioners litigated the products-liability merits. The
Oklahoma Supreme Court assumed original jurisdiction and denied the writ. 1978 OK 131, paragraphs 0-3, 17.
Oklahoma statutory analysis: Section 1701.03(a)(3) covered tortious injury in Oklahoma caused by an act or omission in Oklahoma.
The state court held that the alleged design and distribution acts occurred outside Oklahoma. It declined to classify the place of injury as the place of the act under Gray, because subsection (a)(4) separately governed an Oklahoma injury caused by an out-of-state act.
Subsection (a)(4) authorized jurisdiction when the defendant regularly did or solicited Oklahoma business, engaged in another persistent Oklahoma course of conduct, or derived substantial revenue from goods used or consumed or services rendered in
Oklahoma. 585 P.2d at 353-54.
Oklahoma ruling under subsection (a)(4): The state court reasoned that an automobile's designed mobility made Oklahoma use foreseeable and inferred from the vehicle's value and evidence of petitioners' goods being used in Oklahoma that the petitioners derived substantial revenue from goods used there. Five justices joined the opinion, two concurred in the result, and one concurred in part and dissented in part without opinion. The court therefore found statutory authority, assumed original jurisdiction, and denied prohibition. 585 P.2d at 354-55.
U.S. Supreme Court review and disposition: The Supreme Court granted certiorari, 440 U.S. 907 (1979), to resolve a conflict between Oklahoma and the highest courts of at least four other States concerning jurisdiction over out-of-state automobile sellers. The
Court reviewed the Fourteenth Amendment question while accepting Oklahoma's authoritative construction of its own long-arm statute. By a six-to-three judgment, it reversed the Oklahoma Supreme Court. Oklahoma consequently lacked power to bind
World-Wide and Seaway in this action; the claims against Audi and Volkswagen remained pending in Creek County. 444 U.S. at
290-91, 299 & nn.6, 9.

== Material Facts ==
Harry and Kay Robinson, then New York residents, purchased a new Audi from Seaway in Massena, New York, in 1976. In 1977 the family left New York to establish a new home in Arizona. While they drove through Oklahoma, another vehicle struck the Audi from behind. The gasoline tank ruptured, fire entered the passenger compartment, and Kay Robinson and the children suffered severe burns.
The other driver played no role in the jurisdictional litigation.
World-Wide's New York office administered a contractual regional distribution territory limited to New York, New Jersey, and
Connecticut. Seaway was a New York corporation operating its dealership in Massena. The record treated World-Wide, Seaway,
Volkswagen, and Audi as independent corporations connected by contracts. World-Wide and Seaway conducted no Oklahoma activity, closed no Oklahoma sales, performed no Oklahoma services, maintained no Oklahoma agent, shipped no products to
Oklahoma, and purchased no advertising calculated to reach Oklahoma.
The evidentiary record contained one vehicle sold by these petitioners that had entered Oklahoma: the Robinson Audi, carried there by its purchasers during their interstate move. The petitioners received no direct revenue from Volkswagen service centers in Oklahoma.
The forum relationship therefore depended on the vehicle's mobility, petitioners' role in the national automobile system, the Oklahoma accident, and the state court's inference of substantial revenue from cars used there.

== Issue ==
Does the Fourteenth Amendment permit an Oklahoma court to exercise specific in-personam jurisdiction over a New York automobile retailer and a regional distributor when they limited their sales and distribution to the New York-New Jersey-Connecticut market, and their only case-specific Oklahoma connection arose when New York purchasers drove the automobile through Oklahoma and suffered the accident there?

== Holding ==
Constitutional authority: No. Due process requires defendant-created affiliating circumstances connecting the defendant to the forum. World-Wide and Seaway directed no commercial activity toward Oklahoma, and the purchasers' unilateral transportation of one car through the State supplied the record's sole product entry. Designed mobility made interstate travel predictable, while the constitutionally operative foreseeability asked whether petitioners' own conduct made Oklahoma suit reasonably anticipatable.
Statutory ruling below: The Oklahoma Supreme Court had found subsection 1701.03(a)(4) satisfied; the U.S. Supreme Court held the resulting assertion beyond the Fourteenth Amendment. Disposition: The judgment denying prohibition was reversed.

== Reasoning ==
Source of law and procedural posture: Oklahoma law defined the reach of the Oklahoma courts; the Due Process Clause supplied an independent federal ceiling. The Oklahoma Supreme Court was the final expositor of subsection 1701.03(a)(4), so certiorari brought the constitutional judgment to the U.S. Supreme Court. Because the case arrived through prohibition before trial, the record addressed forum affiliations rather than products-liability merits. The manufacturer and importer remained in the trial court, and the ruling concerned World-Wide and Seaway individually.
Minimum contacts and the two functions of due process: International Shoe requires a forum relationship consistent with fair play and substantial justice. The majority identified two functions for that requirement: protecting a defendant from distant or inconvenient litigation and maintaining the territorial allocation of coercive authority among coequal States. The latter function allows due process to withhold state power even where litigation burden is light and forum convenience is strong. 444 U.S. at 291-94.
Sequence of contacts and reasonableness: Forum interests enter the reasonableness inquiry through the forum's regulatory stake, the plaintiff's interest in convenient and effective relief, interstate judicial efficiency, and shared substantive policies. A defendant-created forum affiliation remains the predicate for exercising specific jurisdiction. Oklahoma's accident evidence, resident safety interest, and convenient location could inform fairness after that predicate; those interests could not create World-Wide's or Seaway's forum contact. 444 U.S. at 292-95.
Defendant-specific affiliations: Neither petitioner served or sought to serve the Oklahoma market. World-Wide's distribution territory ended with New York, New Jersey, and Connecticut; Seaway's retail sales occurred in Massena. The record showed no Oklahoma sales, services, agents, shipments, targeted advertisements, or indirect market cultivation by either company. The corporate relationships with Audi and Volkswagen were contractual, so the broader market presence of the manufacturer, importer, or service network did not become the contacts of these petitioners. 444 U.S. at 288-89, 295.
Operative foreseeability: The jurisdictional foreseeability described a legal consequence of the defendant's own forum-directed conduct: that conduct must make being haled into the forum's courts reasonably anticipatable. The physical likelihood that a mobile product will arrive in a State describes product movement. Here, the Robinsons' decision to transport the Audi supplied that movement, and Hanson assigned the unilateral activity to the purchasers. Petitioners' commercial conduct supplied no
Oklahoma-directed counterpart. 444 U.S. at 295-98.
Products distributed to a forum market: The majority distinguished consumer travel from a commercial effort to serve a forum. A manufacturer or distributor that directly or indirectly cultivates a State's market and expects consumers there to purchase its products receives predictable exposure to suit when the product causes injury there. The opinion cited Gray as an example of products delivered into commercial distribution with an expectation of forum purchase. World-Wide and Seaway had restricted their own selling territory to the tristate region, leaving Oklahoma outside the markets they served. 444 U.S. at 297-98.
Substantial-revenue theory: The Oklahoma court inferred substantial revenue from the mobility and retail value of automobiles used in Oklahoma. The Supreme Court required the revenue to stem from a constitutionally cognizable forum contact. Petitioners received their sales revenue from their own regional market and received no direct revenue from Oklahoma service centers. Any value attributable to nationwide travel or collateral service availability remained too attenuated to establish Oklahoma affiliation. 444 U.S. at
298-99.
Predictability and primary conduct: Purposeful availment gives commercial actors advance notice of where their activity creates exposure to suit. They can structure distribution territories, purchase insurance, price litigation risk, and withdraw from markets whose costs they reject. Jurisdiction tied to every destination a consumer might choose would make the consumer's travel decision define the seller's judicial exposure. The majority assigned that exposure to markets the seller itself served. 444 U.S. at 297.
Relationship to Pennoyer, International Shoe, McGee, and Hanson: Pennoyer supplied the territorial and interstate-sovereignty inheritance; International Shoe translated amenability into minimum contacts and fair play; McGee showed that one defendant-created, claim-producing forum relationship can suffice; Hanson required purposeful availment and assigned unilateral contacts to the person who created them. World-Wide synthesized those decisions into a defendant-directed affiliation requirement and treated the fairness factors as part of a structured analysis. 444 U.S. at 291-98.
Justice Brennan's dissent: Brennan read International Shoe as a fairness-and-reasonableness standard in which contacts give content to the inquiry alongside the forum's interest and actual defense burden. Oklahoma hosted the accident, injury evidence, emergency response, and likely witnesses. Petitioners benefited from the national highway and dealership system that made interstate automobile use valuable. Those concrete interests and benefits made Oklahoma adjudication fair in his analysis. 444 U.S. at 299-313.
Justice Marshall's dissent, joined by Justice Blackmun: Marshall attributed interstate effects to petitioners' deliberate participation in a nationwide automobile marketing and servicing system. World-Wide was one of seven regional Audi distributors, and Seaway sold a product designed for long-distance travel whose value depended partly on nationwide service. He viewed out-of-state operation as the product's intended commercial use and found that purposeful economic structure sufficient when the defect caused injury in
Oklahoma. 444 U.S. at 313-17.
Justice Blackmun's separate dissent: Blackmun emphasized the automobile as a distinct product whose ordinary function includes interstate movement. Oklahoma roads, police, accident investigation, and emergency aid supported that use and benefited professional distributors and dealers. He would have upheld jurisdiction over automobile professionals when the sold vehicle caused an Oklahoma accident, while leaving other product categories for their own factual analysis. 444 U.S. at 317-19.
Narrow holding and doctrinal handoff: The judgment addresses a local retailer and a regional distributor whose proved market activity stopped outside Oklahoma, coupled with one car carried into Oklahoma by its purchasers. The opinion also recognizes jurisdiction when a business itself serves the forum market and the distributed product causes injury there. Later product-jurisdiction disputes build on the boundary between a defendant's market-directed distribution and movement attributable to another actor.
