Definiteness and Preliminary Agreements: Supplement Provisions

Source: added-sources/Contracts_Definiteness_and_Preliminary_Agreements_Briefs.pdf, pages 1–2

== Sources ==
Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§ 27, 33, 34, 204, and 362, and UCC §§ 2-204(3) and 2-305. The syllabus assigns the supplement without naming sections; these are the provisions the assigned cases apply or that govern the same questions. The Restatement describes common-law rules and is persuasive authority unless a court adopts it; the UCC provisions govern sales of goods as enacted in each state, and courts sometimes apply them by analogy to other contracts, as the Ohio Supreme Court did in Oglebay Norton. §
2-204; § 2-305.
The Definiteness Requirement
Restatement § 33(1) provides that even a manifestation intended as an offer cannot be accepted so as to form a contract unless the terms of the contract are reasonably certain. Under § 33(2), terms are reasonably certain if they provide a basis for determining whether a breach has occurred and for giving an appropriate remedy.
Section 33(3) adds that leaving one or more terms open or uncertain may show that the parties did not intend their communications as an offer or an acceptance. Definiteness therefore does two jobs: it tests whether a court can enforce the bargain, and it serves as evidence of whether the parties meant to be bound at all. The comments recognize that the more terms the parties leave open, the less likely it is that they intended a binding agreement, and that a court may supply a reasonable price when the parties manifested an intention to conclude a contract but left price unsettled.
Several provisions reduce the demand for completeness. Section 34 treats terms as reasonably certain even when they let a party choose terms during performance, allows part performance to remove uncertainty and show that a bargain was formed, and permits action in reliance to make a contractual remedy appropriate even when uncertainty remains. Section 204 directs a court, when the parties have not agreed on a term essential to determining their rights and duties, to supply a term that is reasonable in the circumstances.
Section 362 sets the corresponding limit on equitable relief: specific performance or an injunction will not be granted unless the terms are certain enough to provide a basis for an appropriate order.
Open Terms in Sales of Goods: UCC §§ 2-204(3) and 2-305
Section 2-204(3) provides that a contract for sale does not fail for indefiniteness even though one or more terms are left open, if the parties intended to make a contract and there is a reasonably certain basis for giving an appropriate remedy. Section 2-305 applies that principle to price. Under § 2-305(1), the parties can conclude a contract even though the price is not settled, and the price is a reasonable price at the time for delivery if nothing is said about price, if the price is left to be agreed and the parties fail to agree, or if the price is to be fixed by an agreed market or other standard set or recorded by a third person and it is not so set or recorded. A price to be fixed by one party must be fixed in good faith, and if a price fails to be fixed through one party's fault, the other may treat the contract as cancelled or fix a reasonable price. Under §
2-305(4), however, if the parties intended not to be bound unless the price was fixed or agreed, and it was not, there is no contract.
The Code thus reverses the common-law presumption applied in Sun Printing: when price is left to future agreement, the question under § 2-305 is whether the parties intended to be bound, and if they did, the court supplies a reasonable price. Section 2-305(1)(c) also addresses the failure of an external pricing index, the problem in Oglebay Norton, where the published rate the parties relied on ceased to exist.
Preliminary Agreements and Written Memorials
Restatement § 27 provides that manifestations of assent that are sufficient to conclude a contract are not prevented from doing so because the parties also intend to prepare and adopt a written memorial of their agreement, although the circumstances may show that the agreements are only preliminary negotiations. The comments identify circumstances that bear on that question, including the extent of agreement, whether the contract is of a type usually put in writing, whether it needs a formal writing for its full expression, whether it has few or many details, the amount involved, and whether it is common or unusual. Teachers Insurance &
Annuity Ass'n v. Tribune builds on the same inquiry and adds a middle category: a preliminary commitment that binds the parties to negotiate open terms in good faith without binding them to the final transaction.
Reliance Without a Contract
When the parties never form a contract because terms remain open or because they did not intend to be bound, a party that relied on the other's assurances may still recover under Restatement § 90 if a promise foreseeably induced its action and injustice can be avoided only by enforcement; the remedy may be limited as justice requires. Cyberchron and Dixon apply that doctrine to assurances made during negotiations and limit recovery to reliance expenditures. Restitution for benefits conferred, sometimes pleaded as quantum meruit (recovery of the reasonable value of services or goods provided), is a separate route that depends on a benefit to the defendant rather than on a promise.

== Application ==
Begin by asking whether the parties intended to be bound, using their language, the context of negotiations, the number and importance of open terms, any partial performance, and the customs of the relevant market. If they intended a final contract, ask whether the terms are reasonably certain enough for a court to identify a breach and fashion a remedy, and whether a missing term can be supplied by § 204, § 2-305, or an agreed standard. If they intended only to commit to negotiate, analyze a duty of good-faith negotiation as in Tribune and Channel Home Centers. If neither a contract nor a negotiation commitment exists, consider promissory estoppel and restitution. The remedy follows the theory: expectation damages or specific performance for a complete contract, damages for breach of a duty to negotiate, and reliance damages under § 90.
