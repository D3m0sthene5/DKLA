Cohen v. Cowles Media Co.
501 U.S. 663 (1991), reversing 457 N.W.2d 199 (Minn. 1990) and remanding; on remand, 479 N.W.2d 387 (Minn. 1992), rehearing denied, 481 N.W.2d 840 (Minn. 1992). White, J., for the U.S. Supreme Court majority; Simonett, J., for the Minnesota Supreme Court on remand.
Source: added-sources/Contracts_Promissory_Estoppel_Restitution_Big_Brief.pdf, pages 5–7

== Parties ==
Plaintiff / U.S. Supreme Court petitioner: Dan Cohen, an experienced political and public-relations participant associated with Wheelock Whitney's Independent-Republican gubernatorial campaign. Defendants / respondents:
Cowles Media Company, publisher of the Minneapolis Star and Tribune, and Northwest Publications, Inc., publisher of the St. Paul Pioneer Press Dispatch. Reporters: Lori Sturdevant of the Star Tribune and Bill Salisbury of the Pioneer
Press, who promised anonymity and objected when editors overruled them. Subject of the records: Marlene Johnson,
Democratic-Farmer-Labor candidate for lieutenant governor.

== Procedural History ==
Cohen sued both publishers in Minnesota state court for breach of contract and fraudulent misrepresentation after the newspapers identified him and his employer fired him. The trial court rejected the publishers' First Amendment defense. A jury found liability on both theories, awarded $200,000 in compensatory damages jointly and severally, and awarded $250,000 in punitive damages against each newspaper on the fraud claim.
In a 2-1 decision, the Minnesota Court of Appeals held that Cohen had not proved fraud because the reporters intended to keep their promises when they made them. It vacated the two punitive awards, upheld contract liability, and affirmed the $200,000 compensatory award. Cohen v. Cowles Media Co., 445 N.W.2d 248 (Minn. Ct. App. 1989).
The Minnesota Supreme Court affirmed the fraud ruling and reversed the contract judgment. It concluded that the parties understood source confidentiality as a special ethical commitment rather than an arrangement intended to create ordinary contractual liability. During oral argument a justice raised estoppel. The court then considered promissory estoppel even though Cohen had not pleaded it, the jury had not tried it, and the parties had not briefed it. The court focused on Section 90's injustice element and held that judicial balancing of the promise against editorial interests would intrude on First Amendment freedoms. Cohen I, 457 N.W.2d 199 (Minn. 1990).
The U.S. Supreme Court granted certiorari, confirmed federal jurisdiction because the state court had actually decided a federal question, and reversed by a 5-4 vote. The Court held that the First Amendment permits application of a generally applicable promissory-estoppel rule to the press. It left Minnesota-law elements, the Minnesota Constitution, public policy, and the verdict's reinstatement to the state court on remand. Cohen, 501 U.S. 663 (1991).
On remand, the Minnesota Supreme Court exercised its interest-of-justice authority to decide promissory estoppel on the existing record, declined to create broader protection under the state press clause for this case, found the promise enforceable, and reinstated the $200,000 compensatory judgment. Cohen II, 479 N.W.2d 387 (Minn. 1992). Rehearing was denied at 481 N.W.2d 840.

== Material Facts ==
On October 27, 1982, during the closing days of Minnesota's gubernatorial election, Cohen separately approached
Sturdevant and Salisbury. He offered documents connected to a candidate only on the reporters' promises that his identity would remain anonymous, his name would stay out of related coverage, and they would refrain from investigating his own source. Each experienced reporter agreed and intended to honor the commitment. Neither reporter told Cohen that an editor could revoke the assurance.
Cohen disclosed copies of two public court records concerning Johnson. One showed three 1969 unlawful-assembly charges that were dismissed. The other showed a 1970 petit-theft conviction that was later vacated. The files did not contain the underlying circumstances. Johnson told the reporters that the first incident arose from a protest over minority hiring on municipal construction and that the second involved leaving a store with six dollars of sewing materials while distressed by her father's death.
The newspapers interviewed Johnson. The Star Tribune located the original files and learned that Gary Flakne, a
Whitney supporter, had retrieved records that had sat untouched for years; Flakne told a reporter that he obtained them for Cohen. Cohen had also told Sturdevant that he planned to offer the documents to other news organizations.
Both editorial staffs independently debated publication. Editors viewed the source's partisan identity and the late-election leak as part of the political story. They also believed a veiled attribution could cast suspicion on others, expected competing media to discover Cohen, and recognized that the Star Tribune had endorsed the Perpich-Johnson ticket. Other staff members urged compliance with the promise and proposed identifying the source only as someone close to the opposing campaign. Sturdevant and Salisbury objected strongly; Sturdevant withheld her byline.
On October 28 both newspapers published Cohen's name, identified his connection to Whitney's campaign, and reported campaign officials' denial of involvement. The Star Tribune also named the advertising firm that employed him. His employer fired him the same day. Cohen sought compensation for lost employment and diminished earning capacity. His claim addressed the economic consequences of the promise's breach rather than reputational or emotional injury from false reporting.
Trial testimony established that promising and protecting source anonymity was a common journalistic practice. The reporters maintained that their promises should have been honored. Editors described source protection as extremely important and could recall no comparable instance in which an editor overruled an anonymity promise. Cohen, who had journalism-related experience, relied on that established practice when he demanded and received confidentiality.
Cohen testified that he sought anonymity because he feared retaliation from media and political actors. His background included work as a lawyer, stockbroker, public-relations official, author, freelance newspaper columnist, campaign worker, candidate, and elected official. That experience informed both his understanding of source protection and the courts' treatment of his identity as political information.

== Issue ==
U.S. Supreme Court: Does the First Amendment bar recovery under a state's generally applicable promissory-estoppel law when newspapers break promises of source confidentiality and publish truthful information? Minnesota on remand: Does the record establish promissory estoppel under state law, and should the existing $200,000 verdict be reinstated?

== Holding ==
U.S. Supreme Court: The First Amendment permits application of Minnesota's generally applicable promissory-estoppel doctrine to the press. Judicial enforcement constitutes state action, yet the doctrine regulates promises across ordinary transactions and places no press-specific burden. The Court reversed and remanded without reinstating damages. Minnesota Supreme Court: The reporters gave a clear promise designed to induce disclosure;
Cohen relied by delivering the records; publication caused his job loss; and the circumstances required a remedy to prevent injustice. The court reinstated the $200,000 compensatory judgment.

== Reasoning ==
Fraud claim: Fraud based on a future act requires proof that the speaker lacked an intention to perform when the representation was made. Sturdevant and Salisbury intended to protect Cohen and objected when their editors overruled them. That undisputed intent defeated fraudulent misrepresentation and removed the only basis for punitive damages.
Contract claim in Cohen I: Minnesota treated the reporters' assurance as clear yet understood the source-reporter relationship as an ethical commitment outside ordinary legal contracting. The court emphasized oral and often open-ended confidentiality, editorial evaluation after information arrives, uncertain duration, and the participants' lack of intent to create standard contractual liability. It therefore used promissory estoppel's injustice inquiry to assess the promise's substance and setting.
Federal jurisdiction: The publishers argued that the promissory-estoppel issue originated in the state supreme court and rested on state law. The U.S. Supreme Court had jurisdiction because the Minnesota court actually decided the federal constitutional question and clearly grounded its result on the First Amendment. The publishers had also invoked the First Amendment throughout the litigation.
State action: Promissory estoppel creates obligations enforceable through the official power of state courts. A damages judgment therefore activates Fourteenth Amendment state-action analysis and subjects the application of state law to
First Amendment limits.
Generally applicable law: Minnesota's doctrine governs promises in citizens' daily transactions and does not target press content, speakers, or publishers. The Court placed it with labor, antitrust, tax, copyright, subpoena, and similar rules that bind the press on the same terms as other actors. Its application requires newspapers to bear the legal consequences of commitments they choose to make.
Acquisition and self-imposed obligation: The newspapers obtained Cohen's identity together with a promise restricting its use. The majority treated resulting damages as a cost tied to the manner in which the information was acquired. The parties themselves defined the confidentiality obligation; the state supplied a general enforcement rule.
Truthful-publication precedents: The publishers relied on cases requiring an interest of the highest order before government penalizes publication of lawfully obtained truthful information. The majority distinguished those decisions because the challenged liability arose from a generally applicable promise doctrine and the newspapers obtained access through the assurances at issue.
Nature of Cohen's damages: Cohen sought economic losses caused when breach of the anonymity promise cost him his job and reduced earning capacity. The claim did not seek redress for a false statement, injured feelings, or reputational harm. The majority therefore declined to import the constitutional elements governing defamation and related speech torts.
Federal disposition: The Supreme Court decided the federal barrier and stopped there. State courts retained responsibility for the elements of promissory estoppel, any broader state constitutional protection, public-policy defenses, and an appropriate remedy. That division explains why the federal Court reversed while declining Cohen's request for immediate reinstatement of the verdict.
Blackmun dissent: Justice Blackmun, joined by Justices Marshall and Souter, treated the liability as a direct burden on publication of truthful political information. He applied the highest-order-interest standard from the truthful-publication cases and found Minnesota's interest in enforcing this promise insufficient. He also viewed Hustler Magazine v.
Falwell as showing that a generally applicable cause of action remains subject to speech protections when publication itself triggers liability.
Souter dissent: Justice Souter, joined by Justices Marshall, Blackmun, and O'Connor, favored case-specific constitutional balancing. Cohen's partisan identity carried substantial value to voters evaluating an election-eve leak. In his view, the public interest in unfettered publication outweighed the state's promise-enforcement interest on these facts, while other confidentiality settings could support liability.
Late use of promissory estoppel on remand: Minnesota ordinarily holds parties to the theories tried. It used its appellate interest-of-justice authority because enforceability of the anonymity promise had driven the case from the beginning, the trial evidence addressed the same facts, contract and promissory estoppel were closely related, both sides had fully briefed the doctrine after remand, and the publishers had a fair opportunity to contest the factual record.
Minnesota Constitution and public policy: Minnesota's press clause has distinct wording and can receive broader construction. The court declined that extension in this first-impression setting. It also required a clear public-policy basis before invalidating an enforceable promise and found Cohen's identity short of the grave importance needed to override this commitment. The record implicated no recognized emergency such as correcting a source's own misstatement or averting substantial libel exposure.
Section 90 elements on remand: The promise was unambiguous. The reporters offered anonymity to obtain the documents, so inducement was intended. Cohen delivered them because of the assurance, and disclosure caused his firing. The injustice issue was a legal policy question for the court. Longstanding newsroom practice, the parties' shared recognition of confidentiality's importance, the reporters' objections, and the absence of a compelling need to identify
Cohen supported enforcement.
Damages: Section 90 permits a remedy calibrated to justice. The jury had received a foreseeability instruction allowing losses that arise naturally from breach or from special circumstances within the parties' contemplation. Evidence supported $200,000 in employment and earning-capacity losses. Because that instruction and record fit promissory-estoppel relief, a new damages trial would duplicate completed work.
Doctrinal significance: Cohen shows promissory estoppel operating as a generally applicable state obligation while
Section 90's injustice requirement performs case-specific policy work. The procedural arc also separates constitutional permission from state-law entitlement: the U.S. Supreme Court removed the federal barrier; Minnesota then supplied liability and remedy.
