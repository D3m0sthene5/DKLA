Fuentes v. Shevin
Fuentes v. Shevin, 407 U.S. 67 (1972), reh'g denied, 409 U.S. 902 (1972). Supreme Court of the United
States, argued November 9, 1971, decided June 12, 1972, together with Parham v. Cortese. Justice Stewart wrote for a four-Justice majority joined by Justices Douglas, Brennan, and Marshall. Justice White dissented, joined by Chief Justice Burger and Justice Blackmun. Justices Powell and Rehnquist, who joined the Court after argument, took no part. The casebook presents the case in Note 2 at page 280. Full judicial opinion.
Source: added-sources/CivPro_Notice_Service_and_Hearing_Briefs.pdf, pages 27–29

== Parties ==
Margarita Fuentes, a Florida consumer whose household goods were seized, was the appellant; Robert
Shevin, Florida's Attorney General, defended the Florida statute, and Firestone Tire and Rubber Company, the seller, was also an appellee. In the companion Pennsylvania case, consumers whose goods had been seized under Pennsylvania's replevin statute challenged it against state officials and the creditors. The consumers sought declarations that the statutes were unconstitutional and injunctions against their enforcement.

== Material Facts ==
Fuentes bought a gas stove and a service policy from Firestone under a conditional sales contract, and later a stereophonic phonograph on the same terms. The total cost was about $500 plus a financing charge of more than $100. Firestone retained title, but Fuentes was entitled to possession unless she defaulted. She made installment payments for more than a year. With about $200 remaining, a dispute arose over the servicing of the stove. Firestone sued in small-claims court for repossession of the stove and stereo, claiming she had refused to make her remaining payments. At the same time, and before Fuentes had received the summons,
Firestone obtained a writ of replevin, and a deputy sheriff and a Firestone agent seized the goods from her home. 407 U.S. at 70-71.
Florida's statute allowed a writ to issue on the applicant's complaint and bond alone. The applicant had to allege, in conclusory fashion, that it was lawfully entitled to possession and post a bond for double the value of the property. The clerk issued the writ, which commanded the officer to seize the goods and to summon the defendant to answer; if the goods were in a dwelling and not surrendered, the officer could break the building open. The officer held the goods for three days, during which the defendant could reclaim them by posting its own bond for double their value; otherwise they went to the applicant pending final judgment.
Pennsylvania's statute was more summary: the applicant needed only to file an affidavit of the value of the property and a bond, did not have to file any suit, and the person whose goods were seized had to sue to obtain a hearing. Id. at 73-78.
The Pennsylvania appellants included buyers of household goods under installment contracts whose sellers obtained writs by claiming default, and Rosa Washington, whose former husband, a local deputy sheriff, obtained a writ to seize their son's clothes, furniture, and toys during a custody dispute. Id. at 71-72.

== Procedural History ==
Fuentes sued in federal district court, challenging Florida's prejudgment replevin procedures under the Due
Process Clause. The Pennsylvania appellants brought a similar suit. In each case a three-judge district court, a special panel then required for suits seeking to enjoin state statutes on constitutional grounds, upheld the statute: Fuentes v. Faircloth, 317 F. Supp. 954 (S.D. Fla. 1970), and Epps v. Cortese, 326 F. Supp. 127 (E.D.
Pa. 1971). The consumers appealed directly to the Supreme Court. Id. at 71-72.

== Issue ==
Do statutes that allow a private party to obtain a writ of replevin and have state officers seize goods from another person's possession, on an ex parte application and a bond, without notice or an opportunity for a hearing before the seizure, violate the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment?

== Holding ==
Yes. The Florida and Pennsylvania statutes are unconstitutional insofar as they deny the right to an opportunity to be heard before chattels are taken from their possessor. To serve its purpose, the hearing must come at a time when the deprivation can still be prevented. A temporary, nonfinal deprivation is a deprivation; a buyer's possessory interest in goods purchased under a conditional sales contract is property even though the seller retains title; and due process protects property generally, not only necessities. Seizure without a prior hearing is permitted only in extraordinary situations, which were absent where only private gain was at stake. The printed contract clauses permitting the seller to retake the goods did not waive the right to a prior hearing. Id. at 80-96.

== Judgment ==
The Supreme Court vacated the judgments of both three-judge district courts and remanded for further proceedings consistent with its opinion.

== Reasoning ==
The Court first described the procedure's practical effect. At the moment the defendant receives the complaint seeking repossession, the property has already been seized, without prior notice or any chance to challenge the writ. The defendant will eventually have a hearing as defendant in the repossession action and can recover the goods in the meantime only by posting a bond for double their value. The process also lacked judicial supervision: private parties, serving their own advantage, could unilaterally invoke state power to take goods from another, with no state official deciding whether to issue the writ, reviewing the basis of the claim, or evaluating the need for immediate seizure. The State, the Court wrote, acted largely in the dark. Id.
at 73-75, 93.
The purpose of the right to be heard is to protect a person's use and possession of property from arbitrary encroachment and to minimize substantively unfair or mistaken deprivations, a danger that is especially great when the State seizes goods simply on a private party's application. That purpose is served only if the hearing comes when the deprivation can still be prevented. A later hearing can return the goods and may award damages, but it cannot undo the fact that an arbitrary taking has occurred. The Florida and Pennsylvania safeguards, a bond, a conclusory allegation, and exposure to damages, tested no more than the strength of the applicant's own belief in its rights, and a bond's minimal deterrent effect was no substitute for an informed evaluation by a neutral official. Id. at 80-84.
The Court rejected three arguments for limiting the right. First, the opportunity to post a counterbond within three days did not make the seizure a non-deprivation, because a temporary, nonfinal deprivation of property is a deprivation under the Fourteenth Amendment. Second, although the sellers retained title, the buyers had a possessory interest in the goods, dearly bought through installment payments and protected by contract, and that interest was property. Third, the Court refused to confine due process to necessities such as wages; the
Fourteenth Amendment speaks of property generally, and courts should not decide which consumer goods are important enough to protect. Id. at 84-90.
Extraordinary situations can justify seizure without a prior hearing, but they must be truly unusual. In the cases allowing it, the seizure was directly necessary to secure an important governmental or general public interest; there was a special need for very prompt action; and the State kept strict control over its monopoly of legitimate force, with a government official deciding under a narrowly drawn statute that the seizure was necessary in the particular case. Examples included tax collection, war needs, bank failures, and misbranded drugs and contaminated food. No such interest justified seizing household goods when no more than private gain was directly at stake. Id. at 90-93.
Finally, the consumers had not waived their rights. The contracts provided that on default the seller "may take back," "may retake," or "may repossess" the goods, but they said nothing about waiving a prior hearing or about the process, whether final judgment, self-help, or prejudgment replevin with or without a hearing, by which repossession would occur. A waiver of constitutional rights must, at the least, be clear. The Court contrasted D.H. Overmyer Co. v. Frick Co., which upheld a cognovit note negotiated between corporations with the advice of counsel; here there was no bargaining over terms, and the parties were far from equal in bargaining power. Id. at 94-96.
The Court stressed the limits of its holding. It did not decide the form of the required hearing, which may vary and is a subject for legislation, and it did not question the power of a state to seize goods before final judgment; it held only that the seizure must follow a hearing that tests at least the probable validity of the underlying claim. Id. at 96-97.

== Separate Opinions ==
Justice White, joined by Chief Justice Burger and Justice Blackmun, dissented. He emphasized that the creditor also has a property interest in goods bought on installment, including a security interest that can be impaired if the goods are hidden, damaged, or depreciate while the debtor, who has stopped paying, keeps them. Sellers have strong economic reasons not to repossess without a real default, and in most cases the default is undisputed, so a prior hearing would add cost and delay with little benefit. He would have deferred to the legislative judgments reflected in state law and in the Uniform Commercial Code's rules on secured transactions. He also predicted that creditors could avoid the decision by stating in their credit instruments that they may retake possession without a hearing, so that the result would have little impact and represented "no more than ideological tinkering with state law." Id. at 97-103 (White, J., dissenting).

== Notes ==
Fuentes was the high point of the prior-hearing requirement, and the four-to-three vote with two Justices absent made it vulnerable. Two years later, after Justices Powell and Rehnquist had joined the Court,
Mitchell v. W.T. Grant Co. upheld Louisiana's sequestration procedure, stressing judicial supervision, sworn and specific allegations, the creditor's vendor's lien, a bond, and an immediate post-seizure hearing. Justice
Stewart's dissent in Mitchell argued that the decision effectively overruled Fuentes. Doehr reconciled the line through a Mathews-style balance in which a prior hearing is the default and the plaintiff's pre-existing interest, exigency, and procedural safeguards can justify postponing it.
Two further limits matter. Fuentes concerns seizures carried out by state officers under state procedures;
private self-help repossession under Article 9 of the Uniform Commercial Code, when conducted without a state actor, generally falls outside the Due Process Clause, compare Flagg Bros., Inc. v. Brooks, 436 U.S. 149 (1978). The waiver analysis also connects to Szukhent and Overmyer: a printed form can appoint an agent for service, but it waives a constitutional hearing right only if the waiver is clear, and bargaining power bears on enforcement.
