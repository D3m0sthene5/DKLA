Burnham v. Superior Court of California, County of Marin
Burnham v. Superior Court of California, County of Marin, 495 U.S. 604 (1990). United States Supreme
Court, decided May 29, 1990. All nine Justices agreed to affirm, but no single rationale commanded five votes. Justice Scalia announced the judgment. Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justice Kennedy joined his opinion; Justice White joined Parts I, II-A, II-B, and II-C and wrote separately. Justice Brennan concurred in the judgment with Justices Marshall, Blackmun, and O'Connor. Justice Stevens wrote a separate concurrence in the judgment.

== Parties ==
Dennis Burnham, a New Jersey resident, was the husband and defendant in the California divorce proceeding. He petitioned for relief from the California court's assertion of personal jurisdiction and later sought Supreme Court review. Francie Burnham, his estranged wife, had moved to California with their children and filed the California action. The Superior Court appears in the caption because Dennis pursued extraordinary relief directed at the trial court's jurisdictional ruling, rather than an ordinary appeal from a final divorce decree.

== Material Facts ==
The Burnhams married in West Virginia in 1976 and moved to New Jersey in 1977. Their two children were born there. In July 1987, the couple agreed to separate. Francie planned to move to California with the children, and the spouses agreed that she would file for divorce on irreconcilable-differences grounds. In
October, Dennis instead filed a New Jersey divorce complaint alleging desertion. He did not obtain issuance of a summons or attempt to serve Francie in that proceeding.
Francie filed for divorce in California in early January 1988 after Dennis did not comply with their earlier agreement. Later that month, Dennis traveled to southern California on business and then went to the San
Francisco Bay Area to visit his children. He took the older child to San Francisco for the weekend. On
January 24, when he returned the child to Francie's home, he was personally served with the California summons and divorce petition. He then returned to New Jersey.
The service occurred while Dennis was voluntarily and knowingly within California. His visits were brief, and the marital history was principally connected to New Jersey. The constitutional issue was presented as whether in-state service could support jurisdiction even though the suit was unrelated to his California activities. The case did not involve a defendant whose physical presence had been procured by fraud or force. 495 U.S. at 607-08.

== Procedural History ==
Dennis made a special appearance in the California Superior Court and moved to quash service of process for lack of personal jurisdiction. He argued that his only California contacts were a few short trips for business and to see his children, insufficient to support the divorce action under minimum-contacts principles. His special appearance sought to preserve that objection while asking the court to resolve its power over him.
The Superior Court denied the motion. Dennis then sought mandamus relief in the California Court of
Appeal for the First Appellate District. That court denied relief, holding that personal service on a defendant physically present within the state furnished a valid basis for personal jurisdiction. The United States
Supreme Court granted certiorari to review the Court of Appeal's judgment.
The Supreme Court reviewed the constitutional validity of this basis of jurisdiction. The case had not reached it as review of a final ruling on marital status, property division, support, or custody. The Court also did not decide the priority or ultimate effect of Dennis's unserved New Jersey complaint. The operative question was whether California could proceed against him on the strength of the personal service made during his visit. 495 U.S. at 607-08.

== Issue ==
Does the Fourteenth Amendment permit a state court to exercise personal jurisdiction over a nonresident individual personally served while voluntarily and temporarily in the state, when the lawsuit does not arise from the activities that brought him there? After International Shoe and Shaffer, must the plaintiff separately establish claim-related minimum contacts despite valid in-state personal service?

== Holding ==
Personal service on Dennis while he was voluntarily present in California constitutionally supported jurisdiction in this case, even though the visit was temporary and the suit unrelated to his local activities. The
Court unanimously sustained that result. The opinions did not produce a single majority explanation of why transient, often called tag, jurisdiction satisfies due process. Scalia relied on the enduring historical rule;
Brennan independently evaluated present fairness; White and Stevens offered their own grounds. 495 U.S. at
619, 628-40.

== Judgment ==
The Supreme Court affirmed the California Court of Appeal. The denial of Dennis's motion to quash and his request for extraordinary relief remained in place, permitting the California proceeding to continue with personal jurisdiction over him. The ruling did not itself dissolve the marriage or determine the substantive rights of either spouse.

== Reasoning ==
Scalia began with the long acceptance of jurisdiction over individuals served within a state's territory. He examined English and American sources, emphasizing the rule's acceptance around the adoption of the
Fourteenth Amendment and its continued use by American courts. For him, an established and still-accepted method helped define the traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice invoked in International
Shoe. A brief visit did not defeat a valid personal service, and a claim did not need to arise from that visit.
Scalia understood International Shoe as expanding the circumstances in which courts could exercise jurisdiction over defendants absent from the forum. Minimum contacts provided a means of evaluating an absent defendant's relationship to the state when physical service within it was unavailable. The decision did not make actual presence insufficient merely because it ceased to be necessary in other situations. A rule developed to justify jurisdiction over absent defendants therefore did not eliminate the traditional rule governing a defendant actually served in the state.
Scalia also read Shaffer in its context. That case involved absent individuals whose unrelated property was assigned a forum location by statute. Its extension of minimum contacts to quasi in rem proceedings prevented indirect adjudication against absent defendants on a basis that would not support a direct personal action. Scalia did not read its general language as compelling identical treatment of individuals personally present and individuals reached only through unrelated assets. This part of his discussion, and his response to
Brennan's proposed fairness inquiry, received only three votes.
Scalia criticized an additional case-by-case fairness test as an uncertain means of resolving a preliminary issue. If every valid in-state service required further assessment of how fair a particular visit made litigation, the parties could incur substantial jurisdictional litigation before reaching the dispute itself. The traditional rule supplied an ascertainable event and predictable consequence. His opinion nevertheless concerned the accepted rule of voluntary presence and service, not every imaginable method of bringing a defendant physically within a state.
The opposing rationale came from Brennan's concurrence. He read International Shoe and Shaffer as requiring every jurisdictional rule, including an old one, to satisfy contemporary due process. Historical pedigree was relevant but not conclusive. A rule could persist for many years and still need a present justification. He nevertheless concluded that transient jurisdiction ordinarily met the fairness standard because voluntary visitors had notice of the rule and enjoyed real benefits from the forum's laws and services. Thus, the methodological disagreement did not change Dennis's result. 495 U.S. at 610-27, 629-39.

== Separate Opinions ==
Justice White joined substantial portions of Scalia's opinion and agreed that personal service was enough here. He would not invalidate the traditionally accepted rule without a showing that it was so arbitrary and unreasonable in its general operation that it violated due process. Until such a showing, he saw no need to entertain endless assertions of unfairness in individual cases, at least when the defendant's presence was intentional. He did not join Scalia's full discussion of Shaffer or his attack on Brennan's approach. 495 U.S.
at 628.
Justice Brennan, joined by Marshall, Blackmun, and O'Connor, gave tradition weight because it supplied fair warning, rather than because it automatically established constitutionality. A visitor used the state's roads, received police and emergency protection, participated in its economy, and could invoke its courts. Allowing a visitor to sue while exempting him from being sued there would produce an asymmetry. Modern travel and communications reduced defense burdens, and devices such as forum non conveniens could address exceptional inconvenience. Because Dennis was voluntarily and knowingly in California when served,
Brennan found the exercise of jurisdiction fair. His concurrence did not hold that historical practice was irrelevant; it required history to work with present fairness. 495 U.S. at 628-40.
Justice Stevens found the historical evidence, the continuing consensus, fairness, and common sense collectively sufficient to affirm. He regarded the broad disagreement between the other opinions as unnecessary to resolve the actual case. His concurrence supplied a ninth vote for the judgment without making either Scalia's historical rationale or Brennan's fairness rationale an opinion of the Court. 495 U.S. at
640.

== Notes ==
The common result is narrower than a claim that any physical connection always resolves personal jurisdiction. Dennis himself, an individual, was served while voluntarily in the forum. The opinion does not hold that serving a corporate employee who happens to be visiting a state automatically makes the corporation subject to all-purpose jurisdiction there. It also does not decide every immunity, fraud, coercion, or involuntary-presence problem that can arise in service practice.
The case distinguishes a rule's validity from agreement about its constitutional foundation. The judgment is unanimous, while the principal explanations are pluralities or concurrences. In Mallory, Gorsuch later invokes the continued validity of traditional jurisdictional bases to support registration-based consent, while
Barrett disputes the analogy between personal service on an individual and a corporation's mandatory registration. Both arguments depend on reading Burnham with its actual voting alignment and factual limits in view.
