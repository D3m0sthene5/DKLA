Insurance Corp. of Ireland, Ltd. v. Compagnie des Bauxites de Guinée
Insurance Corp. of Ireland, Ltd. v. Compagnie des Bauxites de Guinée, 456 U.S. 694 (1982). United States
Supreme Court, decided June 1, 1982. Justice White wrote for eight Justices. Justice Powell concurred in the judgment. The Court reviewed whether a district court could deem jurisdictional facts established as a sanction for disobeying discovery orders.
Full judicial opinion.

== Parties ==
Compagnie des Bauxites de Guinée, or CBG, was the plaintiff and Supreme Court respondent. It was incorporated in Delaware, maintained its principal place of business in Guinea, and operated bauxite mines and processing facilities there. Fourteen foreign excess insurers, including Insurance Corporation of Ireland, were the Supreme Court petitioners. Other insurers had participated below, but four did not contest personal jurisdiction and three obtained dismissal in the court of appeals. The primary insurer, Insurance Company of
North America, or INA, did not challenge the district court's personal or subject-matter jurisdiction.

== Material Facts ==
CBG was 49 percent owned by the Republic of Guinea and 51 percent by Halco, which operated in
Pennsylvania and provided administrative services including procurement of insurance. Halco instructed its broker to obtain $20 million in business-interruption coverage for CBG's Guinean operations. INA supplied the first $10 million. Twenty-one foreign insurers subscribed to portions of the next $10 million through the
London market. The excess insurers adopted INA's policy terms as applicable rather than issuing wholly separate policies.
CBG alleged that mechanical problems interrupted its operations and caused losses exceeding the primary coverage. The insurers refused indemnification. Because the excess insurers disputed personal jurisdiction in
Pennsylvania, CBG sought records showing the nature and extent of their Pennsylvania insurance business.
The relevant information was largely within the insurers' or their brokers' control. The dispute over those records became the basis for the sanction ultimately reviewed by the Supreme Court. 456 U.S. at 696-99.

== Procedural History ==
In December 1975, CBG sued in the United States District Court for the Western District of Pennsylvania, invoking diversity jurisdiction. One count concerned INA and another the excess insurers. The excess insurers raised lack of personal jurisdiction in their answer and later included that objection in a motion for summary judgment. They also sought dismissal on forum non conveniens grounds. The jurisdictional objection was therefore preserved; the later loss of the defense resulted from discovery misconduct rather than a failure to raise it initially.
CBG requested insurance-policy records beginning in 1976. The insurers objected that production would be burdensome. After the court overruled the objections, CBG narrowed its request to policies delivered in
Pennsylvania or covering Pennsylvania risks. The insurers then said that brokers in London held the relevant documents. In July 1978, the court directed them to request the material from those brokers and allowed ninety days for production. It extended that deadline in November. The insurers offered access in London to roughly four million files rather than producing the information the orders required.
In December 1978, the district judge found that the insurers had made no conscientious effort to comply, allowed another sixty days, and expressly warned that continued noncompliance would result in a finding of personal jurisdiction under Rule 37(b)(2)(A), as then numbered. The judge also allowed them to produce statistics or other information showing the absence of jurisdiction. When they still failed to comply, the court imposed the sanction on April 19, 1979, treating Pennsylvania business contacts sufficient for personal jurisdiction as established. It independently found sufficient contacts on the existing record and inferred submission to jurisdiction from adoption of the Pennsylvania insurance contract. It also enjoined a parallel
English action that the excess insurers had recently filed.
The Third Circuit affirmed the jurisdictional ruling as to the noncomplying insurers solely on the sanction ground, 651 F.2d 877 (1981). It reversed as to three insurers that had complied with discovery and whose contacts were insufficient, and it reversed the injunction against the English action. The Supreme Court granted the insurers' cross-petition, limiting review to the validity of the Rule 37 sanction. It distinguished the legal permissibility of such a sanction from whether this particular order was an abuse of discretion. The insurance-coverage merits, injunction, and dismissals of the three complying insurers were not the question accepted for decision. 456 U.S. at 698-701 & nn.1, 5-8.

== Issue ==
May a federal district court treat facts supporting personal jurisdiction as established when defendants disobey discovery orders directed to those facts, even though the defendants are appearing to contest jurisdiction? Did the district court abuse its discretion by imposing that consequence after repeated extensions and explicit warnings?

== Holding ==
Personal jurisdiction protects an individual liberty interest and may be waived, consented to, or lost through the legal consequences of litigation conduct. Rule 37 may therefore establish jurisdictional facts as a discovery sanction without violating due process. The sanction must be just and specifically related to the claim or issue that the discovery order addressed. Here, treating the withheld jurisdictional facts as established satisfied those requirements, and the district court did not abuse its discretion. The rule is quoted under its numbering at the time of the decision. 456 U.S. at 701-09.

== Judgment ==
The Supreme Court affirmed the Third Circuit on the question before it. The sanction supporting personal jurisdiction over the petitioning insurers remained valid, allowing the coverage litigation against them to proceed. The Court did not award CBG the policy proceeds, affirm the separate injunction issue, or disturb the dismissals of insurers outside the granted question.

== Reasoning ==
The insurers argued that a court lacking personal jurisdiction cannot make orders binding them and therefore cannot acquire jurisdiction by punishing their refusal to obey. The Court rejected that circular account. A defendant who appears to challenge jurisdiction submits that question for adjudication and must comply with lawful procedures for resolving it. Such an appearance does not surrender the objection simply by being made; it commits the defendant to the court's determination of that objection through the applicable process.
The Court distinguished personal from subject-matter jurisdiction. Federal subject-matter jurisdiction rests on constitutional and statutory allocations of judicial authority and cannot be supplied by party agreement or forfeited merely by failing to object. Personal jurisdiction protects a defendant's individual due-process interest. It may be accepted in a contract, waived by failure to raise the defense properly, or established through procedural consequences attached to the defendant's conduct. The existence of federalism concerns did not make the individual protection categorically incapable of waiver.
A jurisdictional sanction also had an evidentiary basis. Under Hammond Packing, a refusal to produce relevant evidence may support a presumption that the resisting party's position lacks merit. That differs from stripping a party of a defense solely to punish unrelated misconduct, as in Hovey v. Elliott. CBG sought evidence of Pennsylvania contacts; the insurers blocked that evidence; the sanction established those very contacts. The consequence was tailored to the factual issue frustrated by the disobedience.
The record supported the sanction's justice. The court repeatedly extended deadlines, the insurers promised compliance and failed to perform, CBG's jurisdictional allegations were substantial rather than speculative, and the judge gave unmistakable warning of the consequence. The insurers could have complied or provided information disproving the alleged contacts. They did neither. Review asked whether the district court exceeded its discretion, rather than whether the appellate judges would have selected exactly the same sanction in the first instance.
The Court also explained the risks of alternative procedural choices. A defendant may stay out of the litigation, risk a default judgment, and later contest personal jurisdiction in a collateral proceeding. A defendant who actually litigates and loses the jurisdictional issue is ordinarily bound by that determination and cannot relitigate it elsewhere. This finality rule does not mean that ignoring suit is costless: if jurisdiction exists, the default can stand and the merits opportunity may have been lost. 456 U.S. at 701-09.

== Separate Opinions ==
Justice Powell agreed with affirmance but preferred a narrower rationale. CBG had already made a prima facie showing of minimum contacts, which justified discovery; the insurers' refusal then permitted that showing to be treated as sufficient. He worried that the majority's broad discussion of personal rights and waiver obscured statutory limits on a federal court's jurisdiction and the role of state long-arm law. He would not treat Rule 37 as an independent grant of jurisdiction or permit jurisdictional discovery based on wholly speculative allegations. 456 U.S. at 709-17.

== Notes ==
This case concerns the consequences of failing to provide evidence needed to decide jurisdiction. It does not authorize a court to invent subject-matter jurisdiction or declare personal jurisdiction as punishment for any misconduct. Its individual-right rationale connects to the contractual consent in The Bremen and Carnival
Cruise and to Justice Jackson's concurrence in Mallory. A preserved jurisdictional objection still carries obligations to participate properly in its adjudication.
