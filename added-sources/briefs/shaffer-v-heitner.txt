Shaffer v. Heitner
Shaffer v. Heitner, 433 U.S. 186 (1977). United States Supreme Court, decided June 24, 1977. Justice
Marshall delivered the Court's opinion. Chief Justice Burger and Justices Stewart, White, Blackmun, and
Powell joined it; Justice Brennan joined Parts I-III but dissented from the application and result. Justice
Powell also concurred separately, and Justice Stevens concurred in the judgment. Justice Rehnquist did not participate. The case reached the Court by appeal from the Delaware Supreme Court under the jurisdictional statute then in effect.

== Parties ==
Arnold Heitner, a nonresident of Delaware who owned one share of Greyhound Corporation stock, brought a shareholder derivative action. He was the plaintiff and Supreme Court appellee. A derivative plaintiff seeks recovery on a claim belonging to the corporation, so the alleged corporate injury, rather than a separate individual injury to Heitner's single share, was the basis of the lawsuit. He named Greyhound Corporation, its wholly owned subsidiary Greyhound Lines, Inc., and twenty-eight current or former officers and directors as defendants. Twenty-one individuals whose property was sequestered challenged jurisdiction and were the
Supreme Court appellants.
Greyhound Corporation was incorporated in Delaware and headquartered in Phoenix, Arizona. Greyhound
Lines was incorporated in California and also headquartered in Phoenix. The individual defendants were not
Delaware residents. The corporate parent's Delaware incorporation supplied the link used to assign a
Delaware location to the individuals' shares, but it did not automatically place the individual officers and directors within Delaware's personal jurisdiction.

== Material Facts ==
Heitner alleged that the officers and directors breached their duties to Greyhound by causing it and its subsidiary to engage in conduct that led to a substantial private antitrust judgment and criminal contempt fines. The underlying activities occurred in Oregon. The antitrust judgment exceeded $13 million, and the fines totaled $600,000. The derivative action sought to hold the fiduciaries responsible for those corporate losses. It did not dispute ownership of the defendants' stock or seek to establish rights in the stock itself.
Delaware law provided a sequestration procedure under which a court could seize a nonresident defendant's property in the state to compel a personal appearance. A separate statute deemed stock in a Delaware corporation located in Delaware for attachment and jurisdictional purposes, even when the owner and stock certificates were elsewhere. Heitner used these provisions to reach the defendants' shares, options, and related interests. The sequestrator placed stop-transfer orders on Greyhound's books covering approximately
82,000 shares owned by nineteen defendants and options owned by two others. None of the share certificates was shown to be physically present in Delaware.
The statutory mechanism ordinarily released the property once the defendant made a general appearance, unless the plaintiff showed a sufficient reason to continue the seizure. A defendant could challenge jurisdiction by special appearance, but could not defend the merits while retaining only a limited exposure to the attached property. That feature placed pressure on the individuals to submit to full personal jurisdiction in order to protect their assets. 433 U.S. at 189-95 & nn.2-9, 12.

== Procedural History ==
On May 22, 1974, Heitner filed his derivative complaint in the Delaware Court of Chancery for New Castle
County and simultaneously requested sequestration under title 10, §366 of the Delaware Code. The court issued the order that day. The defendants received notice by certified mail and newspaper publication.
Twenty-one individuals entered special appearances, moved to quash service, and sought to vacate the sequestration. They argued that the ex parte seizure lacked adequate procedural safeguards, that the property was not properly attachable in Delaware, and that they lacked sufficient Delaware contacts under
International Shoe.
The Court of Chancery rejected the challenges. It viewed sequestration primarily as a means of compelling appearance, rather than merely preserving assets pending a merits decision. It found the statutory location of the stock sufficient for quasi in rem jurisdiction and treated the procedure's purpose and limits as satisfying due process. The Delaware Supreme Court affirmed, 361 A.2d 225 (Del. 1976). It expressly reasoned that
International Shoe's contacts analysis did not govern because jurisdiction rested on property located in
Delaware rather than prior contacts of the defendants with the state.
The United States Supreme Court noted probable jurisdiction and took the appeal before a trial on the derivative claims. In footnote 12, it explained why the state judgment was sufficiently final for federal review. The federal jurisdictional question had been conclusively resolved in the state system, while further litigation would force the individuals either to accept a default or to appear generally and defend on the merits. A later merits victory could prevent further review even though an erroneous jurisdictional ruling would have required a trial that should never have occurred. Applying the pragmatic finality approach of
Cox Broadcasting, the Court treated the judgment as reviewable under then-existing §1257.
The Supreme Court resolved the case on adjudicatory jurisdiction and found it unnecessary to decide the separate challenge to the procedural safeguards surrounding the seizure. Actual notice was not disputed. The legal issue was whether Delaware could bind these individuals at all on the asserted basis, even after properly informing them that suit had been filed. 433 U.S. at 189-95, 196 n.12, 213 n.40.

== Issue ==
May a state obtain jurisdiction over nonresident individuals on an unrelated claim solely by declaring their stock to be located in the state and seizing it? Must the standards of International Shoe apply to in rem and quasi in rem jurisdiction, and did the defendants' stock ownership or corporate positions supply adequate contacts in this case?

== Holding ==
Assertions of state-court jurisdiction over property must be evaluated under International Shoe and its progeny. A proceeding against property ultimately adjudicates persons' interests in that property, so the label in rem or quasi in rem cannot avoid due-process limits on adjudicatory authority. Property may supply relevant contacts, especially when the litigation concerns rights in the property itself. Here, the statutory presence of unrelated stock and the asserted corporate affiliations did not establish sufficient contacts to sustain Delaware's exercise of jurisdiction over the individual appellants. 433 U.S. at 207-17.

== Judgment ==
The Supreme Court reversed the Delaware Supreme Court. The sequestration-based assertion of jurisdiction over the appellants could not stand. It did not determine whether the officers or directors had breached fiduciary duties, whether the corporation was entitled to indemnification, or whether every attachment procedure was constitutionally defective. It also left the independent procedural due-process challenge to the ex parte seizure undecided because the jurisdictional defect resolved the appeal.

== Reasoning ==
The Court reconsidered the territorial framework associated with Pennoyer. Traditional doctrine had treated power over property in the state as sufficient to adjudicate interests in that property, even when the owner could not otherwise be sued there. International Shoe shifted the inquiry toward the relationship among the defendant, forum, and litigation. The Court found no justification for applying that fairness-based inquiry to a direct personal claim while allowing an identical underlying claim to escape scrutiny simply because the plaintiff attached an asset.
An in rem action determines interests in a thing, and quasi in rem jurisdiction traditionally permits a plaintiff to reach a particular defendant's interest in property to satisfy a claim. Both affect the rights of people. The constitutional question therefore cannot stop at identifying a res within the state. The property must be considered as part of the defendant-forum-litigation relationship. Where ownership or possession of land is the dispute, local property will ordinarily create strong links, and the state has an obvious interest in resolving it. Many traditional property cases can therefore satisfy minimum contacts without difficulty.
Here the Greyhound stock was an asset used to force appearance, not the subject of the derivative claim. The alleged wrong was managerial misconduct leading to corporate liability for conduct in Oregon. Whether the individuals happened to own Greyhound shares did not connect that wrong to Delaware. Delaware's statutory assignment of a location to intangible shares could not supply the missing substantive relationship merely by legislative declaration.
The Court rejected the idea that unrelated-property jurisdiction was necessary to prevent debtors from escaping liability by moving assets. Proper attachment could preserve assets to secure a judgment being sought in a forum with authority to decide the claim. A valid personal judgment could then receive full faith and credit elsewhere. In footnote 36, the Court distinguished using property to enforce an already established obligation from using it as the sole basis for first adjudicating liability. It also left unresolved, in footnote 37, whether property might furnish jurisdiction when no other forum was available.
Heitner alternatively emphasized Delaware's interest in regulating fiduciaries of its corporations. The majority found the existing statute poorly matched to that interest: it reached nonresident asset owners generally and failed to reach seven named fiduciaries who lacked attachable property. A law aimed at corporate officials' responsibilities would operate differently. Moreover, Delaware's interest in applying its corporate law did not itself establish authority over the individuals. Choice of law and personal jurisdiction remained separate questions.
The majority also found no adequate purposeful affiliation from accepting the positions or acquiring shares on this record. Delaware had not then enacted a statute treating acceptance of a directorship as consent to jurisdiction. The individuals were not required to own shares to hold office, and purchase of stock did not amount to consent to suit there on any claim. The Court acknowledged tradition but refused to make historical longevity conclusive when an old jurisdictional form lacked an adequate contemporary justification. 433 U.S. at 207-17.

== Separate Opinions ==
Justice Powell joined the Court but would expressly reserve whether ownership of property with a fixed, unmistakable location, particularly real estate, could alone support jurisdiction up to the property's value. He regarded that issue as different from the statutory situs assigned to intangible shares. Justice Stevens concurred in the judgment because an ordinary securities purchaser lacked fair warning of being forced into unrelated Delaware litigation. He emphasized the combination of an artificial stock location and the requirement of a general appearance to defend the merits. He also cautioned against reading the opinion to invalidate established methods involving adequate notice and predictable local activity.
Justice Brennan agreed that International Shoe should govern property-based jurisdiction, but objected to the
Court's immediate finding of insufficient contacts. The parties had litigated under a framework in which those contacts supposedly did not matter, so the plaintiff had not had a normal opportunity to develop the relevant facts through discovery. He favored allowing Delaware to reconsider its law and permitting development of the record. Substantively, he thought acceptance of fiduciary responsibilities under
Delaware law, the state's strong interest in corporate accountability, and the derivative nature of the claim ordinarily supplied a substantial relationship supporting jurisdiction. His disagreement thus concerned both the factual record and the majority's evaluation of the corporate relationship. 433 U.S. at 217-28.

== Notes ==
The case distinguishes authority to decide liability from notice of suit and from fairness of the asset-seizure procedure. Satisfying one requirement does not automatically satisfy the others. Its broad statement about all assertions of state jurisdiction later generated disagreement in Burnham, where every Justice sustained personal service on a voluntarily present individual but differed over whether historical practice alone was sufficient. Mallory likewise addresses consent rather than treating the mere ownership of unrelated property as a universal jurisdictional basis. The discussion of Delaware's consent law describes the law before the
Court in 1977, not the content of every later corporate-jurisdiction statute.
