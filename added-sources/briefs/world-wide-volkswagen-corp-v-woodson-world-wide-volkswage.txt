World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson
444 U.S. 286 (1980). U.S. Supreme Court. White, J. No. 78-1078; argued October 3, 1979; decided January 21, 1980. Certiorari to World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson, 1978 OK 131, 585 P.2d 351 (Okla. 1978).
Source: added-sources/Civil_Procedure_World_Wide_Volkswagen_Burger_King_Asahi_Big_Brief.pdf, pages 1–3

== Parties ==
Petitioners: World-Wide Volkswagen Corporation, the regional wholesale distributor for Volkswagen and Audi vehicles in New York, New Jersey, and Connecticut, and Seaway Volkswagen, Inc., the New York retail dealer that sold the Robinsons' Audi. They were defendants in the products-liability action, applicants for prohibition in
Oklahoma, and petitioners in the U.S. Supreme Court. Respondents: Charles S. Woodson, the Creek County district judge whose exercise of personal jurisdiction the petitioners sought to prohibit; Kay Eloise Robinson, who drove the
Audi and sued for her injuries; Harry Robinson, her husband, who sued individually and as next friend for their children; and the injured children. Underlying defendants outside the Supreme Court appeal: Audi NSU Auto
Union Aktiengesellschaft manufactured the car, and Volkswagen of America, Inc. imported it. Both remained parties in
Oklahoma.

== Procedural History ==
Underlying tort action: The Robinson family filed a manufacturers' products-liability action in the District Court for
Creek County, Oklahoma. They alleged that defective design and placement of the Audi's gasoline tank and fuel system caused the post-collision fire and their burn injuries. They sued the German manufacturer, the American importer, the regional distributor, and the retail dealer in the same action.
Special appearances and preservation: World-Wide and Seaway entered special appearances to contest Oklahoma's power to exercise in-personam jurisdiction over them. Their papers also used the phrase 'venue of the subject matter,' and they took depositions touching the merits. The Robinsons later argued that those steps waived the personal-jurisdiction objection. The trial court classified the appearances as special, and the Oklahoma Supreme Court decided the jurisdictional issues. The U.S. Supreme Court therefore treated the objection as preserved. Volkswagen of
America also entered a special appearance in the trial court. It stopped its challenge at that level and consequently remained outside the U.S. Supreme Court proceeding.
Trial-court ruling: World-Wide and Seaway submitted evidence defining the geographic limits of their businesses.
The Creek County court rejected their due-process objection. It later denied reconsideration. Those unreported rulings left the tort action pending and required the two companies to defend on the merits if immediate review remained unavailable.
Original writ proceeding in Oklahoma: The companies commenced a separate original proceeding in the Supreme
Court of Oklahoma. They asked that court to assume original jurisdiction and issue a writ of prohibition directing Judge
Woodson to stop exercising personal jurisdiction over them. A writ of prohibition supplies immediate supervisory review when a lower court is asserted to be acting beyond its adjudicatory power; the requested remedy addressed the jurisdictional ruling before a products-liability trial occurred.
On October 10, 1978, the Oklahoma Supreme Court assumed original jurisdiction but denied the writ. 1978 OK 131,
585 P.2d 351. It applied Oklahoma's Uniform Interstate and International Procedure Act, commonly called the long-arm statute. Section 1701.03(a)(3) covered tortious injury caused by an act or omission inside Oklahoma; the court required a further statutory basis because the alleged design and distribution conduct occurred elsewhere. Section
1701.03(a)(4) covered in-state tortious injury caused outside the state when the defendant regularly conducted business, pursued a persistent course of conduct, or derived substantial revenue from goods used or consumed in Oklahoma. The court inferred substantial Oklahoma-derived revenue from the retail value and mobility of automobiles and therefore found statutory authority. Oklahoma precedent construed the provision to extend through the federal constitutional limit, so the state court's statutory and due-process analyses converged. Five justices joined the opinion, two concurred in the result, and one concurred in part and dissented in part.
U.S. Supreme Court review and disposition: The Supreme Court granted certiorari, 440 U.S. 907 (1979), from the completed Oklahoma writ proceeding to resolve a conflict among state high courts concerning jurisdiction over remote vehicle sellers. The underlying tort action remained pending. By a 6-3 vote, the Court reversed the Oklahoma Supreme
Court. The judgment established that Oklahoma lacked personal jurisdiction over World-Wide and Seaway on this record. The disposition applied to those two petitioners; Audi and Volkswagen of America remained defendants in
Creek County.
Scope of the Supreme Court's decision: Adequacy of notice was uncontested. The Court decided adjudicatory power under the Fourteenth Amendment. It reviewed the jurisdictional record and the constitutional question generated by the interlocutory trial ruling and the state supreme court's writ judgment. It left product defect, causation, damages, venue, choice of law, and the liabilities of the manufacturer and importer for later proceedings.

== Material Facts ==
Harry and Kay Robinson bought a new Audi from Seaway in Massena, New York, in 1976 while the family lived in
New York. In 1977, they left New York to establish a new home in Arizona. While Kay drove through Oklahoma, another driver struck the Audi from behind. The impact ruptured the gasoline tank, ignited a fire in the passenger compartment, and severely burned Kay and two children. The other driver played no part in the Supreme Court litigation.
World-Wide was incorporated and maintained its business office in New York. Under its contract with Volkswagen, it distributed vehicles, parts, and accessories to dealers in New York, New Jersey, and Connecticut. Seaway was incorporated and operated in New York. The record treated World-Wide, Seaway, Volkswagen of America, and Audi as independent entities connected through contracts.
The companies' commercial activity reached their northeastern territory. Their sales, shipments, agents, advertising, and services were directed there. They closed no Oklahoma sale and performed no Oklahoma service. The record identified the Robinson Audi as the single World-Wide or Seaway vehicle shown to have entered Oklahoma.
Volkswagen and Audi maintained a nationwide service-center system, while World-Wide and Seaway held no financial interest in those Oklahoma facilities, received no service revenue from them, and exercised no control over them.

== Issue ==
Does the Fourteenth Amendment permit Oklahoma to exercise specific personal jurisdiction over a New York automobile retailer and a regional distributor when their own commercial conduct served New York, New Jersey, and
Connecticut, and their connection to Oklahoma arose when their customers drove the purchased car through Oklahoma and suffered an accident there?

== Holding ==
No. Specific jurisdiction requires a defendant-created affiliation with the forum that gives reasonable advance notice of exposure to suit there. World-Wide and Seaway directed their sales and distribution activity toward a defined northeastern market. The Robinsons created the Oklahoma connection by driving the Audi there. Purposeful forum conduct supplies the required affiliation; the accident's location, Oklahoma's regulatory interest, and litigation convenience then inform reasonableness. The Court reversed the judgment denying prohibition.

== Reasoning ==
Constitutional source and historical position: The Fourteenth Amendment Due Process Clause limits a state court's power to enter a binding personal judgment against a nonresident. Pennoyer v. Neff expressed that power through territorial presence and warned that a judgment entered without jurisdiction is void and receives no full-faith-and-credit effect. International Shoe Co. v. Washington translated the corporate-jurisdiction inquiry into minimum contacts whose quality and relation to the litigation make suit consistent with traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.
World-Wide Volkswagen identifies the defendant's forum-directed conduct as the constitutional anchor within that flexible standard.
Two functions of minimum contacts: Minimum contacts protect a defendant against the burdens of litigating in an affiliated but distant forum and allocate adjudicatory authority among coequal states. The first function concerns individual fairness. The second preserves interstate federalism by tying a state's coercive judicial power to a defendant-created relationship with that state. A convenient forum with a substantial regulatory interest therefore still needs a constitutionally adequate defendant-forum connection.
The five reasonableness interests: Once the defendant has established a forum affiliation, a court evaluates the burden on the defendant, the forum state's interest in adjudication, the plaintiff's interest in convenient and effective relief, the interstate judicial system's interest in efficient resolution, and the several states' shared interest in advancing substantive social policies. Oklahoma possessed strong accident, evidence, witness, highway-safety, and plaintiff-convenience interests. Those interests measure the fairness of exercising authority already grounded in a defendant-created contact.
Purposeful availment and operative foreseeability: Hanson v. Denckla requires an act through which the defendant purposefully affiliates itself with the forum and invokes the benefits and protections of its law. The useful foreseeability is the defendant's ability to anticipate suit from its own conduct and connection with the state. That notice permits a business to insure, distribute expected costs, alter distribution, or sever a forum relationship. Product mobility predicts physical travel; forum-directed commercial conduct predicts amenability to suit.
Application to the retailer and distributor: World-Wide selected a three-state distribution territory, and Seaway operated a New York dealership. Their business conduct created affiliations within that territory. Their customers, agents, and controlled distribution remained concentrated there. The Robinsons' relocation supplied the Oklahoma link by transporting the product after a completed retail sale.
Stream of commerce: The Court explained that a manufacturer or distributor may create forum contacts by delivering products into a distribution stream with the expectation that consumers in the forum will purchase them. Regular efforts to serve a state's market provide clear notice when the product later causes injury there. World-Wide and Seaway occupied different positions on this record: their market-facing conduct ended within the northeastern territory, and the vehicle reached Oklahoma through its owners' post-sale travel. This distinction later generated the stream-of-commerce dispute in Asahi.
Substantial-revenue theory: Oklahoma inferred that expensive automobiles sold by the petitioners would from time to time be used in Oklahoma and would therefore produce substantial revenue connected with in-state use. The Supreme
Court found that economic link too attenuated. The record tied the petitioners' revenue to transactions in their northeastern territory and tied the Oklahoma use to a consumer's later travel.
Automobile history and Hess: Automobiles earlier expanded jurisdiction through statutes appointing a state official to receive process for claims arising from a nonresident's act of driving inside the forum, as in Hess v. Pawloski. There, the defendant personally used the forum's roads and the suit arose from that use. The Robinsons used Oklahoma's roads here. The commercial defendants remained outside Oklahoma and directed their conduct toward another region. The
Court therefore applied the ordinary chattel rule to the automobile.
Brennan dissent: Justice Brennan's dissent covered this case and the companion decision in Rush v. Savchuk. For the
Volkswagen dispute, he emphasized Oklahoma's accident site, hospitalized plaintiffs, witnesses, evidence, and highway-safety interest, together with modern transportation and insurance. He viewed the minimum-contacts inquiry as a flexible implementation of fairness and argued that the forum's strong interests reduced the quantum of defendant contact required.
Marshall dissent, joined by Blackmun: Justice Marshall characterized World-Wide and Seaway as deliberate participants in a nationwide marketing and service network. The economic value of a car included long-distance travel and nationwide authorized service. In his analysis, selling a mobile product for its intended interstate use and benefiting from that network supplied purposeful commercial conduct tied to Oklahoma when the car caused injury there.
Blackmun dissent: Justice Blackmun separately treated the automobile's long-distance function as a critical feature.
State highways, police, accident investigation, and victim assistance supported the vehicle market and the defense of resulting claims. He would have upheld jurisdiction over the professional distributor and dealer when the accident occurred in Oklahoma.
Doctrinal significance and exam sequence: Identify the defendant whose contacts are under review; preserve separate treatment for every member of a distribution chain. Ask what that defendant deliberately did to affiliate with the forum, whether the claim arose from or related to that conduct, and then apply the five fairness interests. World-Wide
Volkswagen locates the constitutional dividing line between defendant-directed market activity and a consumer's post-sale movement of the product.
