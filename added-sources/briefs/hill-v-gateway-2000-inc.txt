Hill v. Gateway 2000, Inc.
Hill v. Gateway 2000, Inc., 105 F.3d 1147 (7th Cir. 1997), cert. denied, 522 U.S. 808 (1997). United States
Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit, No. 96-3294, argued December 10, 1996, decided January 6, 1997;
rehearing and rehearing en banc denied February 3, 1997. Judge Easterbrook wrote the opinion for a panel that included Judges Cummings and Wood. The case concerns whether terms shipped inside the box with a computer, including an arbitration clause, bind a consumer who keeps the machine beyond the stated return period. Full judicial opinion.
Source: added-sources/Contracts_Battle_of_the_Forms_and_Rolling_Contracts_Briefs.pdf, pages 9–11

== Parties ==
Rich and Enza Hill, who bought a Gateway computer by telephone, were the plaintiffs and appellees. They sued on behalf of a class of similarly situated purchasers and wanted their claims heard in court. Gateway
2000, Inc., a direct seller of personal computers by telephone and mail order, and David Prais, an individual defendant whose role the opinion does not describe, were the defendants and appellants. Gateway sought to enforce the arbitration clause included in the terms shipped with the computer.

== Material Facts ==
The Hills ordered a Gateway computer by telephone and gave a credit card number. A box arrived containing the computer, software, and a four-page statement of terms that, by its own words, governed unless the customer returned the computer within 30 days. One of the terms was an arbitration clause. Gateway's advertisements stated that its products came with limited warranties and lifetime support. The Hills kept the computer more than 30 days before complaining about its components and performance. They conceded noticing the statement of terms but denied reading it closely enough to discover the agreement to arbitrate.
The box did not display a notice that additional terms were inside. 105 F.3d at 1148, 1150.

== Procedural History ==
The Hills sued Gateway in federal district court in Illinois. They alleged that the product's shortcomings made Gateway a racketeer, with mail and wire fraud as the predicate offenses, and sought treble damages under the Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act (RICO) for themselves and a class of all other purchasers. Gateway moved to enforce the arbitration clause under the Federal Arbitration Act. The district court refused, finding that the record was insufficient to support a valid arbitration agreement between the parties or to show that the plaintiffs had been given adequate notice of the arbitration clause. Gateway took an interlocutory appeal, an appeal before final judgment, under 9 U.S.C. § 16(a)(1)(A), which permits immediate review of an order refusing to compel arbitration. Id. at 1148.

== Issue ==
Did the Hills accept Gateway's terms, including the arbitration clause, by keeping the computer beyond the
30-day return period, so that the Federal Arbitration Act requires enforcement of the clause? Does the approach of ProCD, Inc. v. Zeidenberg, 86 F.3d 1447 (7th Cir. 1996), extend beyond software and beyond contracts that remain to be performed on both sides? Does UCC § 2-207 govern a transaction in which only one party's form exists? Does the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act, or any requirement that an arbitration clause be conspicuous or disclosed before the sale, defeat the clause?

== Holding ==
A vendor, as master of the offer, may invite acceptance by conduct and may propose limitations on the kind of conduct that constitutes acceptance. A buyer who receives goods together with terms, has the opportunity to read the terms and return the goods, and instead keeps them past the stated period accepts the terms;
competent adults are bound by such documents whether or not they read them. That rule concerns contract formation generally, not software, and it applies to a transaction in which the buyer pays before seeing the full terms. Section 2-207 has no application when there is only one form. The Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act does not require a seller to recite its terms before the sale, and the Federal Arbitration Act forbids any special requirement that an arbitration clause be prominent. By keeping the computer beyond 30 days, the Hills accepted Gateway's offer, including the arbitration clause. Id. at 1148-51.

== Judgment ==
The Seventh Circuit vacated the district court's decision and remanded with instructions to compel the Hills to submit their dispute to arbitration.

== Reasoning ==
The Federal Arbitration Act requires courts to enforce an agreement to arbitrate "save upon such grounds as exist at law or in equity for the revocation of any contract," and the Hills' argument that the arbitration clause did not stand out was not such a ground. Whether a contract including the clause was formed depended on ordinary contract law, which the court took to be the same in Illinois and South Dakota, the two states whose law might govern. Id. at 1148.
The court treated ProCD as controlling. ProCD held that a vendor may propose that a contract be formed only after the customer has had a chance to inspect both the product and the terms, with retention as acceptance and return as rejection; the software buyer there was bound by a license inside the box because he used the product after reading the terms. Gateway shipped computers with the same accept-or-return offer that ProCD made. A contract need not be read to be effective. Payment before the disclosure of full terms is common for air travel, insurance, and many other transactions, and Carnival Cruise Lines, Inc. v. Shute, 499
U.S. 585 (1991), enforced a forum-selection clause printed among three pages of terms attached to a cruise ticket. Practical considerations pointed the same way: if a telephone sales representative had to read a four-page statement of terms before taking a credit card number, "the droning voice would anesthetize rather than enlighten many potential buyers," and customers would simply hang up. Id. at 1148-49.
The Hills offered several distinctions, each of which the court rejected. To the argument that ProCD was limited to software, the court answered that "ProCD is about the law of contract, not the law of software," and that Gateway's box was in any event crammed with software. To the argument that ProCD applied only to executory contracts, those with performance still owed on both sides, the court answered that the objection was legally wrong because the question was formation rather than performance, and factually wrong because both contracts were incompletely performed: Gateway's warranty and lifetime-support promises bound it to future performance that consumers value. To the argument that a merchant must give notice of terms before the sale, the court observed that Gateway's advertisements told buyers the products came with limited warranties and lifetime support, and that how limited the warranty was, and what support was offered, could be learned only from the terms. It identified three ways shoppers learn the terms of direct-sale goods. They can ask the vendor to send the terms before deciding whether to buy; the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act requires firms to distribute their warranty terms on request, and the Hills did not contend that Gateway would have refused to enclose its remaining terms as well. They can consult public sources such as computer
magazines and vendors' Web sites. Or they can inspect the documents after delivery and return the product if the terms are unacceptable. The Hills took the third route and were bound by its outcome. Id. at 1149-50.
The court held that the Hills' reliance on § 2-207 was misplaced: the battle-of-the-forms provision addresses conflicting forms exchanged by two parties, and when there is only one form, § 2-207 is irrelevant, as ProCD had already concluded. It also rejected the argument that the arbitration clause had to be prominent. Under
Doctor's Associates, Inc. v. Casarotto, 517 U.S. 681 (1996), the Federal Arbitration Act is inconsistent with any requirement that an arbitration clause be singled out for special notice; the terms inside Gateway's box stand or fall together. The Hills' final distinction, raised at oral argument, was that ProCD's box announced that terms were inside while Gateway's did not. The court found that difference functional rather than legal: a notice on the box serves shoppers browsing store shelves, while a telephone buyer has already ordered before seeing the box and receives the terms with the product, together with the return period in which to evaluate them. Id. at 1150.
As to the fraud allegations, Prima Paint Corp. v. Flood & Conklin Manufacturing Co., 388 U.S. 395 (1967), sends a claim that the contract as a whole was induced by fraud to the arbitrator; only a claim that the arbitration clause itself is unenforceable as part of a scheme to defraud is for the court, and the Hills made no such claim. RICO claims are no less arbitrable than claims in contract or tort. Whatever may be said for or against the cost and efficacy of arbitration was, in the court's view, for Congress and the contracting parties to weigh. Id. at 1151.

== Separate Opinions ==
The panel was unanimous. The reported decision contains no concurrence or dissent.

== Notes ==
Hill extends ProCD from a software license read before use to a consumer goods transaction in which the buyer pays by telephone and first sees the terms on delivery. The literature calls this structure a rolling contract or a "money now, terms later" transaction; those are commentators' labels, and the opinion itself speaks of an accept-or-return offer. The holding rests entirely on formation. Easterbrook treated the vendor as the offeror, retention past the return period as acceptance, and § 2-207 as inapplicable; the substance of the arbitration clause, including its cost and forum, was not examined, and no unconscionability argument was presented.
The case has been contested on each of those steps. Klocek v. Gateway, Inc., 104 F. Supp. 2d 1332 (D. Kan.
2000), rejected the premise that the vendor is the offeror and held that § 2-207 applies to a single form, reading Official Comment 1 to cover a written confirmation that follows an earlier agreement. Brower v.
Gateway 2000, Inc., 246 A.D.2d 246 (N.Y. App. Div. 1998), followed Hill on formation but held the same clause's arbitration-cost provisions unconscionable. Restatement of the Law, Consumer Contracts § 2(b) accepts post-assent terms only if the business gave notice of them before the consumer assented and the consumer could terminate without unreasonable cost, loss of value, or personal burden; on the Hills' facts the reasonableness of Gateway's pre-sale notice and of a 30-day return window would be contested questions rather than settled ones.
