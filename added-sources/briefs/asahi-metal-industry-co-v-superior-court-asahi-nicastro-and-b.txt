Asahi Metal Industry Co. v. Superior Court
480 U.S. 102 (1987). Supreme Court of the United States. No. 85-693; argued November 5, 1986; decided February 24, 1987. Certiorari to the Supreme Court of California. O'Connor, J., announced the judgment. The judgment was unanimous; the minimum-contacts reasoning produced no majority.
Source: added-sources/CivPro_Asahi_Nicastro_Burger_King_Case_Briefs (1).pdf, pages 1–2

== Parties ==
Petitioner / cross-defendant: Asahi Metal Industry Co., Ltd., a Japanese corporation that manufactured tire-tube valve assemblies in Japan. Real party in interest / cross-complainant: Cheng Shin Rubber Industrial Co., Ltd., a Taiwanese corporation that incorporated Asahi valve assemblies into finished tire tubes and sought indemnification from Asahi.
Nominal respondent: the Superior Court of California for Solano County, whose assertion of personal jurisdiction
Asahi challenged. Original plaintiffs: Gary Zurcher, the injured motorcycle driver, and claims arising from the death of his wife and passenger, Ruth Ann Moreno. Their product-liability claims had settled before the Supreme Court decided the jurisdictional dispute.

== Procedural History ==
On September 23, 1978, Zurcher's motorcycle crashed in Solano County, California. In September 1979, Zurcher filed a California product-liability action against Cheng Shin and other entities. Cheng Shin filed a cross-complaint for indemnification against Asahi, the maker of the valve assembly inside Cheng Shin's tube. California Code of Civil
Procedure § 410.10 authorizes jurisdiction to the limits permitted by the California and federal Constitutions.
Asahi specially challenged jurisdiction through a motion to quash service of summons. The parties submitted affidavits and documentary material directed to Asahi's sales, distribution knowledge, and California connections. On April 20,
1983, the Superior Court denied the motion, reasoning that Asahi conducted business internationally and could defend an international product claim.
Asahi petitioned the California Court of Appeal for extraordinary relief. That court issued a peremptory writ of mandate directing the Superior Court to quash service. It treated worldwide foreseeability of the component's ultimate destination as an insufficient basis for California jurisdiction. The California Supreme Court reversed and discharged the writ. Asahi Metal Industry Co. v. Superior Court, 39 Cal. 3d 35, 702 P.2d 543 (1985). It found awareness that components would reach California, plus an indirect economic benefit from California sales, constitutionally sufficient.
By this stage, Zurcher's claims against Cheng Shin and the other defendants had settled and were dismissed. The only surviving merits dispute was Cheng Shin's indemnity claim against Asahi. The U.S. Supreme Court granted certiorari,
475 U.S. 1044 (1986), reversed the California Supreme Court, and remanded. Part I recounted the case for all nine
Justices. Eight Justices joined Part II-B's conclusion that jurisdiction failed the fair-play-and-substantial-justice inquiry.
Justice Scalia joined the four-Justice minimum-contacts plurality and the judgment.

== Material Facts ==
Asahi manufactured valve assemblies in Japan and sold them to Cheng Shin and other tire manufacturers. Its sales to
Cheng Shin occurred in Taiwan, with shipment from Japan to Taiwan. Cheng Shin purchased 150,000 Asahi assemblies in 1978, 500,000 in 1979, 500,000 in 1980, 100,000 in 1981, and 100,000 in 1982. Sales to Cheng Shin represented 1.24 percent of Asahi's income in 1981 and 0.44 percent in 1982.
Cheng Shin sold finished tubes throughout the world and alleged that about twenty percent of its United States sales occurred in California. A Cheng Shin manager stated that the companies had discussed Cheng Shin's worldwide and
U.S. sales and that Asahi understood that its assemblies would reach the United States and California. Asahi's president stated that the company understood its Taiwan sales as transactions that would be litigated outside California. The record contained no contract between Asahi and Cheng Shin.
A 1983 examination of one Solano County cycle store found about 115 tubes. Ninety-seven appeared to have been made in Japan or Taiwan; twenty-one valve stems carried Asahi's circled-A mark; twelve of those twenty-one appeared in Cheng Shin tubes. The store also held forty-one Cheng Shin tubes using other manufacturers' valve assemblies.
Asahi maintained its corporate operations, manufacturing, and sales outside California. It had no California office, agents, employees, or property; made no direct California sales; solicited no California business; created and controlled no distribution system for Cheng Shin's finished tubes; and used a standard valve design rather than a design adapted for California.

== Issue ==
Two questions governed: (1) Does a foreign component manufacturer purposefully establish minimum contacts with
California by regularly selling components to a foreign finished-product manufacturer while aware that some finished products will enter California? (2) Given the surviving indemnity dispute between Taiwanese and Japanese corporations, does California's exercise of jurisdiction satisfy traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice?

== Holding ==
California lacked constitutional authority to adjudicate Cheng Shin's indemnity claim against Asahi. Eight Justices concluded that the international burden on Asahi, California's diminished regulatory interest after settlement of the
California plaintiff's claim, Cheng Shin's limited showing of a California litigation interest, and the interests of foreign sovereigns made jurisdiction unreasonable. The Court unanimously reversed. The Justices produced three approaches to minimum contacts, and none received five votes.

== Reasoning ==
Governing structure: The Fourteenth Amendment requires a forum connection created through the defendant's own purposeful conduct and a jurisdictional result consistent with fair play and substantial justice. World-Wide Volkswagen identifies the relationship among the defendant, forum, and litigation and supplies five fairness considerations: the defendant's burden, the forum's interest, the plaintiff's interest in effective relief, systemic efficiency, and the shared substantive policies of the sovereigns concerned.
Justice O'Connor's stream-of-commerce-plus approach - four votes: A substantial forum connection must arise from conduct purposefully directed toward that State. Placing a product into a distribution stream while aware that it may arrive in the forum establishes a distribution fact. Purposeful direction appears through additional conduct such as forum-specific product design, forum advertising, regular customer-advice channels, or use of a distributor obligated to serve the forum. Asahi's conduct contained none of those forum-directed features.
Justice Brennan's regular-flow approach - four votes: The stream of commerce is the regular and anticipated movement of products from manufacture through distribution to retail sale. A participant who knows that the final product is marketed in the forum obtains economic benefit from those sales and indirect benefit from the forum's commercial laws. Asahi's repeated, high-volume sales to Cheng Shin, combined with its knowledge of regular
California sales, therefore established minimum contacts under this approach.
Justice Stevens's volume-value-hazard approach - three votes: Justice Stevens found the fairness holding sufficient to decide the case and left the minimum-contacts question open. He added that purposeful availment may depend on the volume, value, and hazardous character of the component. Deliveries exceeding 100,000 units annually over several years could amount to purposeful availment even for a standard product sold worldwide.
Fairness holding: The burden required Asahi to travel from Japan and defend in a foreign legal system. Cheng Shin was a Taiwanese corporation seeking indemnity based on transactions and shipments in Taiwan and Japan. It supplied no concrete reason why California offered a more effective forum than Taiwan or Japan. California's consumer-protection interest had largely been served through the original tort case, and its remaining interest in a foreign indemnity dispute was slight. The interests of Japan and Taiwan and the Federal Government's foreign-relations concerns called for caution.
Doctrinal status: Part II-B is a holding of the Court because eight Justices joined it. The competing minimum-contacts formulations are separate opinions. A court can resolve an Asahi-type case by identifying the governing approach in its jurisdiction or by showing the result under each formulation.
Exam rule: Identify the defendant's distribution conduct, knowledge, sales pattern, control, design, advertising, advice channels, and distributor mandate. Apply O'Connor's additional-conduct test, Brennan's regular-and-anticipated-flow test, and Stevens's volume-value-hazard factors. Then conduct the fairness analysis independently, giving international burdens and foreign sovereign interests their full weight.
