Connecticut v. Doehr
Connecticut v. Doehr, 501 U.S. 1 (1991). Supreme Court of the United States, argued January 7, 1991, decided June 6, 1991. Justice White delivered the opinion of the Court as to Parts I, II, and III, which Chief
Justice Rehnquist and Justices Marshall, Blackmun, Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, and Souter joined in full and Justice Scalia joined as to Parts I and III. Part IV, on the need for a bond, was joined only by Justices
Marshall, Stevens, and O'Connor. Chief Justice Rehnquist, joined by Justice Blackmun, and Justice Scalia concurred in part and in the judgment, which was unanimous. The case appears at casebook page 286. Full judicial opinion.
Source: added-sources/CivPro_Notice_Service_and_Hearing_Briefs.pdf, pages 30–33

== Parties ==
John F. DiGiovanni, who planned to sue Brian K. Doehr for assault and battery, obtained a prejudgment attachment on Doehr's home. The State of Connecticut intervened in the Court of Appeals, at that court's invitation under 28 U.S.C. § 2403(b), which allows a state to intervene to defend the constitutionality of its statute; Connecticut and DiGiovanni were the petitioners. Doehr, the homeowner, was the respondent. Doehr sought a declaration that the attachment statute violated due process; Connecticut and DiGiovanni defended it. 501 U.S. at 4-8.

== Material Facts ==
On March 15, 1988, DiGiovanni applied to the Connecticut Superior Court for an attachment of $75,000 on
Doehr's home in Meriden, Connecticut, in connection with a civil action for assault and battery he was bringing against Doehr in the same court. The suit did not involve Doehr's real estate, and DiGiovanni had no pre-existing interest in the home or any of Doehr's other property. Connecticut law allowed a court or judge to authorize prejudgment attachment of real property without a hearing on verification by oath of the plaintiff or another competent affiant that there was probable cause to sustain the validity of the plaintiff's claim. The statute did not require the plaintiff to post a bond to cover the defendant's damages if the attachment proved wrongful. Id. at 5-6.
DiGiovanni's affidavit, in five one-sentence paragraphs, stated that the facts in his complaint were true, that
Doehr had willfully, wantonly, and maliciously assaulted him, that the assault broke his left wrist and bruised his right eye among other injuries, that he had incurred medical expenses, and that in his opinion these facts showed probable cause that judgment would be rendered for him. On the strength of these submissions, a
Superior Court judge found probable cause and ordered the attachment to the value of $75,000. The sheriff attached the property four days later. Only then did Doehr receive notice, and he had not yet been served with the complaint. The notice told him he could seek a hearing to argue that there was no probable cause, to ask that the attachment be vacated or modified or a bond substituted, or to claim an exemption. Id. at 6-7.

== Procedural History ==
Instead of seeking the post-attachment hearing, Doehr sued DiGiovanni in federal district court, claiming that the statute violated the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. The District Court upheld the statute and granted summary judgment to DiGiovanni. A divided panel of the Second Circuit reversed in an opinion by Judge Pratt, reported as Pinsky v. Duncan, 898 F.2d 852 (2d Cir. 1990). The court held that
Sniadach and its progeny permit a prior hearing to be postponed only where exceptional circumstances justify the delay and sufficient additional safeguards are present. It emphasized that, unlike the documentary issues in Mitchell, an assault claim turned on disputed facts about a fight that a judge could not reliably assess on the plaintiff's version alone, and Judge Pratt concluded, in his own view, that the lack of a bond was an independent defect. Connecticut had intervened in the Court of Appeals after oral argument there, at that court's invitation, and the Supreme Court granted certiorari. Id. at 7-8.

== Issue ==
Does a state statute that authorizes prejudgment attachment of real estate without prior notice or hearing, without a showing of extraordinary circumstances, and without requiring the plaintiff to post a bond satisfy the Due Process Clause when invoked by a tort plaintiff who has no pre-existing interest in the property?
Must due process also require a bond?

== Holding ==
When a state statute lets a private party enlist state power to deprive another of property through prejudgment attachment, the adequacy of the procedure is judged by a modified version of the Mathews v. Eldridge test.
The court considers, first, the private interest affected by the prejudgment measure; second, the risk of erroneous deprivation through the procedures used and the probable value of additional or alternative safeguards; and third, with principal attention, the interest of the party seeking the prejudgment remedy, with due regard for any ancillary interest the government may have in providing the procedure or avoiding the burden of greater protections.
Applying that test, the Connecticut statute violated due process as applied: the homeowner's interest was significant, the risk of error was substantial, and the plaintiff's interest in an ex parte attachment was minimal because he had no pre-existing interest in the property and did not allege that Doehr was about to transfer or encumber it. Id. at 9-18.
A four-Justice plurality would further have held that due process requires the plaintiff to post a bond or other security in addition to a hearing or a showing of exigency, and that a bond does not excuse the need for a hearing. Id. at 18-24 (plurality opinion).

== Judgment ==
The Supreme Court affirmed the judgment of the Court of Appeals and remanded the case for further proceedings consistent with its opinion.

== Reasoning ==
The Court began by placing the case in the line from Sniadach through Fuentes, Mitchell, and North Georgia
Finishing, whose results varied with the type of remedy. Mathews had drawn on those cases to decide what process is due when the government itself deprives a person of property. Prejudgment remedies ordinarily arise in disputes between private parties, so the Court adjusted the third Mathews factor to focus principally on the interest of the party seeking the remedy rather than on the government's. Id. at 9-11.
The private interest was significant. For a homeowner, attachment ordinarily clouds title, impairs the ability to sell or otherwise transfer the property, taints the owner's credit rating, reduces the chance of obtaining a home equity loan or additional mortgage, and can even place an existing mortgage in technical default where the mortgage has an insecurity clause. Connecticut correctly argued that these effects do not amount to a complete, physical, or permanent deprivation and are less severe than the temporary total loss of household goods or wages. But the Court had never limited due process protection to such extreme deprivations;
temporary or partial impairments of property rights caused by attachments, liens, and similar encumbrances are enough. Id. at 11-12.
The risk of erroneous deprivation was substantial. Attachment deprives the defendant of property on the basis of a contingency, a future damages award, that may never occur. Connecticut required a finding of probable cause to sustain the validity of the claim, but the meaning of that standard was obscure: the State first said it required a showing of the suit's objective likelihood of success; Doehr read it as requiring only the plaintiff's subjective good-faith belief; and at oral argument the State said it required something like a claim sufficient to survive a motion to dismiss. Under any of these readings the risk of error was too great. A standard satisfied by a facially valid complaint or the plaintiff's belief would allow attachment on claims a jury would reject or that the defendant would dispute, and in the good-faith case even on claims that failed to state a cause of action. Even under the strictest reading, the judge received only a skeletal, one-sided affidavit and could make no realistic assessment of the likelihood of success. An assault case does not involve the ordinarily uncomplicated matters that lend themselves to documentary proof, as a debt or delinquent payments do. Id. at 12-14.
The safeguards Connecticut provided did not adequately reduce that risk. The statute offered an expeditious post-attachment hearing, notice of that hearing, judicial review of an adverse decision, and a double-damages action if the suit was begun without probable cause. Similar protections existed in Mitchell, but there the plaintiff had a vendor's lien to protect, the dispute involved documentary matters, and the plaintiff had to post a bond. None of those features was present here. A later hearing might show that probable cause was lacking, but it could not cure the temporary deprivation an earlier hearing could have prevented. Id. at 14-15.
The interests favoring an ex parte attachment were too slight to justify the procedure. DiGiovanni had no existing interest in Doehr's real estate; his only interest was ensuring that assets would be available to satisfy a judgment if he won. He did not allege that Doehr was about to transfer or encumber the property or take any other step that would make it unavailable. The Court acknowledged that such an allegation, properly supported, would be an exigent circumstance permitting the hearing to be postponed until after attachment.
The State's interest could not exceed the plaintiff's, and Connecticut could not plead administrative burdens because it already provided an immediate post-deprivation hearing. Id. at 16.
History and current practice supported the result. Traditional attachment statutes were limited to situations in which the defendant had taken or threatened action that would render a judgment uncollectible, were confined to creditors' claims, and required a bond. A survey of modern statutes showed that nearly every state requires a pre-attachment hearing, a showing of exigent circumstances, or both before an attachment may issue. Id. at 16-18.
In Part IV, the plurality addressed the bond. Without a bond, the risk of wrongful deprivation remains unacceptably high even with a hearing or exigency requirement, because until the post-attachment hearing the defendant has no protection against damages where no exigency in fact existed or the claim was worthless, and even a full hearing yields only an educated prediction of who will win. The double-damages remedy did not substitute for a bond, because recovering under it would require proof of something like
fraud, malice, or failure to state a claim. At the same time, a bond does not excuse a hearing: the harms from an erroneous attachment, such as a lost sale, a lost loan for a child's education, or a mortgage placed in technical default, cannot be fully undone by payment at the end of the case. Id. at 18-23 (plurality opinion).

== Separate Opinions ==
Chief Justice Rehnquist, joined by Justice Blackmun, concurred in Parts I, II, and III and in the judgment. He observed that all the earlier cases involved personal property, such as bank deposits and chattels, and the physical seizure of the property, so the defendant lost its use and possession; Connecticut's attachment created only an incipient lien and left Doehr in possession. The Court's holding therefore broke new ground, but he agreed that the deprivation was significant. He declined to join Part IV, calling it unwise and unnecessary to decide hypothetical questions about bonds and exigent circumstances before concrete cases presented them. Id. at 26-30 (Rehnquist, C.J., concurring).
Justice Scalia joined Parts I and III and concurred in the judgment. Because the kind of attachment at issue was not a procedure recognized at common law, he agreed that its validity should be tested under Mathews v.
Eldridge and that it failed that test. The casebook omits his opinion. Id. at 30 (Scalia, J., concurring in part and concurring in the judgment).

== Notes ==
Doehr supplies the current framework for prejudgment remedies and answers the question the casebook asks after Fuentes: the Court now evaluates such remedies with a Mathews-type balance in which the plaintiff's interest replaces the government's as the principal third factor. The case also extends Sniadach and Fuentes to real estate and to deprivations that leave the owner in possession, which is the point Chief Justice Rehnquist identified as new. The holding is as applied: a plaintiff with a pre-existing interest, a documentary claim, or a supported allegation that the defendant is about to dissipate the property could justify a different result.
Shaumyan v. O'Neill shows that effect: the Second Circuit upheld the same statute as applied to a contractor who attached a homeowner's property in a payment dispute, because the evidence was largely documentary and the contractor had a substantial pre-existing interest once its labor and materials were incorporated into the home. The bond requirement in Part IV commanded only four votes and is not a holding of the Court.
After Doehr, commentators questioned lis pendens statutes, under which a plaintiff claiming an interest in real property records a notice of the pending suit without a hearing; the Second Circuit upheld New York's statute in Diaz v. Paterson, applying the Mathews factors as construed in Doehr.
