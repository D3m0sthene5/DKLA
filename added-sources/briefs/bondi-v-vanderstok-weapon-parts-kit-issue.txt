Bondi v. Vanderstok - weapon-parts-kit issue
604 U.S. 458, 145 S. Ct. 857 (2025). U.S. Supreme Court. Gorsuch, J. Argued October 8, 2024; decided March 26, 2025. Certiorari to 86 F.4th 179. This brief follows the assigned excerpt and addresses the kit issue under 18 U.S.C. Section 921(a)(3)(A).
Source: added-sources/Legislation_Regulatory_State_Class3_Case_Briefs.pdf, pages 3–4

== Parties ==
Petitioners: the Attorney General and federal officials responsible for the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and
Explosives (ATF), defending ATF's 2022 rule. Respondents: individual gun owners, at-home gunsmiths, firearm manufacturers, dealers, and allied organizations that brought a pre-enforcement Administrative Procedure Act challenge to the rule.

== Procedural History ==
Acting under authority to prescribe regulations necessary to carry out the Gun Control Act of 1968 (GCA), ATF issued a 2022 rule addressing privately assembled firearms. The rule's weapon-parts-kit provision interpreted the statutory definition of a firearm to cover a kit designed to, or readily capable of being completed, assembled, restored, or otherwise converted to, expel a projectile by explosive action. ATF stated that ready conversion depends on factors including time, ease, expertise, equipment, and the availability of additional parts.
Before enforcement began, respondents filed what they described as a facial challenge under the APA, 5 U.S.C. Section
706(2)(C). They argued that the GCA categorically excludes weapon parts kits, making ATF's kit provision an exercise of authority beyond the statute. On summary judgment, the Northern District of Texas agreed and vacated the rule. The
Fifth Circuit largely affirmed, holding that a parts kit could never satisfy the statutory requirements regardless of completeness or ease of assembly. 680 F. Supp. 3d 741; 86 F.4th 179. The Supreme Court granted certiorari, reversed, and remanded.

== Material Facts ==
Congress enacted the GCA in 1968 after finding that existing law allowed criminals to obtain largely untraceable guns too easily. The Act requires many firearm importers, manufacturers, and dealers to obtain federal licenses, preserve sales records, perform background checks, and mark firearms with serial numbers. Those requirements keep firearms from prohibited buyers and allow law enforcement to trace a recovered gun through manufacture and transfer records.
Section 921(a)(3)(A) defines a firearm to include 'any weapon (including a starter gun) which will or is designed to or may readily be converted to expel a projectile by the action of an explosive.' New manufacturing technologies, including reinforced polymers and 3D printing, made home assembly practical. Available kits span a broad range: some omit essential parts and demand specialized expertise, uncommon equipment, substantial effort, and considerable time;
others contain every component for a pistol and can be completed in roughly half an hour with common tools.
Some sellers treated kits as outside the GCA and therefore sold them without licenses, background checks, sales records, or serial numbers. Law-enforcement submissions of untraceable 'ghost guns' for federal tracing rose from about 1,600 in 2017 to more than 19,000 in 2021, and tracing efforts were usually futile.
The record centered on Polymer80's 'Buy Build Shoot' kit. It contained every component needed for a Glock-variant semiautomatic pistol. In an ATF test, a person with no previous exposure to the kit produced a working gun in twenty-one minutes using common tools and publicly available YouTube instructions. The product name and complete component set made its intended combat function apparent when sold.

== Issue ==
Does Section 921(a)(3)(A) authorize ATF to regulate at least some weapon parts kits as a 'weapon' that 'may readily be converted' to expel a projectile, thereby defeating respondents' facial claim that ATF lacks authority over every kit?

== Holding ==
Substantive holding: Yes. Some weapon parts kits, including Polymer80's 'Buy Build Shoot' kit, are weapons capable of ready conversion within Section 921(a)(3)(A). ATF's kit provision therefore fits the GCA in at least some applications and survives the facial challenge. Procedural disposition: The Supreme Court reversed the Fifth Circuit and remanded for further proceedings.

== Reasoning ==
Source of law, posture, and competing positions: The controlling provisions were the GCA's definition of 'firearm,'
ATF's delegated rulemaking authority, and APA review for agency action exceeding statutory authority. The government defended a rule reaching kits that meet the statutory terms. Respondents argued that a collection of unfinished parts can never be a 'weapon,' so every application of the kit rule exceeds ATF's authority. Because respondents did not contest the government's account of their facial burden, the Court treated one valid application as sufficient to defeat the challenge.
The statutory definition contains two requirements. First, the object must qualify as a 'weapon.' Second, the weapon must presently fire, be designed to fire, or be capable of ready conversion to fire a projectile by explosive action. The
Polymer80 kit satisfied both.
'Weapon' as an artifact noun: The ordinary meaning of weapon is an instrument intended for offensive or defensive combat. 'Weapon' is also an artifact noun: its application follows an object's intended human function. Ordinary speakers therefore use artifact nouns for unfinished objects whose intended function is clear, such as an author's unfinished novel, a boxed IKEA table awaiting assembly, or a rifle disassembled for cleaning. Polymer80 supplied every necessary component, required about twenty-one minutes of ordinary work, and marketed the product as 'Buy
Build Shoot.' Those features made the kit an unfinished instrument of combat and therefore a weapon.
Statutory context: Congress expressly included starter guns within the term weapon. A starter gun fires blanks and can be converted to live fire with a common power tool in less than an hour by a person lacking specialized knowledge. Its inclusion shows that operability at the moment of sale is unnecessary. The alternatives 'is designed to' and 'may readily be converted to' likewise give statutory work to weapons awaiting completion or conversion.
Ready conversion: The starter gun supplies a statutory yardstick. Its express inclusion makes sense because it can be converted to live fire with common tools, ordinary skill, and less than an hour of work. Polymer80's kit demanded no greater time, effort, expertise, or specialized equipment. It therefore met the ready-conversion requirement as well.
The Court left the boundary for later cases. A kit missing essential parts or requiring substantial time, specialized skill, or uncommon equipment may fall beyond the terms weapon and ready conversion. That range of harder applications could support future as-applied challenges; it could not establish that the rule conflicts with the statute in every application. The statute's text, context, and structure resolved the examples before the Court, so lenity and constitutional avoidance supplied no further decision rule.
Justice Jackson's concurrence: Jackson framed the case as a straightforward excess-of-authority inquiry. Congress delegated rulemaking power; a reviewing court identifies the delegation's actual statutory boundary and asks whether the agency crossed it. Once the statute permits the challenged action, disagreement with ATF's discretionary policy choices cannot establish an APA violation.
Justice Thomas's dissent: Thomas read the ordinary meaning of weapon to require an existing instrument usable in combat. On that view, an unfinished parts kit becomes a weapon only after conversion into an operable gun. He distinguished a kit from a disassembled rifle and a starter gun because those objects ordinarily contain functional frames or receivers, and he treated Congress's express inclusion of starter guns as a specific expansion that did not extend to additional unfinished objects. Any remaining ambiguity in this statute with criminal applications would trigger the rule of lenity.
Narrow holding and significance: The Court decided only the facial kit issue. It established that Section 921(a)(3)(A) reaches some sufficiently complete and easily assembled kits. It reserved the classification of more incomplete or labor-intensive products and evaluated neither a specific enforcement action nor every factor in ATF's readiness test.
1L explanation: Start with the litigation posture. Respondents asserted that Congress gave ATF zero authority over weapon parts kits. Polymer80's complete, twenty-one-minute kit defeated that categorical proposition. The majority then proved statutory coverage in sequence: ordinary meaning allowed this unfinished artifact to count as a weapon; the starter-gun example and the statute's three operative alternatives confirmed that reading; and the starter gun supplied the benchmark for ready conversion.
