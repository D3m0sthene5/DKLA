Mallory v. Norfolk Southern Railway Co.
Mallory v. Norfolk Southern Railway Co., 600 U.S. 122 (2023). United States Supreme Court, decided June
27, 2023. Justice Gorsuch announced the judgment. Parts I and III-B of his opinion commanded a five-Justice majority comprising Gorsuch, Thomas, Alito, Sotomayor, and Jackson. Parts II, III-A, and IV were a four-Justice plurality, joined by Thomas, Sotomayor, and Jackson. Jackson concurred separately.
Alito concurred in part and in the judgment. Justice Barrett dissented, joined by Chief Justice Roberts and
Justices Kagan and Kavanaugh. References to opinion parts below preserve the difference between the majority holding and the broader plurality discussion.

== Parties ==
Robert Mallory, a former Norfolk Southern employee, was plaintiff below and petitioner in the Supreme
Court. He was a Virginia resident when he filed suit. Norfolk Southern Railway Company was the defendant and respondent. At that time it was incorporated in Virginia and had its headquarters there. The relevant corporate locations are those in the record of this suit; the jurisdictional question was whether Pennsylvania could hear Mallory's unrelated claim through the railroad's registration there.

== Material Facts ==
Mallory worked for Norfolk Southern as a freight-car mechanic for almost twenty years in Ohio and
Virginia. He alleged that he sprayed asbestos on boxcar pipes, handled chemicals in a paint shop, and demolished car interiors containing carcinogens. After leaving the railroad, he lived in Pennsylvania for a period and then returned to Virginia. He was diagnosed with cancer and attributed the illness to his employment. He did not allege exposure to the carcinogens in Pennsylvania.
Norfolk Southern conducted substantial business in Pennsylvania, including more than 2,000 miles of track, eleven rail yards, and three locomotive repair shops. It had registered to do business there in 1998 and maintained and updated its registration over the following decades. Its registration designated a commercial registered office provider through which it could be located for service. The Pennsylvania statutory scheme made qualification as a foreign corporation a sufficient basis for general personal jurisdiction, extending to any cause of action, whether or not related to Pennsylvania business.
The statutory combination mattered. Pennsylvania required registration for the local business at issue, and expressly attached jurisdictional consequences to qualification. Mallory relied on that legal act as consent rather than trying to prove that the railroad's operations made Pennsylvania its corporate home. Norfolk
Southern understood that state law attached all-purpose jurisdiction to its registration, but argued that requiring that submission as a price of doing business was unconstitutional. The alleged injury and the conventional corporate homes supplied no ordinary Pennsylvania basis for specific or at-home general jurisdiction. Gorsuch opinion, Parts I and III-B.

== Procedural History ==
Mallory filed his FELA action in the Philadelphia County Court of Common Pleas in September 2017.
Norfolk Southern filed preliminary objections challenging personal jurisdiction. It argued that the claim did not arise in Pennsylvania, that it was not at home there under Goodyear and Daimler, and that registration did not furnish valid consent. On February 7, 2018, the trial court sustained the objections and dismissed the action with prejudice for lack of personal jurisdiction. Its rationale was that the statutory scheme forced corporations to choose between doing business in Pennsylvania and surrendering their jurisdictional protection.
Mallory appealed to the Pennsylvania Superior Court. While the appeal was pending, that court decided
Webb-Benjamin, which treated registration as a continuing basis of consent after Daimler. The trial court issued a supplemental opinion explaining why it regarded the federalism concerns in Mallory's case as different. In October 2020, on Norfolk Southern's application, the Superior Court transferred Mallory's appeal to the Pennsylvania Supreme Court. Section 722(7) gave that court exclusive appellate jurisdiction over final common-pleas orders holding a Pennsylvania statute unconstitutional, and §5103 permitted transfer to the proper tribunal. The transfer was therefore a matter of appellate authority, not a transfer of the underlying tort claim to a different trial forum.
The Pennsylvania Supreme Court affirmed on December 22, 2021, 266 A.3d 542. It accepted that the state statutes authorized all-purpose jurisdiction upon registration but held that requiring registration as a condition of business made the supposed consent involuntary and inconsistent with due process. It regarded
International Shoe and later cases as having displaced the older registration precedent, Pennsylvania Fire.
The United States Supreme Court granted certiorari in light of disagreement among state high courts and limited its decision to the Fourteenth Amendment due-process question.
Norfolk Southern had also raised a dormant Commerce Clause objection, which the Pennsylvania Supreme
Court had not resolved. That additional constitutional issue remained available for consideration on remand.
The Supreme Court's decision did not adjudicate whether Mallory's work caused his cancer, whether Norfolk
Southern was negligent, or whether the statutory scheme survived every other constitutional challenge.
Gorsuch opinion, Part I & n.3; Alito opinion, opening discussion. State procedural details: Pennsylvania
Supreme Court opinion, 266 A.3d 542 (2021), Part IV.

== Issue ==
Does the Due Process Clause forbid Pennsylvania from requiring an out-of-state corporation to submit to general personal jurisdiction as a condition of registering to do business, when the company knowingly complied with that express scheme and is sued on a claim unrelated to Pennsylvania? Did International Shoe and the at-home cases displace the controlling consent precedent in Pennsylvania Fire?

== Holding ==
On the Pennsylvania statutory scheme and facts presented, enforcing registration-based consent to suit did not violate the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause. Pennsylvania Fire Insurance Co. v. Gold Issue
Mining & Milling Co., 243 U.S. 93 (1917), controlled the materially similar situation and had not been overruled. Lower courts must follow directly controlling Supreme Court precedent even when they perceive tension with a different line of decisions. Consent remains a basis of jurisdiction distinct from the contacts inquiry applicable to a nonconsenting corporation. The Court did not decide whether every state's registration statute establishes consent or whether Pennsylvania's scheme violates the dormant Commerce
Clause. Gorsuch opinion, Part III-B; Alito opinion, Part I.

== Judgment ==
The Supreme Court vacated the Pennsylvania Supreme Court's judgment and remanded for further proceedings. Mallory prevailed on the due-process question, removing the constitutional ground on which his action had been dismissed. The disposition did not award damages or guarantee that Pennsylvania would ultimately adjudicate the merits. The unresolved Commerce Clause challenge could still affect whether the suit proceeded there.

== Reasoning ==
The five-Justice portion of Gorsuch's opinion relied on the close fit with Pennsylvania Fire. That case allowed an Arizona plaintiff to sue a Pennsylvania insurer in Missouri on an insurance obligation concerning
Colorado property. The insurer had complied with a Missouri law requiring an appointment for service understood to cover any suit. Its agreement supplied jurisdiction despite the absence of a connection between the underlying claim and Missouri. Norfolk Southern likewise had registered under an explicit law imposing all-purpose jurisdiction and maintained that status while conducting substantial local business.
The state court could not disregard Pennsylvania Fire simply because later minimum-contacts decisions seemed inconsistent with it. When a Supreme Court precedent directly controls, lower courts must apply it and leave overruling to the Supreme Court. The absence of a Pennsylvania injury or a current Pennsylvania residence for Mallory did not distinguish the case: the older precedent also involved an out-of-state plaintiff and an out-of-state claim. The holding was tied to this clear legal consequence and this company's compliance, rather than an assumption that every filing with a secretary of state entails blanket consent.
Gorsuch's plurality developed a broader historical account. It compared registration-based consent with personal service on a visiting individual and discussed state laws requiring foreign corporations to accept local adjudicatory authority as a condition of entering local markets. It read International Shoe as adding a route to jurisdiction over corporations that had not consented, without abolishing traditional consent-based authority. The plurality invoked Burnham to show that minimum contacts did not displace every established jurisdictional basis.
The plurality also rejected Norfolk Southern's attempt to characterize all-purpose jurisdiction as limited exclusively to incorporation and principal place of business. Those paradigms govern jurisdiction based on a corporation's relationship to the forum when consent is absent. A defendant may waive an individual due-process protection, including through conduct with known legal consequences. Norfolk Southern's long registration history and substantial use of Pennsylvania's market and legal protections reinforced the plurality's view that enforcing the arrangement was fair. Alito accepted the narrower conclusion that consent under this scheme satisfied due process, while declining to join all of the plurality's reasoning.
The disagreement was therefore not over whether valid consent can ever establish jurisdiction; all sides recognized that principle. It concerned whether legally required registration with known consequences constituted consent of a kind the Constitution permits a state to obtain. The majority applied the surviving precedent to answer the due-process objection. Alito's separate constitutional concern ensured that the judgment left a different challenge unresolved. Gorsuch opinion, Parts II-IV; Alito opinion, Parts I-II.

== Separate Opinions ==
Justice Jackson emphasized Insurance Corp. of Ireland. Personal jurisdiction protects an individual, waivable right, and conduct can constitute a legal submission to adjudicatory authority. Norfolk Southern chose registration and the benefits of extensive local business despite the clear statutory consequence. In her view, asking whether the company could have been sued without that waiver missed the point of consent.
She also distinguished local business requiring registration from trains merely passing through the state.
Justice Alito supplied the fifth vote for the controlling holding and disposition. He found Pennsylvania Fire directly applicable and saw no fundamental unfairness in requiring this sophisticated company, with extensive Pennsylvania operations and clear notice, to defend there. He nevertheless doubted that
Pennsylvania could constitutionally impose the condition under other provisions. In particular, a state might burden interstate commerce by compelling out-of-state businesses to defend claims having no local connection. He discussed both discrimination against interstate commerce and burdens excessive in relation to local benefits, and suggested a substantial prospect that the scheme would fail that inquiry. Those views were his concurrence, not a Court holding that the statute violated the Commerce Clause; the issue remained for remand.
Justice Barrett, joined by Roberts, Kagan, and Kavanaugh, would have affirmed. She considered the consent label a means of evading Goodyear, Daimler, and BNSF. If a state could impose all-purpose jurisdiction merely by requiring registration, the at-home limitation would become avoidable by a legislative relabeling.
She distinguished actual voluntary consent through contracts or litigation from a compulsory business condition. She also noted that Norfolk Southern's registration paperwork itself did not expressly promise jurisdiction, although the statute attached that consequence.
Barrett disputed the plurality's history and its analogy to Burnham. Personal service on an individual had both a long tradition and continuing general acceptance. She viewed unlimited registration jurisdiction as lacking that comparable foundation and as inconsistent with the modern rejection of fictional corporate presence. She also stressed due process's role in protecting the federal balance among states when a forum has no connection to the particular controversy. These objections explain the four-Justice dissent; they do not alter the five-Justice rejection of Norfolk Southern's due-process defense. Jackson, Alito, and Barrett opinions.

== Notes ==
The binding result concerns due process and this express registration scheme. It neither overrules Daimler's at-home rule for jurisdiction based on contacts nor makes every registered corporation generally suable in every state. The content and authoritative construction of the particular state's statute remain essential. BNSF had expressly left consent undecided, so its rejection of jurisdiction based on tracks and employees did not resolve the issue presented here.
The opinion's voting structure also limits attribution. Five Justices agreed on application of Pennsylvania
Fire and vacatur, while only four joined Gorsuch's entire historical and doctrinal explanation. Alito's
Commerce Clause discussion identifies a potential obstacle left open by the 2023 decision. That reservation should be stated as the scope of this judgment, rather than as a claim about the outcome of any subsequent proceedings. The railroad-derailment scenario that opens Gorsuch's opinion is an illustration; Mallory's actual claim concerns alleged occupational exposure and cancer.
