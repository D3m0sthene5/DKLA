Termination of the power of acceptance
Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§ 25, 36-48, 59, 61, 87; UCC § 2-205; casebook printed pages 167-185, PDF pages 217-235.
Source: added-sources/Contracts_Offer_and_Acceptance_Briefs.pdf, pages 14–16

== Preamble ==
The power of acceptance is the offeree's ability to complete the proposed bargain by accepting. That power lasts only while the offer remains legally available. Under Restatement § 36, rejection or counteroffer, lapse of time, revocation, and death or incapacity can terminate it. Failure of a condition governing acceptance can also end the power. An effective acceptance must therefore occur before a terminating event, unless a rule protecting an option or another irrevocable offer changes the result. The analysis requires a chronological account of communications and conduct, together with the rule that determines when each event becomes legally effective.
An offer lapses at its stated expiration time. If it gives no time, it remains open for a reasonable period determined by its terms and surrounding circumstances. Relevant considerations include the subject matter, price fluctuations, the communication method, the parties' prior dealings, and the apparent urgency of the transaction. An offer made during a face-to-face conversation ordinarily expires when the conversation ends unless the circumstances indicate continuing availability. In the casebook's example, Akers v. J.B. Sedberry,
Inc., 286 S.W.2d 617 (Tenn. Ct. App. 1955), an employer could not wait several days and then accept an employee's oral resignation offer made during a concluded meeting. Restatement § 41; casebook at 168.
Reasonable duration also depends on the offer's purpose. In Loring v. City of Boston, 48 Mass. (7 Met.) 409 (1844), the casebook describes a reward advertised in response to a period of arson. A claimant sought the reward for apprehending an offender three years and eight months later. The court concluded that the offer had lapsed because the original emergency and public notice did not reasonably support continuing availability that long. Other reward offers may contemplate future events over a longer period. The lesson is to explain why the particular offer's context supports a particular duration. Casebook at 169, 174-75.
Lapse occurs through expiration without requiring a new communication from the offeror. A response after lapse ordinarily cannot accept the expired offer, but it may itself operate as a new offer if it sufficiently proposes a bargain. The original offeror can then accept that new proposal. Whether a delayed communication creates a contract thus may depend on subsequent conduct and on whether the parties renewed the offer, rather than on a power to erase the expiration retrospectively. A stated deadline also must be read with the applicable rule concerning dispatch or receipt of acceptance. Restatement §§ 35, 41;
casebook at 169-70, 187-88.
Revocation occurs when the offeror withdraws an offer before effective acceptance. For a direct revocation under § 42, the offeree must receive a communication indicating that the offeror no longer intends to enter the proposed bargain. Dispatch alone ordinarily does not terminate the power. An uncommunicated change of mind likewise leaves the outward offer in place. The offeror need not use a technical word if the communication reasonably conveys withdrawal. Once acceptance has already formed a contract, an attempted withdrawal raises questions about the existing contractual duties rather than the availability of an outstanding offer.
An ordinary common-law promise to keep an offer open until a particular date may remain revocable before that date if no consideration or other rule makes the promise enforceable. A stated acceptance deadline establishes the latest time for acceptance; a binding commitment to maintain availability additionally restricts revocation. In Dickinson v. Dodds, 2 Ch. D. 463 (C.A. 1876), the seller's promise to leave an offer open did not create a binding option, and the buyer learned before his attempted acceptance that the seller had disposed of the property to another person. The casebook uses the decision to connect revocability with indirect communication. Casebook at 171-75.
Section 43 recognizes indirect revocation when the offeror takes definite action inconsistent with an intention to enter the proposed contract and the offeree acquires reliable information of that action. A reliable report of a completed sale to another buyer can satisfy that rule for an offer to sell a particular parcel. Rumor, an ambiguous statement that the seller is considering other buyers, or information that the offeree never receives presents different facts. The required analysis includes both the inconsistent action and the offeree's reliable information about it. A secret sale alone does not automatically establish the offeree's knowledge.
A public offer can require a different method of withdrawal because the offeror may be unable to identify everyone who learned of it. Under § 46, an offer made by general public announcement can be revoked through publicity comparable to the original announcement when no better means is reasonably available. A person who saw the original announcement may therefore lose the power to accept even without seeing the revocation. The adequacy of the withdrawal depends on the original publication, the available alternatives, and the circumstances. This qualification matters when applying advertisement and reward cases such as
Lefkowitz. Casebook at 174-75.
An option contract is an enforceable promise that limits the offeror's power to revoke an offer. A separate bargained-for payment for keeping an offer open is the familiar example. Restatement § 87(1)(a) also recognizes, subject to its conditions and jurisdictional adoption, a signed written offer that recites purported consideration and proposes a fair exchange within a reasonable time. The casebook notes disagreement among courts concerning an unpaid recital of consideration. The existence of an option must therefore be established from the relevant facts and law. If an option exists, it protects the opportunity to accept according to its terms; it does not itself complete the underlying purchase. Restatement §§ 25, 87; casebook at 176-77.
For goods, UCC § 2-205 makes certain merchant offers irrevocable without consideration. The offer must be in a signed writing and give assurance that it will be held open. The assurance binds for the stated time or, if no time is stated, a reasonable time, with a maximum of three months of irrevocability under this provision. If the assurance appears on a form supplied by the offeree, the offeror must separately sign that assurance. The three-month limit concerns protection supplied by this rule without consideration; a separately supported option can have a longer duration. Expiration of the protected period may leave an offer revocable but still open if it has not otherwise expired or terminated. UCC § 2-205.
The casebook also identifies New York's broader statutory exception. Subject to the UCC's rule for merchant offers involving goods, General Obligations Law § 5-1109 enforces a signed written assurance that an offer is irrevocable for a specified period or until a fixed time despite the absence of consideration for that assurance. If the writing states that the offer is irrevocable without stating a duration, it supplies a reasonable period. A New York services or real-estate transaction may therefore require a different answer from the ordinary common-law rule. N.Y. Gen. Oblig. Law § 5-1109; casebook at 177.
Beginning performance and reliance can also affect revocability. Under § 45, when an offer invites acceptance only by performance, beginning or tendering the invited performance creates an option, while the offeror's duty remains conditional on completion or tender according to the offer. Preparations do not automatically constitute that beginning. Section 87(2) separately addresses an offer that the offeror should reasonably expect to induce substantial action or forbearance before acceptance and that does induce it; the offer can bind as an option to the extent necessary to avoid injustice. These doctrines require distinct findings concerning what the offer requested, what the offeree did, and why enforcement is justified.
The casebook illustrates that distinction through Ragosta v. Wilder, 156 Vt. 390, 592 A.2d 367 (1991). The prospective buyers arranged financing in response to a proposed cash purchase of property, but the seller withdrew before the specified exchange at the bank. The Vermont Supreme Court treated financing arrangements as preparation rather than the requested performance and rejected the trial court's contract and equitable-estoppel grounds. It remanded for further consideration involving promissory estoppel. The decision therefore separates formation by performance from possible protection of reliance. In a new problem, a reliance argument requires its own factual and doctrinal support even when acceptance fails.
Casebook at 177-80.
Under § 48, death or loss of legal capacity of either the offeror or offeree ordinarily terminates the power to accept an ordinary offer, even when the other party has no notice. That rule creates tension with the objective approach to assent because a surviving offeree may reasonably believe the offer remains available.
The Restatement and casebook nevertheless identify it as the ordinary rule. Under § 37, the offeror's death or incapacity does not ordinarily terminate the power under an option contract. Death after a completed contract raises separate questions about surviving duties, the estate, and whether personal performance was essential. Casebook at 180-81.
Rejection is the offeree's manifestation of an intention to decline the offer. It ordinarily terminates the power when received, without requiring proof that the offeror relied on it. A counteroffer proposes a substituted bargain concerning the same subject and ordinarily has the same terminating effect, unless the parties indicate otherwise. Under the common-law mirror-image principle, a purported acceptance that makes agreement conditional on changed terms is a counteroffer. A definite acceptance accompanied by a request for a change may still accept if the requested change is not a condition of assent. That distinction, reflected in §§ 59 and 61, prevents the mere presence of a question from deciding the case. Restatement §§ 38-40, 59,
61; casebook at 182-84.
Under § 37, rejection, counteroffer, revocation, or the offeror's death or incapacity ordinarily does not end an option holder's power of acceptance, subject to rules governing discharge of the contractual duty. The holder has an existing contractual right to the protected opportunity, and an ineffective attempt to exercise it may leave that right available for timely, conforming exercise. Reliance, release, or other grounds of discharge may still matter. The casebook cautions that an option supported by consideration and a statutory merchant firm offer may require separate analysis when an offeree first rejects and then changes course. The terms and legal basis of irrevocability should be identified before applying an exception. Casebook at
184-85.
For any termination problem, determine whether an offer existed, identify the invited form of acceptance, establish any protection against revocation, and put the relevant events in order. Then apply the effective-time rule to each event. A posted revocation, a received revocation, a mailed acceptance, a rejection, and expiration of an option can have different legal dates. The mailbox rule supplies several of the timing rules needed to finish that analysis.
