Federal Rule 12: Defenses, Objections, and Timing

Source: added-sources/CivPro_Notice_Service_and_Hearing_Briefs.pdf, pages 20–21

== Sources ==
Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12 (as amended effective December 1, 2024), read with Rules 4(d) and 7(a).
Rule 12 sets the time to respond to a complaint, lists the defenses a defendant may raise by motion, and determines which defenses are lost if not raised promptly. The syllabus pairs it with Rule 4 because Rule
12(b)(4) and (5) are the vehicles for challenging defective process and service, and because the casebook's note on Roller v. Holly asks how much time a defendant has to respond. Rule 12.
Time to Respond: Rule 12(a)
A defendant must serve an answer within 21 days after being served with the summons and complaint. If the defendant timely waived service under Rule 4(d), the answer is due 60 days after the request for waiver was sent, or 90 days if it was sent to the defendant outside any judicial district of the United States. A party must answer a counterclaim or crossclaim within 21 days after service, and a reply ordered by the court within 21 days after service of the order unless the order specifies otherwise. The United States, its agencies, and its officers and employees sued in their official capacities have 60 days after service on the United States attorney; officers and employees sued individually for acts connected with federal duties have 60 days after service on the officer or the United States attorney, whichever is later. The 2024 amendment made clear that a different time set by a federal statute controls.
Serving a Rule 12 motion alters these periods. If the court denies the motion or postpones deciding it until trial, the responsive pleading is due 14 days after notice of the court's action; if the court grants a motion for a more definite statement, it is due 14 days after that statement is served. A defendant who files a Rule 12 motion therefore does not also have to answer until the motion is resolved.
The Seven Defenses: Rule 12(b)
Every defense must be asserted in the responsive pleading if one is required, but a party may raise seven defenses by motion: (1) lack of subject-matter jurisdiction, meaning the court's authority over the type of case; (2) lack of personal jurisdiction, meaning the court's power over the defendant; (3) improper venue, meaning the wrong district; (4) insufficient process, a defect in the summons itself, such as a missing seal or signature, the wrong defendant's name, or a wrong response time; (5) insufficient service of process, a defect in how or when the summons was delivered, such as leaving papers with someone who does not reside at the defendant's abode or serving after the Rule 4(m) period; (6) failure to state a claim upon which relief can be granted; and (7) failure to join a party required under Rule 19. A motion asserting any of these defenses must be made before pleading if a responsive pleading is allowed.
No defense or objection is waived by joining it with other defenses or objections in a responsive pleading or motion. That sentence abolished the special appearance in federal court. Under older practice, reflected in
Mullane's special appearance before the Surrogate, a defendant who wanted to contest jurisdiction had to appear solely for that purpose, and raising any other matter could be treated as a general appearance that consented to jurisdiction. Under Rule 12, a defendant may combine jurisdictional objections with defenses on the merits in the same filing.
Other Rule 12 Motions
After the pleadings are closed, but early enough not to delay trial, a party may move for judgment on the pleadings under Rule 12(c). If, on a Rule 12(b)(6) or 12(c) motion, the court considers matters outside the pleadings, Rule 12(d) requires it to treat the motion as one for summary judgment under Rule 56 and give all parties a reasonable opportunity to present pertinent material. Rule 12(e) permits a motion for a more definite statement when a pleading is so vague or ambiguous that the party cannot reasonably prepare a response; the motion must be made before responding and must point out the defects. Rule 12(f) permits the court to strike an insufficient defense or any redundant, immaterial, impertinent, or scandalous matter. Under Rule 12(i), on a party's motion, any Rule 12(b) defense and any Rule 12(c) motion must be heard and decided before trial unless the court defers it.
Consolidation and Waiver: Rules 12(g) and 12(h)
A Rule 12 motion may be joined with any other motion allowed by the rule. Once a party makes a Rule 12 motion, it may not make another motion under the rule raising a defense or objection that was available but omitted, except as Rule 12(h)(2) and (3) allow.
The defenses listed in Rule 12(b)(2) through (5), covering personal jurisdiction, venue, process, and service, are waived if omitted from a Rule 12 motion when they were available, or if they are neither made by motion nor included in the responsive pleading or an amendment allowed as a matter of course under Rule 15(a)(1).
A defendant who moves only under Rule 12(b)(6) therefore forfeits objections to personal jurisdiction, venue, process, and service. The defenses of failure to state a claim, failure to join a required party, and failure to state a legal defense to a claim are preserved longer: they may be raised in any pleading allowed under Rule 7(a), by a Rule 12(c) motion, or at trial. Lack of subject-matter jurisdiction is never waived; if the court determines at any time that it lacks subject-matter jurisdiction, it must dismiss the action.
Appearing, Defaulting, and Collateral Attack
A defendant who believes the court lacks personal jurisdiction or that service was defective has two broad options. It may appear and raise the objection under Rule 12, in which case the court's ruling on jurisdiction binds it and cannot be relitigated when the judgment is later enforced, Baldwin v. Iowa State Traveling Men's
Ass'n, 283 U.S. 522 (1931). Or it may stay away, suffer a default judgment, and challenge jurisdiction when the plaintiff seeks to enforce the judgment, as Newhouse did in Wyman. The second course preserves the jurisdictional objection but forfeits any defense on the merits if the jurisdictional challenge fails. Separately, waiving service under Rule 4(d) does not waive objections to personal jurisdiction or venue, which remain subject to Rule 12(h)(1).

== Application ==
Calculate the response deadline from the date of service or, if service was waived, from the date the waiver request was sent. Before filing anything, list every available Rule 12(b) defense. Personal jurisdiction, venue, process, and service objections must appear in the first Rule 12 motion, or in the answer if no motion is made, or they are lost. Distinguish a defect in the summons (Rule 12(b)(4)) from a defect in delivery or timing (Rule 12(b)(5)). Consider whether matters outside the pleadings will convert a Rule 12(b)(6) motion into a summary-judgment motion. Remember that subject-matter jurisdiction can be raised at any time, and that failure to state a claim can be raised through trial.
