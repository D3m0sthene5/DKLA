Perkins v. Benguet Consolidated Mining Co.
Perkins v. Benguet Consolidated Mining Co., 342 U.S. 437 (1952). United States Supreme Court, decided
March 3, 1952. Justice Burton delivered the Court's opinion. Justice Black concurred in the result. Justice
Minton dissented, joined by Chief Justice Vinson. This is the Benguet decision discussed in Goodyear and later general-jurisdiction cases.
Full judicial opinion.

== Parties ==
Idonah Slade Perkins, the plaintiff and Supreme Court petitioner, claimed rights as a shareholder of Benguet
Consolidated Mining Company. The Supreme Court described her as a nonresident of Ohio. Benguet, the defendant and respondent, was a Philippine mining corporation organized as a sociedad anonima. Its president was also its general manager and principal stockholder. His activities in Ohio were the corporate activities on which the jurisdictional question turned.

== Material Facts ==
Benguet owned and operated gold and silver mines in the Philippines. During the Japanese occupation in
World War II, mining operations stopped. The company's president returned to his home in Clermont
County, Ohio, where he conducted the company's business during the wartime interruption and the period immediately afterward. The company's connection to Ohio involved management of its affairs, rather than sales to customers or purchases of supplies alone.
The president maintained an office in Ohio, kept corporate files there, and carried on company correspondence. He drew and distributed salary checks for himself and two secretaries, maintained substantial corporate bank accounts, used an Ohio bank as a stock-transfer agent, and held directors' meetings. He supervised policies for rehabilitating the Philippine mining properties and directed purchases of machinery and the use of funds necessary for that purpose. Although the mines and the underlying assets remained abroad, Ohio was the place from which this continuing corporate activity was directed. The president was personally served in Ohio while acting there for the corporation; the adequacy of the company's actual notice was not disputed.
Perkins sought approximately $68,400 in allegedly unpaid dividends in one action and approximately $2.5 million in damages for an alleged failure to issue 120,000 shares in another. Her claims did not arise from
Benguet's Ohio activities. That absence of a claim-forum connection made the extent and nature of the company's Ohio operations decisive. 342 U.S. at 439-40, 447-48.

== Procedural History ==
Perkins brought two actions in the Court of Common Pleas of Clermont County, Ohio. Benguet challenged the court's jurisdiction, and the trial court quashed service in each case. The Ohio Court of Appeals affirmed.
The Ohio Supreme Court consolidated the cases and affirmed as well, 155 Ohio St. 116, 98 N.E.2d 33 (1951).
The basis of the Ohio Supreme Court's judgment was important to federal review. Its syllabus stated that the company could not be sued in Ohio on the unrelated claims, but did not clearly establish whether the conclusion rested on Ohio law or on the Federal Constitution. Justice Taft's explanatory opinion understood federal due process to forbid jurisdiction. It was uncertain whether all the judges supporting the judgment agreed with that rationale. If Ohio independently chose not to entertain the actions as a matter of state law, the United States Supreme Court would not require it to do so. If Ohio refused jurisdiction because it believed the Federal Constitution compelled that refusal, the premise was reviewable and incorrect.
The Supreme Court therefore addressed the federal limit while leaving Ohio's own choice of jurisdictional policy for the state courts. Its review did not decide the shareholder claims or resolve whether state law ultimately authorized the suits. The jurisdictional record also presented actual service on the company's president during the very activities supporting jurisdiction, avoiding a separate problem of ineffective or unauthorized notice. 342 U.S. at 440-45.

== Issue ==
Does the Fourteenth Amendment prevent Ohio from exercising personal jurisdiction over a Philippine corporation on claims unrelated to Ohio when the corporation's president continuously conducts its business from an Ohio office during the suspension of its Philippine operations? If the Constitution permits jurisdiction, must Ohio exercise it?

== Holding ==
Due process permitted Ohio to entertain these unrelated claims because Benguet's continuous and systematic corporate activities in Ohio were sufficiently substantial. The Constitution did not compel Ohio to provide that forum. The state remained free to permit or withhold jurisdiction under its own law, subject to other applicable constitutional requirements. Later cases treat Perkins as an example of general jurisdiction outside the ordinary places of incorporation and principal place of business, because the forum functioned as the corporation's temporary center of operations. 342 U.S. at 445-49.

== Judgment ==
The Supreme Court vacated the Ohio Supreme Court's judgment and remanded for further proceedings. The state court had to reconsider the cases with the correct understanding that federal due process did not prohibit jurisdiction on these facts. Perkins did not receive a damages judgment or a federal command that the Ohio courts hear her claims. The disposition preserved a possible independent state-law reason for declining jurisdiction.

== Reasoning ==
The Court applied International Shoe's focus on the quality and nature of corporate activity. The relevant question was whether the company's connection to Ohio made it fair to require a defense there, even though these claims arose elsewhere. Service on a corporate official could give notice, but notice alone did not establish the substantive authority to adjudicate. Both the official's actual role and the corporation's business in the forum mattered.
The corporate work conducted in Ohio was extensive and central. The president managed corporate correspondence, finances, directors' meetings, compensation, and rehabilitation of the mines. His Ohio office carried on the company's affairs while wartime conditions prevented its normal overseas operations. Those functions gave the corporation a sustained relationship with the state and made this more than the occasional presence of an officer or a series of isolated purchases. The Court therefore found no federal due-process obstacle to a suit unrelated to the Ohio work.
Benguet's lack of an Ohio business license or specially designated statutory service agent did not determine the constitutional question. The company was actually carrying on sufficient business there, and the president's service supplied proper notice. Earlier decisions involving service on public officers without an adequate basis for binding an absent corporation did not control this situation. The Court's reasoning looked to actual corporate activity and notice rather than treating registration as the sole source of adjudicatory authority.
The Court carefully separated a federal permission from a state obligation. The Fourteenth Amendment limits state power; it does not invariably require a state to use every measure of jurisdiction the Constitution would allow. Because the Ohio judgment might have rested on a mistaken view of that limit, vacatur allowed the Ohio courts to decide what their own law required without the mistaken federal premise. This procedural distinction is central to the holding. 342 U.S. at 444-49.

== Separate Opinions ==
Justice Black concurred in the result without a separate explanation. Justice Minton, joined by Chief Justice
Vinson, would have dismissed the writ of certiorari as improvidently granted. He regarded the Ohio Supreme
Court's authoritative syllabus as a state-law ground adequate to support the judgment, and considered Justice
Taft's federal analysis insufficient to establish that the court itself had rested on federal law. On that view, the Supreme Court's constitutional discussion answered a question that could not change the state judgment.
The disagreement concerned the basis for federal appellate review as well as the practical effect of the remand.

== Notes ==
The modern significance of Perkins is the unusual nature of the forum's role in the corporation's affairs. It does not mean that every corporation with an office, executive, or bank account in a state is subject to unrelated suits there. Goodyear and Daimler use it as the exceptional case in which operations outside the ordinary corporate homes are sufficiently central to support all-purpose jurisdiction. Keep the decision's permissive holding separate from its disposition: the Supreme Court removed a supposed federal prohibition and returned the state-law question to Ohio.
