Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Judicial District Court
Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Judicial District Court, 592 U.S. 351, 141 S. Ct. 1017 (2021), consolidated with Ford Motor Co. v. Bandemer, Nos. 19-368 and 19-369. The United States Supreme Court heard argument on October 7, 2020, and decided the cases on March 25, 2021. Justice Kagan wrote the five-Justice majority opinion, joined by Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Breyer, Sotomayor, and
Kavanaugh. Justice Alito concurred in the judgment. Justice Gorsuch also concurred in the judgment, joined by Justice Thomas. All eight participating Justices agreed that jurisdiction was proper; Justice Barrett did not participate. Kagan's opinion supplies the controlling rationale. The concurrences identify disagreements about that rationale, and there was no dissent from the Supreme Court's judgment.
Full judicial opinions. Pinpoints to the majority and concurrences refer to their separately numbered slip-opinion pages.

== Parties ==
Ford Motor Company was the defendant in both underlying damages actions and the petitioner in both
Supreme Court proceedings. It was incorporated in Delaware and headquartered in Michigan. In Montana,
Charles Lucero, the personal representative of Markkaya Gullett's estate, brought the underlying action on behalf of Gullett and her heirs. In Minnesota, Adam Bandemer was the injured plaintiff and the respondent opposing Ford's jurisdictional challenge. Bandemer also sued Eric Hanson, the driver, and Hanson's father, who owned the vehicle, for negligence. Their alleged responsibility was separate from the defect claims against Ford. Majority, slip op. at 2-3; Montana opinion ¶ 2; Bandemer, 931 N.W.2d 744, 747-48 (Minn.
2019).
The Montana trial court appears in the Supreme Court caption because Ford had sought supervisory control over that court's exercise of jurisdiction. The underlying tort action was Lucero v. Ford Motor Co.; the jurisdictional proceeding challenged the Montana Eighth Judicial District Court's authority to continue hearing it. The caption therefore reflects the route of review. Ford was seeking dismissal of Lucero's claims against it, and the trial court was the object of the requested supervisory relief. Montana opinion ¶¶ 3-7;
majority, slip op. at 3 n.1.

== Material Facts ==
In 2015, Montana resident Markkaya Gullett was driving a 1996 Ford Explorer on a Montana interstate near her home. A rear tire experienced tread separation. The Explorer lost stability, rolled into a ditch, and came to rest upside down; Gullett died at the scene. Lucero alleged strict liability for a design defect, strict liability for failure to warn, and negligence, and sought compensatory and punitive damages. The jurisdictional decision treated the claimed defect and Ford's responsibility as allegations. It did not establish why the tire failed or adjudicate the merits of the Explorer's design. Montana opinion ¶ 2; majority, slip op. at 2.
Ford Motor Co.
Ford designed the Explorer in Michigan, assembled it in Kentucky, and initially sold it to a dealer in
Washington. More than ten years later, the vehicle was resold and registered in Montana. Its eventual presence in Montana followed later consumer transactions and relocation, rather than Ford's original sale of that particular Explorer into Montana. The place of initial sale supplied the factual basis for Ford's argument that its own Montana business had not generated this claim. Montana opinion ¶ 2; majority, slip op. at 3.
In January 2015, Minnesota resident Adam Bandemer rode as a passenger in a 1994 Ford Crown Victoria driven by his friend Eric Hanson on a rural Minnesota road. They were traveling to an ice-fishing location when Hanson rear-ended a county snowplow and the car went into a ditch. Bandemer alleged that the passenger-side airbag failed to deploy and that he suffered severe brain injury as a result. Minnesota officers responded, and Minnesota physicians treated him. His complaint asserted products-liability, negligence, and breach-of-warranty claims against Ford and negligence claims against Hanson and Hanson's father. The complaint attributed the collision to Hanson's negligence and the airbag's failure to a defect. These were distinct alleged causes of the accident and resulting injury. Bandemer, 931 N.W.2d at 747-48; majority, slip op. at 2-3.
Ford designed the Crown Victoria in Michigan, manufactured it in Canada, and originally sold the particular vehicle in North Dakota. Later transactions brought it into Minnesota, where it was registered. Thus, as in
Montana, the state of the accident was also the injured person's home, but differed from the places where
Ford had designed, manufactured, and initially sold the individual car. Bandemer's status as a passenger also shows why a jurisdictional rule framed around the injured person's own purchase would fit the underlying litigation poorly. Majority, slip op. at 3; Bandemer, 931 N.W.2d at 754.
Ford deliberately and repeatedly served both states' automobile markets. It advertised through television, radio, billboards, print media, and direct mail, including promotion of Explorers and Crown Victorias at the relevant times. Those models were available through its dealership network, which included 36 dealerships in Montana and 84 in Minnesota. Minnesota's record included sales of more than 2,000 model-year 1994
Crown Victorias and approximately 200,000 vehicles of all types during 2013-2015. The Supreme Court relied on the substantial business Ford conducted in each particular forum. Its national size supplied context, while its state-specific conduct supplied the relevant contacts. Majority, slip op. at 3-4, 11-12; Bandemer,
931 N.W.2d at 748, 751.
Ford's relationship with those markets continued after a vehicle's first sale. Its dealers sold used Fords, maintained and repaired vehicles after their warranties expired, and supplied continuing services to owners.
Ford distributed replacement parts to both authorized dealers and independent repair shops. These activities made long-term ownership practical and supported the resale market through which the older vehicles could reach local residents. Ford earned money from those activities and encouraged residents to continue driving its vehicles. Minnesota's court also considered Ford's collection of vehicle-performance information through local dealerships for design and repair purposes. The Supreme Court's own explanation centered on advertising, sales, service, parts, and the resulting market for the relevant models. Majority, slip op. at 2,
11-14; Bandemer, 931 N.W.2d at 751, 754.
Ford Motor Co.

== Procedural History ==
Lucero filed the action in Montana's Eighth Judicial District Court, Cascade County, No. DV-18-247. Ford moved to dismiss for lack of personal jurisdiction, arguing that its Montana contacts lacked the necessary connection to Lucero's claims. The district court rejected the objection. Ford then petitioned the Montana
Supreme Court to exercise supervisory control, overturn the jurisdictional ruling, and direct dismissal. This was review of a threshold ruling while the tort litigation remained pending. Montana opinion ¶¶ 2-4;
majority, slip op. at 3 n.1.
The Montana Supreme Court accepted supervisory control under its state constitutional authority and
Montana Rule of Appellate Procedure 14(3). It described supervisory control as an extraordinary remedy and concluded that ordinary appellate review was inadequate because of the urgency of deciding the trial court's power over the parties. The petition presented a purely legal question of statewide constitutional importance concerning Ford's due-process rights. The court reviewed the personal-jurisdiction ruling de novo, meaning that it independently decided the legal issue rather than deferring to the district court's legal conclusion.
Accepting the petition gave Ford immediate review; it did not mean that Ford was entitled to dismissal.
Montana opinion ¶¶ 5-7.
On May 21, 2019, the Montana Supreme Court upheld jurisdiction. It first found authorization under
Montana Rule of Civil Procedure 4(b)(1)(B), which covers claims arising from an act resulting in the accrual of a tort action within Montana. The alleged misconduct had resulted in an accident and injury there. It then separately addressed federal due process and concluded that Ford deliberately served Montana's market and that the local accident sufficiently related to those activities. Its published decision is Ford Motor Co. v.
Montana Eighth Judicial District Court, 2019 MT 115, 395 Mont. 478, 443 P.3d 407. Montana opinion ¶¶
7-12; majority, slip op. at 3-4.
Bandemer filed his action in Minnesota's Seventh Judicial District Court, Todd County, No. 77-cv-16-1025.
Ford moved to dismiss under Minnesota Rule of Civil Procedure 12.02(b), which permits an objection to personal jurisdiction. The district court denied the motion. Ford appealed, and the Minnesota Court of
Appeals affirmed in Bandemer v. Ford Motor Co., 913 N.W.2d 710 (Minn. Ct. App. 2018). Applying
Minnesota precedent, that court found Ford's directed advertising and marketing sufficiently related to the claims. Ford then obtained review by the Minnesota Supreme Court. Majority, slip op. at 3 n.1; Bandemer,
931 N.W.2d at 747-49.
On July 31, 2019, the Minnesota Supreme Court affirmed. It reviewed personal jurisdiction as a question of law de novo. Under the state's approach at the motion-to-dismiss stage, Bandemer had to make a prima facie showing of jurisdiction: allegations and supporting materials sufficient to establish jurisdiction if accepted.
The court accepted the complaint's allegations and supporting affidavits as true under the applicable standard and discussed Ford's discovery responses when evaluating the claimed contacts. Its footnote concerning vehicle-performance data explains that an allegation remained accepted unless Ford's discovery response directly contradicted it. This procedural treatment of allegations supported the jurisdictional inquiry; it did not establish a defect or entitlement to damages at trial. Bandemer, 931 N.W.2d at 749, 751 n.3.
Ford Motor Co.
Minnesota's long-arm statute extended jurisdiction to the limits of federal due process, so the constitutional inquiry governed. Ford conceded the quantity and quality of its Minnesota contacts and the reasonableness of litigating there; it contested the connection between those contacts and Bandemer's claims. The court found that Ford's sales of the relevant model, marketing, and collection of performance information were sufficiently connected to the alleged defect and local accident. Justice Anderson dissented, joined by Chief
Justice Gildea, because he considered Ford's Minnesota activities unrelated to this particular vehicle and claim. Bandemer also advanced a consent theory based on Ford's registered agent. Both Minnesota appellate courts left that alternative undecided because specific jurisdiction resolved the dispute. Bandemer, 931
N.W.2d at 748-55 & n.1; id. at 755 (Anderson, J., dissenting).
Ford petitioned for certiorari from both state supreme court judgments. The Supreme Court granted review and consolidated the cases, then affirmed on March 25, 2021. The Court reviewed a federal constitutional limit on state adjudicatory authority. These cases reached it through the state courts; they did not begin as federal district court diversity actions. The immediate relief Ford sought was dismissal for lack of personal jurisdiction, which would have required the plaintiffs to pursue Ford in another available forum. Majority, slip op. at 1-4, 18.
The affirmance preserved the denials of Ford's dismissal motions. The Supreme Court issued no damages award, found no product defect, and imposed no tort liability. Nor did consolidation turn the two separate accidents into a single underlying tort action. It allowed the Court to resolve the common jurisdictional question in one decision while each state's litigation retained its own plaintiff, claims, evidence, and procedural history. Majority, slip op. at 1-4, 18.

== Issue ==
Does the Fourteenth Amendment permit a state court to exercise specific personal jurisdiction over a manufacturer when the manufacturer extensively markets, sells, and services the relevant vehicle model in the state, and that model allegedly injures a resident in a local accident, although the manufacturer designed, manufactured, and first sold the particular vehicle elsewhere? More specifically, must the plaintiff prove that the manufacturer's conduct in the forum caused the claim, or can a sufficiently close relationship between that conduct and the local controversy satisfy the requirement that the claim arise out of or relate to the defendant's forum contacts? Majority, slip op. at 1, 7-9.

== Holding ==
A court first needs authorization under the applicable state jurisdictional law and must then remain within the Fourteenth Amendment's limits. Montana examined those steps separately; Minnesota interpreted its statute to reach the constitutional boundary. International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310, 316-19 (1945), supplies the constitutional framework: the defendant must have sufficient contacts with the forum for the exercise of jurisdiction to comport with traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice. A state rule authorizing jurisdiction does not by itself resolve that constitutional question. Montana opinion ¶¶ 10-12;
Bandemer, 931 N.W.2d at 749; majority, slip op. at 4-7.
Ford Motor Co.
General jurisdiction permits adjudication of claims regardless of their relationship to the forum. For a corporation, its place of incorporation and principal place of business are the ordinary places where it is at home; exceptional circumstances can establish another home. The parties agreed that Ford was subject to general jurisdiction in Delaware and Michigan and was not at home in Montana or Minnesota. The Court therefore needed to determine whether these particular claims supported specific jurisdiction. Extensive local sales mattered to that inquiry without turning either forum into a place where Ford could be sued on every claim. Majority, slip op. at 5-6, 9 n.3.
Specific jurisdiction requires the defendant's own purposeful contact with the state and a sufficient connection between those contacts and the particular claims. Purposeful availment means that the defendant deliberately takes advantage of opportunities to conduct activities in the forum, such as serving its market or undertaking a relationship centered there. A third party's unilateral movement cannot supply the defendant's own conduct. The claims must then "arise out of or relate to" the relevant contacts. Finally, the exercise of jurisdiction must be reasonable in light of the defendant's position and the interests at stake. In Ford, purposeful availment was conceded, so the disputed issue was the required relationship between Ford's admitted local activities and the suits. Majority, slip op. at 5-9, 14-15.
Montana and Minnesota could exercise specific personal jurisdiction over Ford in these two products-liability suits. Ford extensively advertised, sold, and serviced the same vehicle models in each state; each accident occurred there and injured a state resident. Those facts sufficiently connected Ford, the forum, and the particular litigation even though Ford had designed, manufactured, and initially sold the individual vehicles elsewhere.

== Judgment ==
The Supreme Court affirmed both state supreme court judgments. Ford's motions to dismiss for lack of personal jurisdiction remained denied, and the underlying damages actions could proceed in Montana and
Minnesota. The Court did not determine whether either vehicle was defective, whether Ford caused the injuries, or the amount of damages. Its decision resolved the challenged constitutional basis for jurisdiction, leaving the merits for the trial courts. Majority, slip op. at 18.

== Reasoning ==
Ford proposed a causation requirement focused on the individual vehicle. In its account, the state could exercise specific jurisdiction only if Ford's conduct there gave rise to the plaintiff's claim. The relevant places would therefore be the state where Ford initially sold the car, or the places where it designed or manufactured it. Later consumer resales could not transform unrelated advertising and sales of other vehicles into conduct causing these injuries. Ford emphasized that, even if it had never operated in Montana or
Minnesota, the particular vehicles could have arrived through the same resale transactions and produced the same claims. It invoked Bristol-Myers Squibb and Walden to argue that substantial business and the plaintiff's local injury could not replace a connection to the defendant's own conduct. Majority, slip op. at 3,
7-9, 13, 15-18.
Ford Motor Co.
The plaintiffs defended jurisdiction based on Ford's sustained efforts to develop and support the local market for the very models alleged to be defective. The relevant relationship included Ford's promotion of local ownership, sales of the models, repair and parts networks, and an accident injuring a resident within that market. They opposed making jurisdiction depend on tracing an older car back to Ford's first sale or proving that a particular advertisement induced a particular purchase. Their position treated the local accident as related to Ford's ongoing business in that state. The majority accepted that connection. Majority, slip op. at
11-15; Bandemer, 931 N.W.2d at 751-54.
Kagan gave independent significance to both parts of the established phrase "arise out of or relate to." The first part concerns causation. The second permits some relationships to support jurisdiction without proof that the defendant's forum conduct caused the plaintiff's claim. Ford's proposed rule would eliminate that second route by demanding causation in every case. The majority found no basis in its precedents for that universal requirement. It nevertheless insisted that relatedness has real limits: the defendant's activities, the state, and the specific litigation must remain sufficiently connected. Majority, slip op. at 8-9.
The distinction concerns jurisdictional causation. It addresses whether Ford's Montana or Minnesota activities must have caused these particular claims to arise. The plaintiffs' substantive claims still required whatever proof of defect, breach, injury, and causal responsibility the governing law demanded. A decision that a forum can adjudicate a negligence or products-liability claim does not establish that Ford caused the collision or enhanced the injuries. This separation follows from the motions the Court reviewed and the limited jurisdictional judgments it affirmed. Majority, slip op. at 2-4, 8-9, 18.
The Court connected Ford's activities to the claims at the level of the relevant vehicle models and the market
Ford systematically served. Explorers and Crown Victorias were among the products it advertised, sold, serviced, and supported in these states. The suits alleged that those models malfunctioned while being used there and injured local residents. That combination supplied a concrete connection to the controversy. Ford's other business elsewhere, its overall wealth, or the mere existence of some unrelated local enterprise would not describe the relationship on which the Court relied. Majority, slip op. at 11-12.
The movement of these individual vehicles through the used-car market was compatible with Ford's own business strategy. Ford encouraged resale, stocked replacement parts, and supported repairs long after the first sale. A purchaser could therefore choose an older Ford bought elsewhere partly because of services and parts available at home. The majority identified that possibility to explain why the litigation fit Ford's local activities, while expressly acknowledging that the plaintiffs had neither alleged nor established such individual causal links. The decision does not rest on a finding that Gullett, Bandemer, or the Crown
Victoria's owner actually relied on an advertisement or repair network. Majority, slip op. at 12-14.
Ford Motor Co.
The Court also rejected requiring proof of the particular reasons for a purchase. Its footnote explains that jurisdiction should remain available on these facts even if a plaintiff recently moved into the forum with a car purchased without the move in mind. Ford's independent, established relationship with the destination state's market remains relevant when the vehicle injures a resident there. That observation preserves the defendant-contact requirement: the move changes where the product is used, while Ford's own market activities supply the purposeful forum contacts. Majority, slip op. at 14 & n.5.
World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson, 444 U.S. 286 (1980), involved an automobile sold in New York that later caused an alleged injury in Oklahoma. The regional distributor and retailer challenging jurisdiction had not served Oklahoma's market, so the consumer's trip did not establish jurisdiction over them. That opinion also explained that a manufacturer or distributor deliberately serving a state's market could face suit there when its product caused local injury. Audi and the nationwide Volkswagen importer had not challenged jurisdiction in that litigation. Ford's extensive Montana and Minnesota operations placed it within the manufacturer-and-market situation discussed in World-Wide Volkswagen. Majority, slip op. at 9-11.
Ford correctly observed that the earlier opinion's discussion of the nonchallenging manufacturers was dictum. Kagan acknowledged that status and explained that later decisions repeatedly endorsed the same principle, including Keeton, Burger King, Goodyear, the O'Connor opinion in Asahi, and Daimler. Ford applied that principle to actual jurisdictional challenges by the manufacturer. The warning relevant to due process came from Ford's deliberate market activities and the possibility of suits concerning local failures of its vehicles. Mere foreseeability that a movable car might cross a state line would not supply that relationship. Majority, slip op. at 9-11, 14-15.
Ford benefited from the states' enforcement of contracts, protection of property, and maintenance of markets in which it sold and supported vehicles. The Court viewed adjudication of related local product injuries as a reciprocal responsibility accompanying those benefits. Ford could anticipate those suits and account for litigation costs through insurance, pricing, or changes to its forum activities. This reasoning connected fairness to Ford's deliberate conduct and the nature of the claims. It did not announce that a defendant's size or ability to afford litigation independently supplies jurisdiction. Majority, slip op. at 6-7, 10, 14-15.
Montana and Minnesota had direct interests in providing remedies for injured residents and regulating the safety of products used on their roads. The states of initial sale, Washington and North Dakota, had a more remote connection to these particular disputes: an earlier owner had purchased a car there many years before an accident elsewhere involving other residents. Ford's proposed rule would channel the suits toward those states despite the accident states' substantial interests. The majority used that comparison to support jurisdiction in Montana and Minnesota. It did not hold that the initial-sale states necessarily lacked jurisdiction; the availability of other forums was not the question decided. Majority, slip op. at 15; Gorsuch concurrence at 3-4.
Ford Motor Co.
For the familiar fairness analysis, these facts support attention to Ford's litigation burden, the forum's regulatory and remedial interests, the plaintiffs' access to relief, and the efficient resolution of a local accident dispute. Minnesota's opinion expressly noted local treatment, the local accident, and two resident codefendants, and recorded Ford's concession on reasonableness. The United States Supreme Court did not resolve a disputed five-factor balance in a separate checklist; it explained fairness and federalism as reasons supporting the jurisdictional connection established by the facts. Convenience alone could not supply missing purposeful contacts or a missing relationship to the claim. Bandemer, 931 N.W.2d at 754-55;
majority, slip op. at 6-7, 14-15.
In Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court, 582 U.S. 255 (2017), nonresident plaintiffs sued in
California over Plavix prescribed, obtained, used, and alleged to have caused injury elsewhere. The defendant's California sales and the presence of similar claims by Californians did not connect those nonresidents' own claims to California. Ford read that result as making an out-of-state sale fatal to jurisdiction. Kagan instead emphasized the absence of a forum connection to those plaintiffs' particular injuries. Here, the relevant products were used in the forum, the accidents and injuries occurred there, the injured people were residents, and Ford served the local market for those products. Majority, slip op. at
15-17.
The two decisions consequently require a claim-specific inquiry even when the defendant does substantial business throughout the country. Ford's Montana sales could support the local Explorer suit on these facts without supporting an Ohio resident's suit over a purchase, use, and accident occurring entirely in Ohio. The majority expressly rejected eliminating the connection requirement for nationwide companies. The volume of unrelated local business does not compensate for an absent connection between the forum and the particular controversy. Majority, slip op. at 9 n.3, 15-17.
In Walden v. Fiore, 571 U.S. 277 (2014), a Georgia officer seized money from Nevada residents at an
Atlanta airport. Their later experience of the alleged loss in Nevada could not create the officer's own contact with Nevada; he had directed no relevant activity there. Ford, by contrast, conceded extensive purposeful activity in each forum. Once those defendant-created contacts existed, the location of the accident, injury, and injured person's residence helped assess the claims' relationship to them. Walden prevents the plaintiff's connections from substituting for the defendant's contacts. It does not make the plaintiff's place of injury irrelevant to determining whether an existing defendant-forum relationship encompasses the suit. Majority, slip op. at 17-18.

== Separate Opinions ==
Alito agreed that existing precedent plainly allowed these suits. Ford maintained a substantial presence in the states, sold and serviced vehicles there, and supplied parts; residents suffered local injuries involving vehicles purchased within those states through the later market. In his view, World-Wide Volkswagen already supplied the answer. He rejected Ford's demand for the strict proof of but-for causation described in its position and agreed with the main thrust of the majority's application. Alito concurrence at 1-3.
Ford Motor Co.
His objection concerned treating "arise out of" and "relate to" as two discrete grounds for jurisdiction. He thought the Court parsed language from judicial opinions as though it were statutory text. Alito would understand the phrases together through a practical, broad conception of causation. Ford's promotion, sales, parts, and service made it reasonable to infer that vehicles of this brand were on local roads because of Ford's efforts to create and support that market. That rough causal connection was enough without proof tracing an individual purchase to a particular forum act. Alito concurrence at 3-4.
Alito worried that an independent category of noncausal relatedness would be difficult to administer. Almost any two matters can be described as related at some level, and the majority's assurance that limits existed did not clearly define them. His approach would preserve a causal idea broad enough to decide Ford while constraining remote claims. The disagreement is therefore substantive: Kagan permitted jurisdiction without a causal showing on these facts, while Alito preferred to explain the same facts as sufficiently causal in a commonsense sense. His concurrence agreed with the judgment but did not narrow the five-Justice majority's holding. Alito concurrence at 3-4; majority, slip op. at 8-9, 14.
Gorsuch also accepted jurisdiction over Ford but questioned both the majority's new formulation and the broader structure of the Court's doctrine. He viewed many lower courts as having understood the connection requirement to include at least but-for causation. Replacing that inquiry with terms such as affiliation or relationship left him uncertain about what facts would count and how much connection would suffice. He also thought evidence that local advertising or service availability induced vehicle purchases might establish a causal connection in these cases, making a new verbal formula unnecessary. The majority responded that those individualized causal facts had not been established and should not be required. Gorsuch concurrence at 1-5; majority, slip op. at 14.
He used two examples to expose uncertainty. First, he questioned whether the majority's comparison of the initial-sale states with the accident states implied that the former could not hear a suit, even though selling the defective car there might satisfy a causation test. That was his criticism of a possible implication, rather than a holding of the majority. Second, he discussed an individual Maine craftsperson who sells a defective duck decoy online to an out-of-state buyer. A sale might directly cause an injury, yet the majority distinguished isolated transactions from Ford's sustained market activity. Gorsuch asked whether the new relatedness formulation would sometimes expand jurisdiction and sometimes restrict it, and where courts should draw the line between those situations. Gorsuch concurrence at 3-5.
Gorsuch then examined the history of jurisdiction based on presence and consent, the development of rules for corporations operating across state lines, and International Shoe's replacement of much of that framework with minimum contacts. He questioned why individuals can ordinarily be served while physically present in a state and sued there on unrelated claims, while large corporations often receive general jurisdiction protection outside one or two home states despite extensive operations elsewhere. He also asked whether rules devised for companies sending agents and goods into states fit an economy in which a small online seller can reach customers nationwide. These were reasons to reconsider the doctrine through constitutional text and history. They did not produce a replacement test adopted by the Court. Gorsuch concurrence at 5-10.
Ford Motor Co.
Gorsuch concluded that Ford had deliberately entered these states to conduct business and had identified no constitutional or historical reason to avoid answering these claims there. He left broader doctrinal reconstruction for future litigation. Kagan defended deciding the cases under International Shoe and its successors, and expressly rejected a rule allowing every claim against a nationwide corporation wherever it conducts business. The controlling decision therefore retains the distinction between general and specific jurisdiction and the requirement of a connection to the particular litigation. Gorsuch concurrence at 10-11;
majority, slip op. at 7 n.2, 9 n.3.

== Notes ==
The established result concerns a manufacturer that systematically served the forum's market for the same models that allegedly injured local residents in local accidents. The Court expressly reserved a case in which
Ford marketed the model only in a different state or region. It also distinguished isolated or sporadic transactions and reserved the questions posed by internet sales. The decision establishes no minimum number of dealerships, sales, or advertisements, and it does not resolve every question about how narrowly to define the relevant product category. The safe use of the holding is to identify the particular market, the manufacturer's deliberate activities in that market, and the claim's connection to local use and injury.
Majority, slip op. at 11-13 & n.4.
Plaintiff residence strengthens the factual pattern the Court decided, but Ford does not establish a universal residency requirement for specific jurisdiction. A nonresident injured in the forum would require analysis of that person's particular claim and the defendant's relevant contacts; the majority did not decide that variation.
Conversely, moving to the forum after an entirely out-of-state accident would not reproduce Ford's combination of local product use, local malfunction, and local injury. Residence cannot create a missing contact by the defendant. Majority, slip op. at 12-18.
The opinion also does not make Montana or Minnesota the exclusive permissible forum, settle the substantive law governing the claims, decide venue, or resolve the registered-agent consent theory. Those questions have their own requirements. The significance of the judgment is that the Fourteenth Amendment permitted these state courts to hear these claims against Ford on the specific-jurisdiction ground actually litigated. Majority, slip op. at 5-6, 18; Bandemer, 931 N.W.2d at 748 n.1.
The relationship among these cases depends on the basis of jurisdiction asserted. Goodyear, Daimler, and
BNSF restrict all-purpose jurisdiction based on a corporation's business contacts. Ford concerns jurisdiction over particular claims connected to the defendant's forum business. Extensive local business therefore may be insufficient for general jurisdiction while supporting specific jurisdiction over an appropriately related local injury. Consent supplies a separate possible basis; the Minnesota courts did not need to resolve
Bandemer's registration-based consent argument.
Minnesota Supreme Court opinion, 931 N.W.2d 744 (2019). The Montana procedural discussion draws on
Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Judicial District Court, 2019 MT 115, 443 P.3d 407, especially paragraphs 2-12.
Ford Motor Co.
