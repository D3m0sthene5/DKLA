Wood v. Lucy, Lady Duff-Gordon
222 N.Y. 88, 118 N.E. 214 (1917). New York Court of Appeals. Cardozo, J. Argued November 14, 1917; decided December 4, 1917.
Source: added-sources/Contracts_Consideration_Interpretation_Big_Brief.pdf, pages 6–7

== Preamble ==
Review of 177 A.D. 624, 164 N.Y.S. 576 (App. Div. 1st Dep't 1917).

== Parties ==
Plaintiff / appellant in the Court of Appeals: Otis F. Wood, a marketer with a business organization suited to placing commercial endorsements. The agreement gave him exclusive marketing and licensing authority, and he sued for the share of revenues lost through independent endorsements. Defendant / respondent: Lucy, Lady Duff-Gordon, a prominent fashion creator whose personal approval increased the market value of designs and products. She granted the exclusive rights, retained approval authority, later made endorsements independently, and attacked the agreement's consideration on the pleadings.

== Procedural History ==
Wood filed a contract action alleging that Lady Duff-Gordon violated his exclusive rights by endorsing articles outside his agency and withholding the resulting profits. The complaint set out the signed written agreement in full. Lady
Duff-Gordon used the New York code-pleading procedure described in the reports as a demurrer and as a motion for judgment on the pleadings. The motion accepted the complaint's well-pleaded facts for purposes of testing whether they stated a legal cause of action.
Special Term denied Lady Duff-Gordon's motion, allowing Wood's claim to proceed. The Appellate Division, First
Department, unanimously reversed on April 20, 1917. Justice Davis read the writing as requiring Wood to collect and divide revenues only when he elected to arrange an endorsement, sale, or license. Because the text stated no express undertaking to generate such transactions, the intermediate court held that Wood's promises supplied neither mutuality nor consideration and granted judgment on the pleadings for Lady Duff-Gordon. 177 A.D. 624, 164 N.Y.S. 576.
Wood appealed to New York's highest court. The Court of Appeals reversed the Appellate Division and affirmed
Special Term, with costs in both appellate courts. Judge Cardozo wrote for a four-judge majority joined by Judges
Cuddeback, McLaughlin, and Andrews. Chief Judge Hiscock and Judges Chase and Crane dissented without separate opinions. The ruling resolved only the legal sufficiency of Wood's contract claim; the merits of performance, breach, and damages remained for later adjudication.

== Material Facts ==
Lady Duff-Gordon's approval and name carried commercial value in fashion markets. Manufacturers paid for her endorsement, and her own fabrics, garments, parasols, and related designs gained value when marketed under her name. She retained Wood to convert that commercial influence into licensing and sales revenue.
The signed agreement gave Wood, subject to Lady Duff-Gordon's approval, the exclusive right to place her endorsement on other creators' designs. It also gave him the exclusive right to sell her own designs or license others to manufacture and market them. The arrangement began April 1, 1915, ran for at least one year, and continued from year to year unless terminated by ninety days' notice.
Lady Duff-Gordon's compensation consisted of one-half of all profits and revenues generated by contracts Wood made under the exclusive agency. Wood promised to collect the revenues, remit her share, account monthly for all money received, and obtain patents, copyrights, and trademarks when his judgment found them useful to protect affected names, ideas, and articles. The writing recited that Wood possessed a business organization adapted to placing the endorsements she approved.
Wood alleged full performance on his side. He further alleged that Lady Duff-Gordon independently endorsed fabrics, dresses, and millinery for valuable consideration, excluded him from the transactions, and retained the revenues. Those acts directly implicated the exclusivity grant and the agreed revenue division.

== Issue ==
Does a signed exclusive marketing agreement supported by a revenue-sharing structure, monthly accounting duties, intellectual-property duties, and recitals identifying the marketer's specialized organization imply a promise that the marketer will use reasonable efforts, thereby supplying consideration for the fashion creator's exclusive grant?

== Holding ==
Yes. Reading the complete writing in its commercial setting, Wood assumed a duty to use reasonable efforts to place
Lady Duff-Gordon's endorsements and market her designs. That implied promise supplied consideration and made her exclusivity promise enforceable. The complaint therefore stated a contract claim. The Court of Appeals reversed the
Appellate Division and affirmed Special Term's denial of judgment on the pleadings.

== Reasoning ==
Whole-agreement interpretation: Cardozo drew the obligation from the agreement's recitals, grants, compensation formula, duration, and express administrative duties. Contractual meaning arose from how those provisions operated together in the parties' planned business. The court used implication to articulate the performance commitment carried by the transaction's structure.
Exclusivity: For at least one year, Lady Duff-Gordon placed her endorsement and designs within Wood's sole commercial channel. Wood's acceptance of that exclusive agency carried the duty to operate the channel. Exclusivity concentrated marketing authority in him and made his performance essential to the exchange.
Recital and institutional capacity: The agreement identified Wood's business organization as adapted to placing approved endorsements. That recital supplied a concrete intended use for his organization. It linked the grant to Wood's existing capacity and supported an undertaking to deploy that capacity for the agreed purpose.
Compensation design: Lady Duff-Gordon received half of the revenue produced through Wood's contracts. Her contractual return therefore depended directly on his efforts. A reasonable-efforts duty gave the revenue-sharing formula the business efficacy the parties assigned to it and protected the value she transferred through exclusivity.
Express downstream promises: Wood had to collect and divide money, provide monthly accounts, and secure or protect intellectual-property rights when his judgment called for it. Those duties presupposed commercial activity producing contracts, revenue, and protectable interests. They reinforced the implication that he would make reasonable efforts to bring those predicates into existence.
Content of the implied term: 'Reasonable efforts' creates an objective performance obligation informed by the market, the enterprise, the parties' purpose, available opportunities, and Wood's represented business capacity. It requires diligent pursuit of the exclusive undertaking while preserving business judgment about particular proposed transactions, each of which remained subject to Lady Duff-Gordon's approval.
Consideration: Lady Duff-Gordon exchanged exclusive commercial rights for Wood's promised efforts, revenue administration, profit sharing, accounting, and protective work. The implied efforts term exposed Wood to breach liability for an unreasonable failure to develop the business. That legal commitment supplied the bargained-for obligation supporting her promise.
Historical setting: The Appellate Division used a formal mutuality inquiry focused on an express sentence compelling
Wood to procure deals. Cardozo's approach reflected a broader early-twentieth-century movement toward reading agreements in their commercial context. He relied on The Moorcock's business-efficacy reasoning and New York exclusive-agency authorities. The result treats implication as interpretation of the parties' manifested transaction.
Connection to UCC Section 2-306(2): Article 2 later stated a parallel rule for goods: a lawful exclusive-dealing agreement ordinarily imposes best efforts on the seller to supply and on the buyer to promote. Wood supplies the common-law model for deriving an efforts obligation from exclusivity and commercial purpose.
Pleading significance: The Court of Appeals accepted Wood's allegations at this stage and determined that the writing could support enforcement. Wood would still bear the burden of proving his own performance, Lady Duff-Gordon's breach, causation, and damages. Lady Duff-Gordon could litigate whether her challenged transactions fell within the grant and whether Wood satisfied his implied duty.
Rule and application sequence: Read every express term and recital; identify the transaction's evident purpose;
determine whether exclusivity or compensation makes performance by one side essential; formulate the minimum term that gives the exchange its intended operation; then test whether that implied term supplies a legal obligation. Here, reasonable efforts fit the writing and completed the consideration analysis.
